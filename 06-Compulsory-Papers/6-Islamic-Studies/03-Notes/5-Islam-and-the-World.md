## 1. Impact of Islamic civilization on the West

### 1.1 Channels of contact

| Channel | Detail |
|---|---|
| Muslim Spain (al-Andalus), 711–1492 | Cordoba, Toledo, Seville and Granada were centres where European students studied |
| Sicily | Muslim rule 9th–11th centuries; Norman kings such as Roger II and later Frederick II kept Arab scholars at court |
| The Crusades, 1095–1291 | Two centuries of contact brought goods, techniques and ideas to Europe |
| Trade | Venice, Genoa and others traded with Egypt and Syria |
| Translation | The Toledo school (12th century). Gerard of Cremona translated dozens of Arabic works into Latin, including Ibn Sina's *Canon*. Robert of Chester translated al-Khwarizmi's algebra (1145). |

### 1.2 Contributions by field

| Field | Contribution and names |
|---|---|
| **Scientific method** | Observation and experiment in place of pure speculation. Ibn al-Haytham (Alhazen, d. c. 1040) tested theories of light by experiment in *Kitab al-Manazir*; his work reached Roger Bacon and Kepler. Robert Briffault (*The Making of Humanity*, 1919) argued that experimental science was the most important gift of Arab civilization to the modern world. Iqbal makes the same point in *Reconstruction* (Lecture 5). |
| **Mathematics** | Al-Khwarizmi (9th century): algebra (from his book *al-Jabr*) and the word "algorithm" (from his name). Hindu-Arabic numerals and zero reached Europe through Arabic works; Fibonacci popularised them in 1202. Trigonometry was developed by al-Battani and others. |
| **Medicine** | Al-Razi (Rhazes): first clear account separating smallpox from measles. Ibn Sina (Avicenna, d. 1037): *al-Qanun fi'l-Tibb*, a standard textbook in European universities into the 17th century. Al-Zahrawi (Abulcasis): surgery and surgical instruments. Ibn al-Nafis (13th century): described the circulation of blood through the lungs. Hospitals (*bimaristans*) with wards, pharmacies and teaching. |
| **Chemistry** | Jabir ibn Hayyan (Geber): laboratory methods such as distillation and crystallisation. Words: alchemy, alcohol, alkali. |
| **Astronomy** | Observatories at Baghdad, Maragha and Samarkand. Star names (Aldebaran, Altair) and terms (zenith, nadir, azimuth). The mathematical models of Nasir al-Din al-Tusi and Ibn al-Shatir resemble those later used by Copernicus. |
| **Geography and navigation** | Al-Idrisi made a world map for Roger II of Sicily (1154). The astrolabe and improved maps helped European voyages. |
| **Philosophy** | Al-Kindi, al-Farabi, Ibn Sina and Ibn Rushd preserved and developed Greek philosophy. Ibn Rushd's (Averroes, d. 1198) commentaries on Aristotle shaped scholastic thought; Thomas Aquinas answered and used them. |
| **Social science** | Ibn Khaldun (d. 1406), *Muqaddimah*: a science of society and history centuries before modern sociology. |
| **Education** | Mosque-colleges and endowed universities: al-Qarawiyyin in Fez (founded 859) and al-Azhar in Cairo (c. 970) are older than Bologna, Paris and Oxford. Features such as endowed colleges and the licence to teach have been compared by historians with European practice. |
| **Paper and books** | Papermaking learned from the Chinese (8th century), paper mills in Baghdad, then Spain and Europe. Large public and private libraries. |
| **Agriculture and industry** | Crops brought west: sugar, cotton, rice, citrus fruits. Irrigation techniques. Textiles, glass, ceramics, leather. |
| **Trade and finance** | The cheque (from *sakk*), bills of exchange, partnership forms similar to *mudarabah*. Words: tariff, magazine, arsenal, admiral. |
| **Architecture and arts** | The pointed arch and decorative geometry influenced European building; the Alhambra and the Mosque of Cordoba remain landmarks. |
| **Literature** | Tales, poetic forms and music travelled through Spain and Sicily. Ibn Tufayl's *Hayy ibn Yaqzan* was translated into Latin (1671) and English. |
| **Ideas and values** | Human equality, religious coexistence in Spain, rights of women to property, rules of war. |

**Result.** These transfers fed the European Renaissance. Writers who acknowledge it: W. Montgomery Watt (*The Influence of Islam on Medieval Europe*), George Sarton (*Introduction to the History of Science*), Philip Hitti (*History of the Arabs*). M. Abdur Rahman's *A Brief Survey of Muslim Science and Culture* is on the FPSC list.

**Why the Muslim lead was lost (one paragraph):** the Mongol destruction of Baghdad (1258) and the loss of Spain (1492); political fragmentation; the narrowing of education to religious texts alone; the decline of ijtihad; neglect of printing and new sciences; then colonial rule.

## 2. Impact of Western civilization on the Muslim world

### 2.1 How it came

Colonial rule from the 18th to the 20th century: Britain in India, Egypt and Malaya; France in North and West Africa and Syria; the Netherlands in Indonesia; Russia in Central Asia; Italy in Libya. After the First World War the Ottoman lands were divided (Sykes–Picot Agreement, 1916; Balfour Declaration, 1917) and the caliphate was abolished in 1924. After independence the influence continued through education, media, the economy and international institutions.

### 2.2 Areas of influence (asked 2026)

| Area | Influence |
|---|---|
| **Political** | The nation-state replaced the idea of one Ummah; borders drawn by colonial powers; secular constitutions; Western-style parliaments, parties and bureaucracies; in some countries, authoritarian regimes backed from outside |
| **Legal** | European codes replaced most of the Shariah, leaving mainly family law |
| **Educational** | A dual system: secular schools for jobs and state, madrasahs for religion (Macaulay's Minute on Education, 1835, in India). English and French became languages of power. |
| **Economic** | Interest-based banking; capitalism and consumerism; debt and dependence on the IMF and World Bank; raw-material economies |
| **Intellectual** | Secularism, nationalism, liberalism, Marxism; Orientalist readings of Islam (criticised by Edward Said in *Orientalism*, 1978); doubt about religion among the educated |
| **Social and cultural** | Dress, food, entertainment, language; individualism; weaker joint family; changing gender roles; later marriage; rising divorce; materialism |
| **Media and technology** | Film, television, the internet and social media carry Western lifestyles and values into every home |

**Positive influences to acknowledge**

1. Modern science, medicine and technology.
2. Constitutional government, elections, accountability and rule of law as working institutions.
3. Research methods, universities and printing.
4. Human-rights language and anti-slavery movements.
5. Organisation, punctuality and work ethic.

These renewed debate among Muslims and stirred reform and revival.

**Negative influences**

1. Loss of political unity and sovereignty.
2. Secularisation: religion pushed to private life.
3. Moral permissiveness; pressure on the family.
4. Consumerism and interest-based debt.
5. Inferiority complex and blind imitation. The Prophet (PBUH) foretold that Muslims would follow the ways of earlier peoples span by span (Sahih al-Bukhari 3456; Sahih Muslim 2669).
6. A split between a Westernised elite and the religious public.
7. Cultural identity crisis among the young.

### 2.3 Views in Muslim circles about Western civilization (asked 2025)

| View | Position | Names | Assessment |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Total rejection** | The West is wholly corrupt; avoid its education, language and tools | Some traditional circles in the colonial period | Protected identity but cut Muslims off from science and power |
| **2. Total adoption (Westernisers, secularists)** | Progress requires adopting the West fully, including secularism | Mustafa Kemal Ataturk in Turkey; Taha Husayn in Egypt (*The Future of Culture in Egypt*) | Brought institutions, but at the cost of identity, and without closing the gap |
| **3. Modernist reform** | Islam and modern reason agree; reinterpret Islam and take Western science and institutions | Sir Syed Ahmad Khan (Aligarh), Muhammad Abduh, Jamaluddin Afghani (who also called for Muslim unity against colonialism) | Opened modern education; critics say some went too far in apology |
| **4. Critical synthesis** | Take knowledge and method, which are partly Islam's own legacy; reject the materialist worldview | Allama Iqbal: Europe's intellectual side develops phases of Islamic culture, but its "dazzling exterior" may blind Muslims to its inner emptiness (*Reconstruction*, Lecture 1). Muhammad Asad (*Islam at the Crossroads*, 1934): learn Western science, do not imitate Western life. | The most widely accepted position among Muslim thinkers |
| **5. Revivalist** | Islam is a complete alternative system; the West is a modern *jahiliyyah* in values, though its science is useful | Maulana Maududi, Hasan al-Banna, Sayyid Qutb; Dr Muhammad Amin's *Islam aur Tahzib-e-Maghrib ki Kashmakash* (on the FPSC list) | Restored confidence and a full vision; critics note the difficulty of putting it into practice |
| **6. Violent confrontation** | The West is an enemy to be fought everywhere | Militant extremist groups | Rejected by mainstream scholars as against the Shariah; it harmed Muslims most |
| **7. Dialogue and coexistence** | Civilizations should talk and cooperate on shared values | The call for a "Dialogue among Civilizations" (UN year, 2001); *A Common Word* (2007) | Answers Huntington's "clash of civilizations" thesis (1993; book 1996) |

**Own analysis for the answer:** the Quranic guide is to "listen to what is said and follow the best of it" (Az-Zumar 39:18) and to verify (Al-Hujurat 49:6). Wisdom may be taken from any source; values come from revelation. View 4 fits this best.

### 2.4 Protecting Muslim societies from the negative effects (asked 2026)

1. **Strengthen faith and identity** through Quran-centred education and the Seerah.
2. **Reform education:** one system joining religious and modern knowledge; teach critical thinking; produce original research.
3. **Strengthen the family:** parental time and example; marriage made easy; rights and duties taught.
4. **Media:** create high-quality content of our own; regulate obscenity and hate; teach digital literacy.
5. **Economic independence:** reduce debt; develop interest-free finance; trade within the Muslim world.
6. **Revive ijtihad** so that Islam speaks to new questions and the young are not left with only Western answers.
7. **Selective borrowing:** adopt science, technology, institutional discipline; filter values.
8. **Language and culture:** value national languages and arts.
9. **Role of scholars and mosques:** relevant sermons; engagement with youth.
10. **State policy:** Article 31 of Pakistan's Constitution (Islamic way of life); curriculum; cultural policy.
11. **Confidence, not fear:** Islam spread in open societies before; "You will be uppermost if you are believers." (Al Imran 3:139)
12. **Dialogue from strength:** present Islam's alternative to the West's own crises: loneliness, family breakdown, inequality.

## 3. The role of Islam in the modern world

A syllabus item not yet asked directly. Likely wordings: "What can Islam offer the modern world?" or "Is Islam relevant today?"

**The modern world's problems and Islam's answer**

| Problem | What Islam offers | Reference |
|---|---|---|
| Spiritual emptiness, anxiety, addiction, suicide | Purpose, remembrance of Allah, contentment | Ar-Ra'd 13:28; Adh-Dhariyat 51:56 |
| Moral relativism | A stable moral code | Al-Isra 17:9 |
| Family breakdown | Marriage, mutual rights, care of parents | Ar-Rum 30:21; Al-Isra 17:23 |
| Racism and nationalism | Equality of mankind | Al-Hujurat 49:13 |
| Extreme inequality; debt crises | Zakat, ban on riba, circulation of wealth, risk-sharing finance | Al-Hashr 59:7; Al-Baqarah 2:275 |
| War and weapons of mass destruction | Strict limits on war; sanctity of life; preference for peace | Al-Baqarah 2:190; Al-Ma'idah 5:32; Al-Anfal 8:61 |
| Environmental crisis | Man as trustee; balance in creation; ban on waste and corruption on earth | Al-Baqarah 2:30; Ar-Rahman 55:7–9; Al-A'raf 7:31, 7:56; Ar-Rum 30:41; planting as charity (Sahih al-Bukhari 2320) |
| Abuse of power; corruption | Authority as trust; accountability before people and Allah | An-Nisa 4:58; Sahih al-Bukhari 7138 |
| Religious conflict | No compulsion; justice to all; dialogue | Al-Baqarah 2:256; Al Imran 3:64 |
| Ethics of new technology (AI, genetics, surveillance) | Maqasid al-Shariah as a test: does it protect life, mind, family, property and faith? Verification of information; privacy | Al-Hujurat 49:6, 49:12; Al-Isra 17:36 |
| Refugees and migration | Asylum and brotherhood (the Hijrah model) | At-Tawbah 9:6; Al-Hashr 59:9 |

**Facts to use:** Muslims are about a quarter of the world's population, roughly two billion people, in 57 OIC member states and as minorities elsewhere. Islam is the world's fastest-growing major religion by current estimates (Pew Research Center). Islamic finance has grown into an industry of several trillion US dollars in assets.

**Conditions for Islam to play this role:** Muslims must first practise it (As-Saff 61:2–3); revive knowledge and ijtihad; reform governance; unite; present Islam through character. The Quran calls the Ummah "the best community raised for mankind" because it enjoins good and forbids evil (Al Imran 3:110), and a "middle community" to be witnesses over mankind (Al-Baqarah 2:143).

**Scholars:** Iqbal: humanity needs a spiritual interpretation of the universe, spiritual emancipation of the individual, and basic principles of universal import directing society on a spiritual basis (*Reconstruction*, Lecture 6). Seyyed Hossein Nasr (*Ideals and Realities of Islam*): Islam answers modern man's loss of the sacred. Khurshid Ahmad: Islam as a middle way between capitalism and socialism.

## 4. Muslim world and contemporary challenges

### 4.1 Overview

The Muslim world has people, land, energy and location. It holds a large share of the world's oil and gas and sits on key sea routes. Yet it is politically weak and divided. The Prophet (PBUH) foretold a time when nations would call one another against the Muslims as diners gather round a dish, not because Muslims were few, but because they had become like the foam on a flood, through love of the world and dislike of death (Sunan Abi Dawud 4297).

### 4.2 The challenges (asked 2016; use as a menu)

**A. Political**

1. **Disunity.** Nation-state rivalries; wars between and within Muslim states (Yemen, Syria, Libya, Sudan). The OIC (founded 25 September 1969 in Rabat after the arson attack on al-Aqsa Mosque; 57 members; headquarters in Jeddah) has little enforcing power.
2. **Occupation and persecution of Muslims.** Palestine, with the devastating war on Gaza since October 2023; Indian-occupied Kashmir, especially after the revocation of its special status on 5 August 2019; the Rohingya in Myanmar; Uyghurs; Muslims in India.
3. **Authoritarian rule and weak institutions.** Little consultation or accountability in many states.
4. **Foreign interference and dependence.**

**B. Economic**

5. Poverty in many states beside great wealth in a few; unemployment among the young.
6. Debt and dependence on interest-based lending.
7. Low trade among Muslim states; raw-material economies; little industry and technology.
8. Corruption and capital flight.

**C. Intellectual and educational**

9. Low literacy and low spending on research; few world-class universities.
10. Stagnation in religious thought; neglect of ijtihad; a split between religious and modern education.
11. Brain drain.

**D. Religious and social**

12. Sectarianism (section 4.3).
13. Extremism and terrorism (section 5).
14. A gap between worship and conduct; moral decline.
15. Pressure on the family (see Notes 7).

**E. External**

16. Islamophobia (section 4.4).
17. Cultural and media dominance of the West.
18. Wars and sanctions.

**F. New challenges**

19. Climate change: floods and drought (Pakistan's 2022 floods).
20. Technology gap, including artificial intelligence.
21. Refugees and displacement: a large share of the world's refugees come from or live in Muslim countries.

### 4.3 Sectarianism and polarization (asked 2026)

**Meaning.** Sectarianism is hostility and discrimination between groups within one religion on the basis of doctrinal or legal differences. Difference of opinion (*ikhtilaf*) is natural and accepted in Islam; division and enmity (*tafarruq*) are forbidden.

**The teaching**

- "Hold firmly to the rope of Allah all together and do not be divided." (Al Imran 3:103)
- "Do not be like those who divided and differed after clear proofs had come to them." (Al Imran 3:105)
- "Those who have divided their religion and become sects, you have nothing to do with them." (Al-An'am 6:159; Ar-Rum 30:31–32)
- "Do not dispute, or you will lose heart and your strength will depart." (Al-Anfal 8:46)
- "The believers are but brothers, so make peace between your brothers." (Al-Hujurat 49:10)
- "Insulting a Muslim is wickedness and fighting him is disbelief." (Sahih al-Bukhari 48; Sahih Muslim 64)
- "If a man calls his brother an unbeliever, it returns upon one of them." (Sahih al-Bukhari 6104; Sahih Muslim 60)
- "Do not turn back after me as unbelievers, striking one another's necks." (Sahih al-Bukhari 121)

**Causes**

| Type | Causes |
|---|---|
| Historical | Early political disputes over leadership hardened into separate identities |
| Religious | Giving secondary matters the weight of fundamentals; *takfir* (declaring other Muslims unbelievers); ignorance of the ethics of disagreement; narrow, sect-based religious schooling; inflammatory preachers and literature |
| Political | Rulers and parties using sect for power; proxy rivalries between regional states, sharper after 1979 (the Iranian Revolution and the Afghan war); wars in Iraq, Syria and Yemen |
| External | Colonial "divide and rule"; outside powers exploiting divisions |
| Social and economic | Poverty and unemployment that make recruitment easy; weak rule of law; unregulated loudspeakers and social media |
| Wider polarization | Religious versus secular, ethnic and party hatreds, echo chambers online |

**Solutions in the light of Islamic teachings**

1. **Return to the agreed core:** one God, one Prophet (PBUH), one Book, one qiblah. Refer disputes to Allah and the Messenger (An-Nisa 4:59).
2. **Ethics of disagreement:** the Companions differed on legal points and stayed brothers (the Asr prayer at Banu Qurayzah, Sahih al-Bukhari 946).
3. **End takfir.** The *Amman Message* (2004–2005), endorsed by scholars of all major schools, recognised the Sunni, Shia Ja'fari and Zaydi, Ibadi and Zahiri schools as Muslim and forbade declaring their followers unbelievers.
4. **Reconciliation as a duty:** Al-Hujurat 49:9–10; reconciling people ranks above optional worship (Sunan Abi Dawud 4919). Hasan ibn Ali (RA) gave up his claim to end civil war, as the Prophet (PBUH) had foretold in praise (Sahih al-Bukhari 2704).
5. **Reform religious education:** teach comparative fiqh and respect for all schools; common curriculum elements; exchange between seminaries.
6. **Responsible pulpit and media:** ban hate speech; enforce the law equally.
7. **Justice and good governance:** remove the grievances that feed sectarian groups.
8. **Diplomacy among Muslim states:** end proxy wars. The restoration of Saudi–Iranian relations in March 2023 is a hopeful step.
9. **Joint platforms.** In Pakistan: the 22 Points agreed by scholars of all schools in 1951; the Milli Yakjehti Council; *Paigham-e-Pakistan* (2018); the National Action Plan (2014).
10. **Wise preaching:** An-Nahl 16:125.

### 4.4 Islamophobia (asked 2021)

**Meaning.** Fear of, prejudice against and hostility toward Islam and Muslims, shown in hate speech, discrimination and violence. The term became common after the Runnymede Trust report of 1997.

**How it shows:** attacks on mosques and individuals (the Christchurch mosque attacks of 15 March 2019 killed 51 worshippers); bans on hijab and religious dress in some European states; insulting caricatures and public burnings of the Quran; profiling at borders; discriminatory citizenship and demolition policies against Muslims in India; hostile media and political rhetoric.

**"A sign of extremism" (the 2021 wording):** it is the extremism of the far right. It judges 2 billion people by the acts of a few, rejects pluralism and leads to violence. It and Muslim extremism feed each other.

**Causes:** ignorance of Islam; historical memory (Crusades, colonial Orientalism); media stereotyping; far-right politics using migrants as scapegoats; the "clash of civilizations" thesis; terrorist acts by groups claiming Islam; the weakness and poor image of Muslim states.

**Remedies**

1. **Character first:** "Repel evil with what is better; then the one between whom and you was enmity will become like a close friend." (Fussilat 41:34)
2. **Present the real Islam:** scholarship, media, art and the internet in the languages of the West.
3. **Dialogue:** Al Imran 3:64; An-Nahl 16:125.
4. **Do not answer insult with violence or insult:** Al-An'am 6:108; Al-Furqan 25:63.
5. **Diplomacy and law.** The UN General Assembly in 2022 made 15 March the International Day to Combat Islamophobia, on a resolution moved by Pakistan for the OIC; a further resolution in 2024 called for a UN special envoy. Press for laws against incitement to religious hatred.
6. **Muslim minorities** should be law-abiding, active citizens who serve their societies.
7. **End extremism at home**, which gives Islamophobes their material.
8. **Unity and strength of Muslim states**, so their voice counts.
9. **Protect minorities in Muslim countries**, to practise what we demand.

### 4.5 The Two-Nation Theory and Indian and Kashmiri Muslims (asked 2026)

This question overlaps Pakistan Affairs. Outline:

**The theory.** Muslims and Hindus of the subcontinent are two nations by religion, law, culture, history and social life, so Muslims are entitled to a homeland where they can live by Islam. Roots: the Islamic idea that community rests on faith (Al-Hujurat 49:10; Al-Kafirun 109:6). Voices: Sir Syed Ahmad Khan after the Urdu–Hindi controversy (1867); Allama Iqbal's Allahabad Address (1930); the Quaid-e-Azam's presidential address at Lahore (March 1940) and the Lahore Resolution (23 March 1940).

**Justification from the present condition of Indian Muslims:** the demolition of the Babri Mosque (6 December 1992) and the temple built on its site; the Gujarat killings of 2002; mob lynchings over cow protection; the Citizenship Amendment Act (2019) and the proposed citizens' register; demolition of Muslim homes; restrictions on hijab in some colleges; under-representation in services and legislatures (documented by the Sachar Committee, 2006); the rise of Hindutva politics.

**Kashmir:** the revocation of Articles 370 and 35-A on 5 August 2019; demographic change through new domicile rules; long detentions and communication shutdowns; UN Security Council resolutions on a plebiscite unimplemented.

**Demands (requirements) of the theory today**

1. Pakistan must become what it was meant to be: a just, Islamic welfare state (Objectives Resolution; Articles 2-A, 31).
2. National unity above ethnic, sectarian and party divisions.
3. Protect Pakistan's own minorities, as the Quaid promised on 11 August 1947 and as Islam requires. This is the moral proof of the theory.
4. Economic and defence strength.
5. Education that carries the ideology forward.
6. Active diplomacy for Kashmir and for the rights of Indian Muslims.
7. Muslim-world solidarity.

## 5. Rise of extremism

### 5.1 Terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Extremism** | Going beyond the balanced limit in belief or action, and rejecting others' right to differ. Quranic word: *ghuluw* (excess). Also *tatarruf* (going to the edge) and *tashaddud* (harshness). |
| **Radicalisation** | The process of adopting extreme views |
| **Terrorism** | Unlawful violence against civilians to spread fear for political or ideological aims. In fiqh the closest crimes are *hirabah* (armed attack on public safety, Al-Ma'idah 5:33) and *fasad fi'l-ard* (corruption on earth). |
| **Takfir** | Declaring a Muslim an unbeliever |
| **Khawarij** | The first extremist group in Muslim history. They rebelled against Ali (RA), declared Muslims who disagreed with them unbelievers, and killed them. |

### 5.2 Islam's position: moderation

- "Thus We have made you a middle community." (Al-Baqarah 2:143)
- "Do not commit excess in your religion." (An-Nisa 4:171; Al-Ma'idah 5:77)
- "Allah intends ease for you, not hardship." (Al-Baqarah 2:185)
- "Beware of going to extremes in religion; those before you were destroyed by going to extremes in religion." (Sunan an-Nasa'i 3057; Sunan Ibn Majah 3029)
- "Ruined are those who go to extremes," said three times (Sahih Muslim 2670).
- "The religion is easy; no one makes it hard on himself but it overcomes him." (Sahih al-Bukhari 39)
- Three men resolved to pray all night, fast every day and never marry. The Prophet (PBUH) said he fasts and breaks fast, prays and sleeps, and marries; "whoever turns away from my Sunnah is not of me." (Sahih al-Bukhari 5063)
- About the Khawarij type: they recite the Quran but it does not pass their throats; they pass through the religion as an arrow passes through its target (Sahih al-Bukhari 6930; Sahih Muslim 1066).
- Sanctity of life: Al-Ma'idah 5:32; An-Nisa 4:93; "The passing away of the world is lighter before Allah than the killing of a Muslim." (Sunan an-Nasa'i 3987)
- Suicide is forbidden: An-Nisa 4:29; Sahih al-Bukhari 5778.

**Is extremism a challenge to Islam? (2019).** Yes, in three ways. It distorts the message from within. It kills Muslims and destroys Muslim societies. It gives a pretext for Islamophobia and foreign intervention. Yet it is a challenge *to* Islam, not a product *of* Islam: the texts above condemn it, and the great majority of its victims are Muslims.

### 5.3 Causes of extremism and terrorism in Pakistan (asked 2017, 2018, 2023, 2024)

| Category | Causes |
|---|---|
| **Religious** | Misreading of jihad and of verses out of context; takfir; sectarian hate literature; untrained preachers; some seminaries and groups teaching intolerance; ignorance of the Seerah |
| **Historical and geopolitical** | The Afghan war of 1979–89 and the arming of militant groups; the post-2001 "war on terror", drone strikes and their spillover; proxy rivalries; hostile agencies sponsoring violence; the situation in Afghanistan since 2021 |
| **Political** | Use of religious groups for political ends; weak and unstable governments; political polarisation and the language of hatred; denial of rights in some regions, feeding ethnic militancy |
| **Economic** | Poverty, unemployment, inequality; a large youth population with few opportunities |
| **Social and educational** | Low literacy; three parallel school systems; no training in critical thinking or tolerance; intolerance in daily life; mob violence |
| **Governance and justice** | Slow courts and low conviction rates; corruption; poor policing; ungoverned spaces |
| **Media and technology** | Online radicalisation; sensational coverage; hate speech on social media |
| **Psychological** | Sense of humiliation over Palestine and Kashmir; identity crisis; desire for revenge and significance |

Cost to Pakistan: tens of thousands of lives since 2001, including the Army Public School attack in Peshawar (16 December 2014), and very large economic losses.

### 5.4 Remedies in the light of Islamic teachings

**Ideological**

1. Teach the true meaning of jihad and the sanctity of life (5:32).
2. A unified scholarly stand: *Paigham-e-Pakistan* (2018), signed by over 1,800 scholars of all schools, declares suicide attacks and armed rebellion against the state forbidden and reserves the declaration of jihad to the state.
3. Reform Friday sermons; train imams; regulate hate speech.
4. Promote the ethics of disagreement; end takfir.
5. Revive tazkiyah and the Prophet's (PBUH) gentleness (Al Imran 3:159).

**Educational**

6. Curriculum of tolerance, Seerah-based ethics and critical thinking; bring seminaries into the mainstream with modern subjects and registration.

**Socio-economic**

7. Justice in distribution: zakat, jobs, skills for youth. The Quran links security with freedom from hunger (Quraysh 106:3–4).
8. Development of neglected regions.

**Political and legal**

9. Rule of law and speedy justice (An-Nisa 4:58).
10. Consultation and inclusion; settle political disputes by dialogue (Ash-Shura 42:38).
11. The state's monopoly of force; action against all armed groups without exception. Punishment of *hirabah* (Al-Ma'idah 5:33).
12. Full implementation of the National Action Plan (2014) and the work of NACTA.

**The Prophet's (PBUH) method (the 2024 wording)**

13. Patience and non-retaliation in Makkah; forgiveness at Ta'if and Makkah.
14. A pluralist charter in Madinah.
15. Treaties over war (Hudaybiyyah).
16. Gentle correction of the ignorant (the Bedouin in the mosque).
17. Firmness with those who took up arms and spread disorder.
18. Brotherhood to end tribal hatred; ban on tribal cries (Sahih al-Bukhari 4905).

**For political extremism (2024):** consultation, tolerance of dissent, truthful speech (Al-Ahzab 33:70), verification of news (Al-Hujurat 49:6), no mockery or abuse (Al-Hujurat 49:11), reconciliation (Al-Hujurat 49:9–10), and justice even toward opponents (Al-Ma'idah 5:8).

**International**

19. Just settlement of Palestine and Kashmir, which extremists exploit.
20. Cooperation among Muslim states; counter Islamophobia.

Full answer: MA-12.

### 5.5 Jihad: meaning, kinds, conditions (asked 2020)

**Meaning.** From *jahada*: to strive, to exert effort. Technically: to strive with one's self, tongue, wealth and, when required, arms, for the cause of Allah. *Qital* (fighting) is one form of it. "Holy war" is not a translation of jihad.

**Kinds**

| Kind | Explanation | Reference |
|---|---|---|
| Against the self (*jihad al-nafs*) | Self-discipline and obedience | Al-Ankabut 29:69; Jami at-Tirmidhi 1621 |
| By the Quran, knowledge and argument | Teaching and conveying the message | Al-Furqan 25:52 (a Makkan verse: "strive against them with it a great striving") |
| By the tongue and pen | Speaking truth | "The best jihad is a word of truth before a tyrant ruler." (Sunan Abi Dawud 4344) |
| By wealth | Spending for the cause | As-Saff 61:10–11; At-Tawbah 9:41 |
| By serving parents and family | A man was told: "Strive in their service." | Sahih al-Bukhari 3004 |
| Armed (*qital*) | Defence and removal of oppression | Al-Hajj 22:39; Al-Baqarah 2:190 |

The saying about returning "from the lesser jihad to the greater jihad" is weak in its chain according to hadith scholars. Do not quote it as a hadith; use Tirmidhi 1621 for the same idea.

**Purposes of armed jihad**

1. Self-defence (Al-Hajj 22:39; Al-Baqarah 2:190).
2. Rescue of the oppressed (An-Nisa 4:75).
3. Ending religious persecution so that faith is free (Al-Baqarah 2:193; Al-Anfal 8:39).
4. Protection of all places of worship (Al-Hajj 22:40).
5. Answering treaty-breaking and aggression (At-Tawbah 9:12–13).

It is not for forced conversion (Al-Baqarah 2:256), land, wealth or revenge. A man asked what fighting is in Allah's cause; the Prophet (PBUH) said: whoever fights so that the word of Allah is uppermost (Sahih al-Bukhari 123, 2810).

**Conditions and rules**

| Condition | Basis |
|---|---|
| Just cause | Al-Hajj 22:39 |
| Declared by legitimate authority (the state), not by individuals or groups | "The leader is a shield; fighting is done behind him." (Sahih al-Bukhari 2957; Sahih Muslim 1841). Agreed by classical jurists. |
| Right intention | Sahih al-Bukhari 2810 |
| Last resort; peace accepted when offered | Al-Anfal 8:61; An-Nisa 4:90 |
| Open declaration; no treachery; treaties kept | Al-Anfal 8:58; At-Tawbah 9:4 |
| No killing of non-combatants: women, children, the old, monks, farmers | Sahih al-Bukhari 3014–3015; Sahih Muslim 1731; Muwatta Malik, Jihad, no. 10 |
| No mutilation, no burning | Sahih Muslim 1731; Sahih al-Bukhari 3016 |
| No destruction of crops, trees, animals, buildings without necessity | Abu Bakr's (RA) instructions; Al-Baqarah 2:205 |
| Proportion | Al-Baqarah 2:194; An-Nahl 16:126 |
| Humane treatment of prisoners | Al-Insan 76:8; Muhammad 47:4 |
| Protection for one who seeks it; envoys are safe | At-Tawbah 9:6 |
| Preparation and capability | Al-Anfal 8:60 |

**Verses often quoted out of context.** At-Tawbah 9:5 concerns Arab polytheists who had broken their treaties, after four months' notice; the verses before and after (9:4, 9:6, 9:7) exempt those who kept their treaties and order protection for those who seek it. Al-Baqarah 2:191 follows 2:190, which limits fighting to those who fight you.

**Jihad and terrorism compared**

| | Jihad (qital) | Terrorism |
|---|---|---|
| Authority | Lawful state | Individuals or groups |
| Aim | Defence, justice, ending persecution | Fear, power, revenge |
| Targets | Combatants only | Civilians, worshippers, markets, schools |
| Method | Open, rule-bound | Secret, indiscriminate, suicide attacks |
| Ethics | Strict limits | None |
| Legal status | A regulated duty | A crime: *hirabah*, *fasad* (Al-Ma'idah 5:33) |
| Result | Peace and security | Disorder |

**Today:** defence of Pakistan's borders by its armed forces is lawful defence; attacks by militant groups on citizens and the state are *fasad*. The wider, non-military forms of jihad matter most for the Ummah now: against ignorance, poverty, corruption and the self.
