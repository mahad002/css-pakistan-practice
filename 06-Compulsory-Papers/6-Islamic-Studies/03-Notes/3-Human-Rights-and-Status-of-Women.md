## Part A — Human dignity and human rights

### 1. Concept

- **Human rights** are the basic entitlements every person holds simply by being human.
- **In Islam** the Arabic term is *huquq al-ibad* (rights of people), set beside *huquq Allah* (rights of Allah). Rights of people are so serious that Allah does not waive them unless the wronged person forgives. The Prophet (PBUH) described the truly bankrupt person as one who comes on the Day of Judgment with prayer, fasting and charity but has abused, slandered, robbed or struck others; his good deeds are given to them (Sahih Muslim 2581).

**Distinctive features of the Islamic concept**

| Feature | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Divine source | Rights are granted by Allah. No ruler or parliament can cancel them. |
| Linked to duties | Every right is someone's duty. Rights and responsibilities come together. |
| Universal | For all humans, Muslim or not, in peace and war. |
| Moral and legal | Enforced by courts, and by conscience and the Hereafter. |
| Balanced | Individual freedom within the limits that protect society. |
| Early | Declared in the 7th century and practised in Madinah. |

### 2. Human dignity (asked 2020, 2023 Special, 2024)

**Foundations of dignity in the Quran**

| Ground | Reference |
|---|---|
| Honour given to all humans | "We have certainly honoured the children of Adam." (Al-Isra 17:70) |
| Best of forms | "We created man in the best stature." (At-Tin 95:4) |
| Vicegerent on earth | "I am placing a successor (khalifah) on the earth." (Al-Baqarah 2:30) |
| Angels told to bow before Adam | Al-Baqarah 2:34 |
| Taught knowledge | "He taught Adam the names, all of them." (Al-Baqarah 2:31) |
| Bearer of the trust | Al-Ahzab 33:72 |
| The universe placed at his service | Al-Jathiyah 45:13; Luqman 31:20 |
| One origin | "Created you from a single soul." (An-Nisa 4:1) |
| Only piety ranks people | "The most honoured of you in the sight of Allah is the most righteous of you." (Al-Hujurat 49:13) |
| Life is sacred | "Whoever kills a soul ... it is as if he had killed all mankind; and whoever saves one, it is as if he had saved all mankind." (Al-Ma'idah 5:32) |

**Dignity in the Sunnah**

1. Farewell Sermon: "Your blood, your property and your honour are sacred to one another, like the sanctity of this day, in this month, in this city." (Sahih al-Bukhari 67, 1739; Sahih Muslim 1679)
2. "All of a Muslim is sacred to another Muslim: his blood, his wealth and his honour." (Sahih Muslim 2564)
3. Standing for a funeral of a non-Muslim: "Was it not a soul?" (Sahih al-Bukhari 1312)
4. Servants: "They are your brothers. Feed them from what you eat and clothe them from what you wear." (Sahih al-Bukhari 30, 2545)
5. No mockery, insulting nicknames, suspicion, spying or backbiting (Al-Hujurat 49:11–12).
6. No torture or mutilation, even of an enemy (Sahih Muslim 1731).
7. Dignity of labour: no one eats better food than what he earns with his own hand (Sahih al-Bukhari 2072).
8. Dignity of the poor: Abasa 80:1–10 corrected even the Prophet (PBUH) for turning from a blind poor man to speak to chiefs.

**Regardless of colour, race or creed (2020)**

- Diversity is a sign of Allah (Ar-Rum 30:22) and exists "so that you may know one another" (Al-Hujurat 49:13).
- Bilal the Abyssinian, Salman the Persian and Suhayb the Roman (may Allah be pleased with them) held high rank. Umar (RA) said of Abu Bakr and Bilal: "Abu Bakr is our master and he freed our master" (Sahih al-Bukhari 3754).
- The Prophet (PBUH) rebuked Abu Dharr (RA) for taunting a man about his mother: "You are a man in whom there is still some ignorance." (Sahih al-Bukhari 30)
- The statement in the Farewell Sermon that an Arab has no superiority over a non-Arab, nor white over black, except by piety, is reported in Musnad Ahmad [number not verified; quote it as "reported in Musnad Ahmad"].
- Justice is owed to all: "Let not hatred of a people lead you away from justice." (Al-Ma'idah 5:8)
- Kindness to peaceful non-Muslims: Al-Mumtahanah 60:8.

### 4. Maqasid al-Shariah as a human-rights framework

Al-Ghazali (*al-Mustasfa*) and later al-Shatibi (*al-Muwafaqat*) taught that the Shariah protects five essentials. Each is a cluster of rights.

| Essential | Rights protected |
|---|---|
| Religion (*din*) | Freedom of belief and worship |
| Life (*nafs*) | Life, security, health |
| Intellect (*aql*) | Education, freedom of thought, ban on intoxicants |
| Lineage / family (*nasl*) | Marriage, family, children's rights; some add honour (*ird*) |
| Property (*mal*) | Ownership, fair earning, protection from theft and fraud |

### 5. The Farewell Sermon as a charter of human rights (asked 2020, 2023)

**Occasion.** 9 Dhul Hijjah, 10 AH (632 CE), at Arafat, before more than a hundred thousand pilgrims by the usual estimates. Parts were also spoken at Mina. It is preserved across several narrations: Sahih al-Bukhari 67, 1739, 1741; Sahih Muslim 1218 (Jabir's long narration) and 1679; Jami at-Tirmidhi; Musnad Ahmad; and the Seerah of Ibn Hisham. No single narration contains every sentence.

**Main points and the rights they establish**

| Point | Right | Source |
|---|---|---|
| "Your blood, property and honour are sacred." | Life, property, dignity | Bukhari 1739; Muslim 1679 |
| All claims of blood revenge from the age of ignorance are cancelled, beginning with that of his own kinsman. | Rule of law; end of private vengeance | Muslim 1218 |
| All usury of the age of ignorance is cancelled, beginning with that owed to his uncle Abbas. | Economic justice; freedom from exploitation | Muslim 1218 |
| "Fear Allah concerning women. You have taken them as a trust from Allah." Mutual rights of spouses; provide for them fairly. | Women's rights | Muslim 1218 |
| "I leave among you that which, if you hold to it, you will not go astray: the Book of Allah." | Supremacy of law | Muslim 1218 (Muwatta, Book of Decree, no. 3 adds "and the Sunnah of His Prophet") |
| No superiority of Arab over non-Arab or white over black except by piety; all are from Adam. | Equality; ban on racism | Musnad Ahmad [number not verified] |
| No one is to be punished for another's crime; a father is not answerable for his son. | Individual responsibility | Tirmidhi 2159; Ibn Majah 2669 |
| Believers are brothers; nothing of a brother's is lawful except what he gives willingly. | Brotherhood; property | Seerah of Ibn Hisham; Musnad Ahmad |
| Treat those under your authority well; feed and clothe them as yourselves. | Rights of servants and workers | Bukhari 30 (same teaching) |
| "Do not turn back after me to disbelief, striking one another's necks." | Peace and internal security | Bukhari 121, 1739 |
| "Let those present convey to those absent." | Right to knowledge; duty to inform | Bukhari 67, 1741 |

**Why it is a foundational document**

1. First: 6 centuries before Magna Carta (1215) and 13 before the UDHR (1948).
2. Universal: addressed "O people", not one tribe.
3. Self-applied: he began with his own family's claims.
4. Binding: religious duty, not a political concession.
5. Complete for its time: covers life, property, honour, equality, women, workers, economic justice and law.
6. Implemented: the Rightly Guided Caliphs enforced it.

**Comparison in one line:** Magna Carta protected barons against a king; the Farewell Sermon protected every human being against every other.

### 6. Islam and the UDHR: comparative and critical analysis (asked 2026)

**The UDHR.** Adopted by the UN General Assembly on 10 December 1948 in Paris after the Second World War; 30 articles; not a treaty, but the basis of later covenants (ICCPR and ICESCR, 1966). Pakistan voted for it.

**Muslim responses.** The Universal Islamic Declaration of Human Rights (Islamic Council, 1981) and the Cairo Declaration on Human Rights in Islam (OIC, 1990). Maududi's *Human Rights in Islam* (1976) is a standard short text.

**Common ground**

| UDHR | Islam |
|---|---|
| Art. 1: all are born free and equal in dignity | Al-Isra 17:70; Al-Hujurat 49:13 |
| Art. 3: life, liberty, security | Al-Ma'idah 5:32 |
| Art. 4: no slavery | Bukhari 2227; steady encouragement to free slaves (Al-Balad 90:13) |
| Art. 5: no torture | Muslim 1731; ban on mutilation |
| Art. 7, 10, 11: equality before law, fair trial, presumption of innocence | An-Nisa 4:58; Bukhari 3475; burden of proof on the claimant |
| Art. 12: privacy | An-Nur 24:27; Al-Hujurat 49:12 |
| Art. 14: asylum | At-Tawbah 9:6 |
| Art. 17: property | An-Nisa 4:29 |
| Art. 18: freedom of religion | Al-Baqarah 2:256 |
| Art. 21: participation in government | Ash-Shura 42:38 |
| Art. 22, 25: social security, adequate standard of living | Zakat; At-Tawbah 9:60; Adh-Dhariyat 51:19 |
| Art. 23: work and fair pay | Ibn Majah 2443 |
| Art. 26: education | Al-Alaq 96:1–5 |

**Differences**

| Aspect | UDHR | Islam |
|---|---|---|
| Source | Human agreement among states | Divine revelation |
| Authority | A declaration; moral and political force; applied selectively | Religious duty; accountable before Allah; part of law |
| Rights and duties | Stresses rights; duties in Art. 29 only | Rights and duties joined |
| Philosophy | Secular, individual-centred | God-centred; individual and community balanced |
| Scope | Individual's claims on the state | Also rights of parents, neighbours, orphans, animals, even the environment |
| Limits on freedom | Law, public order, morality as each state defines | Limits set by Shariah |
| Change | Can be amended or reinterpreted | Core fixed; details through ijtihad |
| Sanction | Courts, public opinion | Courts, conscience, Hereafter |

**Areas of debate (state neutrally)**

1. *Article 16* (marriage without limitation of religion) and *Article 18* (freedom to change religion): classical fiqh has rules on interfaith marriage and on apostasy. Contemporary Muslim scholars differ on how those classical rules apply today.
2. *Equality of men and women in family law:* Islam gives equal worth and reward, with some different rules (inheritance shares, maintenance duty) tied to different responsibilities.
3. *Freedom of expression* and its limits regarding sacred persons.
4. *Hudud punishments* and the modern ban on "cruel" punishment.

**Critical evaluation**

- *Of the UDHR in practice:* written mainly by Western powers when much of the Muslim world was colonised; applied selectively (Palestine, Kashmir, Iraq, treatment of refugees, Islamophobia); weak enforcement; strong on civil liberties, weaker on economic justice.
- *Of Muslim practice:* Islamic principles are sound, yet many Muslim states fall short on due process, minority protection, women's inheritance, freedom of expression and labour rights. The gap is between teaching and practice.
- *Balanced conclusion:* the two overlap on most rights. Islam adds a spiritual basis, duties, and an inner sanction. Muslims can support the shared core while keeping their own foundation and working to close their own practice gap.

Full answer: MA-9.

### 7. Rights of non-Muslims in an Islamic state

1. Life, property and honour protected like those of Muslims. Ali (RA) is reported to have said that their blood is like our blood (reported in fiqh and history works).
2. Freedom of worship; places of worship protected (Al-Hajj 22:40). Umar's (RA) assurance to the people of Jerusalem guaranteed their lives, property, churches and crosses (al-Tabari).
3. Personal law: their own courts in family and religious matters.
4. Justice: Al-Ma'idah 5:8, 5:42.
5. Welfare: Umar (RA) gave an old Jewish beggar a stipend from the treasury and excused the old from jizyah (Abu Yusuf, *Kitab al-Kharaj*).
6. Jizyah was a tax in return for protection and exemption from military service; it was refunded when protection could not be given, as Abu Ubaydah (RA) is reported to have done at Hims (Abu Yusuf; al-Baladhuri).
7. The Prophet's (PBUH) warning: whoever wrongs a person under covenant, burdens him beyond his capacity or takes anything from him unwillingly, "I shall be his opponent on the Day of Resurrection." (Sunan Abi Dawud 3052)

Pakistan: Articles 20–22 (freedom of religion), 25 (equality), 36 (protection of minorities).

### 8. Contemporary relevance (human rights)

- Palestine and Kashmir: denial of life, self-determination and worship.
- Islamophobia in the West; hate crimes; bans on religious dress.
- Inside Muslim societies: enforced disappearances, custodial torture, bonded labour, mob violence, denial of women's inheritance, child labour.
- Pakistan: Fundamental Rights, Articles 8–28 of the Constitution; National Commission for Human Rights (2012 Act).
- Recommendations: human-rights education from the Seerah; judicial reform for speedy justice; enforcing women's property rights; protecting minorities; curbing misuse of religion; OIC-level human-rights mechanism with real powers.

## Part B — Status of woman in Islam

### 1. Women before Islam

| Society | Condition |
|---|---|
| Pre-Islamic Arabia | Female infanticide in some tribes (At-Takwir 81:8–9; An-Nahl 16:58–59); women inherited as property (An-Nisa 4:19); no fixed share in inheritance; unlimited polygamy; unrestricted divorce |
| Greece, Rome | Legal minors under father or husband |
| Hindu tradition | Sati; no remarriage for widows; limited property rights in classical law |
| Medieval Europe | Married women's property passed to the husband; in England married women gained independent property rights only by the Married Women's Property Acts of 1870 and 1882 |

Use this to show how early Islam's reforms came. Keep the tone factual.

### 2. Spiritual and human equality

| Point | Reference |
|---|---|
| Same origin | "Created you from a single soul and created from it its mate." (An-Nisa 4:1) |
| Same worth of deeds | "I do not let the work of any worker among you, male or female, be lost. You are of one another." (Al Imran 3:195) |
| Same reward | "Whoever does good, male or female, and is a believer, We shall give a good life." (An-Nahl 16:97; An-Nisa 4:124) |
| Same religious duties and virtues, listed in pairs | Al-Ahzab 33:35 |
| Partners in public duty | "Believing men and believing women are allies of one another; they enjoin right and forbid wrong." (At-Tawbah 9:71) |
| No blame on Eve alone | The Quran holds both Adam and his wife responsible, and both were forgiven (Al-A'raf 7:20–23) |
| "Women are the counterparts of men." | Sunan Abi Dawud 236; Jami at-Tirmidhi 113 |
| Models for all believers | The wife of Pharaoh and Maryam (At-Tahrim 66:11–12); Maryam chosen above the women of the worlds (Al Imran 3:42) |

### 3. Rights of women

**A. As a daughter (asked 2023)**

1. Right to life: infanticide condemned (At-Takwir 81:8–9; Al-Isra 17:31).
2. Her birth is good news; dislike of it is condemned (An-Nahl 16:58–59).
3. Love and good upbringing: "Whoever brings up two girls until they come of age, he and I will come on the Day of Resurrection like this," joining his fingers (Sahih Muslim 2631). Daughters are a shield from the Fire for one who treats them well (Sahih al-Bukhari 5995; Sunan Ibn Majah 3669).
4. No preference of sons: whoever has a daughter and does not bury her alive, does not humiliate her and does not prefer his sons over her, Allah will admit him to Paradise (Sunan Abi Dawud 5146; some scholars grade its chain weak, but the meaning is supported by the sound reports above).
5. Equal treatment in gifts: "Fear Allah and be just among your children." (Sahih al-Bukhari 2587; Sahih Muslim 1623)
6. Education (Sunan Ibn Majah 224 applies to every Muslim).
7. Consent in marriage: "A virgin is not to be married until her permission is sought." (Sahih al-Bukhari 5136). A girl married by her father against her will was given the choice by the Prophet (PBUH) (Sunan Abi Dawud 2096; Sahih al-Bukhari 5138 for a previously married woman).
8. Maintenance by her father until marriage.
9. Inheritance (An-Nisa 4:11).
10. The Prophet's (PBUH) own example with Fatimah (RA): "Fatimah is a part of me." (Sahih al-Bukhari 3714)

*How Muslims deny these today:* son preference; less schooling for girls; forced and early marriages; exchange marriages (*watta satta*), *vani/swara*, marriage to the Quran (customs with no basis in Islam); honour killing; denial of inheritance by forcing daughters to give up their share; dowry demands. *Remedies:* enforcing the law (Pakistan's Prevention of Anti-Women Practices Act 2011; anti-honour-killing amendments 2016; Enforcement of Women's Property Rights Act 2020); education; mosque and media campaigns; automatic transfer of inherited shares in land records.

**B. As a wife**

1. Marriage as tranquillity, love and mercy (Ar-Rum 30:21); spouses are garments for each other (Al-Baqarah 2:187).
2. Consent; a "solemn covenant" (An-Nisa 4:21).
3. Dower (*mahr*) is her own property (An-Nisa 4:4).
4. Maintenance and housing are the husband's duty (An-Nisa 4:34; At-Talaq 65:6). The best spending is on one's family (Sahih Muslim 995).
5. Kind treatment: "Live with them in kindness." (An-Nisa 4:19). "The best of you are the best to their wives." (Jami at-Tirmidhi 1162, 3895). "Treat women well." (Sahih al-Bukhari 3331; Sahih Muslim 1468)
6. Her rights mirror her duties: "They have rights similar to those over them, according to what is fair." (Al-Baqarah 2:228)
7. Right to seek dissolution (*khul'*): the wife of Thabit ibn Qays (Sahih al-Bukhari 5273). Arbitration before break-up (An-Nisa 4:35).
8. Protection in divorce: release with kindness; no taking back the dower; provision for the divorced (Al-Baqarah 2:229, 2:231, 2:241).
9. She keeps her own name, property and legal identity.

**C. As a mother**

1. Kindness to parents is set next to the worship of Allah (Al-Isra 17:23–24).
2. The mother's special hardship is stressed (Luqman 31:14; Al-Ahqaf 46:15).
3. "Who most deserves my good company? Your mother, then your mother, then your mother, then your father." (Sahih al-Bukhari 5971; Sahih Muslim 2548)
4. A man wishing to go on jihad was told to stay with his mother, "for Paradise is beneath her feet" (Sunan an-Nasa'i 3104).

**D. Economic rights**

1. Ownership and earning: "For men is a share of what they earn, and for women a share of what they earn." (An-Nisa 4:32)
2. Inheritance: "For women is a share of what parents and near relatives leave, be it little or much, an obligatory share." (An-Nisa 4:7)
3. Dower and maintenance.
4. Freedom to trade and manage her own wealth without the husband's permission. Khadijah (RA) was a leading merchant.
5. No duty to spend on the household.

**E. Social, educational and political rights**

1. Education: women asked for and were given their own teaching day (Sahih al-Bukhari 101). Aisha (RA) narrated over two thousand ahadith and corrected senior Companions.
2. Worship in the mosque: "Do not stop the women servants of Allah from the mosques of Allah." (Sahih al-Bukhari 900; Sahih Muslim 442)
3. Political pledge: women gave the pledge of allegiance separately (Al-Mumtahanah 60:12).
4. Voice in public affairs: a woman's complaint was heard from above the heavens (Al-Mujadilah 58:1). Umm Salamah's (RA) advice resolved the crisis at Hudaybiyyah (Sahih al-Bukhari 2731). A report says a woman corrected Umar (RA) in public on limiting dowers and he accepted; its chain is disputed, so call it "a well-known report".
5. Giving protection: the Prophet (PBUH) honoured the asylum granted by Umm Hani (RA) (Sahih al-Bukhari 3171).
6. Work and public service: women nursed the wounded in battle (Sahih al-Bukhari 2882). Umar (RA) is reported to have given al-Shifa bint Abdullah a role in supervising the market (reported in biographical works; some scholars question the report).
7. Honour protected: slander of chaste women is a punishable crime (An-Nur 24:4, 24:23).
8. Testimony and legal personality: she can sue, contract and bear witness.

### 4. Women's inheritance (asked 2017)

**Principle.** Before Islam women generally did not inherit. An-Nisa 4:7 gave them a fixed, compulsory share. The occasion reported for the inheritance verses is the complaint of the widow of Sa'd ibn al-Rabi' (RA), whose brother-in-law had taken the whole estate, leaving her two daughters nothing; the Prophet (PBUH) then ordered two-thirds for the daughters and one-eighth for the widow (Jami at-Tirmidhi 2092; Sunan Abi Dawud 2891).

**Shares fixed by the Quran (An-Nisa 4:11, 4:12, 4:176)**

| Woman | Share |
|---|---|
| Daughter (one, no son) | 1/2 |
| Daughters (two or more, no son) | 2/3 together |
| Daughter with son | Half the son's share (residuary) |
| Mother | 1/6 if the deceased left a child (or siblings); 1/3 otherwise |
| Wife | 1/8 if the husband left a child; 1/4 otherwise |
| Full sister (no children, no father) | 1/2; two or more share 2/3; with a brother, half his share |
| Uterine sister | 1/6; two or more share 1/3 equally with uterine brothers |
| Grandmother | 1/6 (established by Sunnah and the practice of the Caliphs) |

**"Why half?"** Answer in four points.

1. It is not a general rule. The 2:1 ratio applies to sons with daughters and brothers with sisters. In several cases male and female get equal shares (father and mother each get 1/6 when there are children; uterine brothers and sisters share equally). In some cases a woman gets more than a male relative, or inherits where he does not.
2. Financial duties differ. The man must pay the dower, maintain wife and children, and support dependent female relatives. The woman keeps her share entirely for herself.
3. She also receives dower and maintenance, which he does not.
4. The system must be judged as a whole: inheritance, dower, maintenance and the ban on exploiting her wealth together.

**Safeguards:** bequests cannot exceed one-third or cut out legal heirs; "these are the limits of Allah" (An-Nisa 4:13–14); consuming an heir's share is condemned (An-Nisa 4:10 on orphans; Al-Fajr 89:19).

**Practice in Pakistan:** many women never receive their share; common devices are pressure to "forgive" the share to brothers, false gift deeds, and marriage within the family to keep land. Law: Section 498-A of the Pakistan Penal Code (2011) makes depriving a woman of inheritance a crime; the Enforcement of Women's Property Rights Act 2020 gives the Ombudsperson power to restore possession. Suggest automatic mutation of land to all heirs, legal aid, and awareness through Friday sermons.

### 5. Common objections and balanced replies

| Issue | Reply |
|---|---|
| **Men are "in charge" (qawwamun), An-Nisa 4:34** | The word means maintainers and protectors. It is a duty of provision and care within the family, linked in the verse to spending. It is not general superiority of men over women. Decisions are to be by consultation (Al-Baqarah 2:233). Scholars differ on the details of this verse; state the mainstream reading. |
| **"A degree" for men, Al-Baqarah 2:228** | The verse first states that rights are mutual. The degree is read by most commentators as the husband's extra responsibility. |
| **Polygamy, An-Nisa 4:3** | Islam restricted an unlimited practice to four and made justice a condition: "if you fear you will not be just, then one". It is a permission, not a command. An-Nisa 4:129 warns that perfect equality is beyond one's power. Pakistan's Muslim Family Laws Ordinance 1961 requires permission of the Arbitration Council. |
| **Testimony, Al-Baqarah 2:282** | The verse concerns written financial contracts and gives a reason (one may remind the other). Jurists differ on how far it extends. In many matters a woman's sole testimony is accepted, and women's narration of hadith is accepted equally with men's. |
| **Hijab, An-Nur 24:30–31; Al-Ahzab 33:59** | Modesty is commanded first to men (24:30), then to women. The stated purpose is dignity and protection from harassment. Jurists differ on details of dress. |
| **Divorce** | Men have the right of talaq with duties attached; women have khul', delegated divorce (*talaq al-tafwid*) and judicial dissolution. Divorce is lawful but disliked. |
| **Leadership** | Jurists differ on the highest office. Women have served as scholars, judges in the view of some jurists, and in Pakistan as prime minister, speaker and judges. |

### 6. Islam and feminism (asked 2019)

**Feminism in brief.** First wave (19th–early 20th century): legal personality, property, the vote. Second wave (1960s–80s): workplace equality, family law, bodily autonomy. Third and fourth waves (1990s onward): identity, intersectionality, online activism (#MeToo, 2017). In Pakistan: the Aurat March since 2018 and the debates around it.

**Where Islam and feminism agree**

- Education, property, inheritance, consent in marriage, dignity, protection from violence and harassment, equal pay for equal work (An-Nisa 4:32), political participation.
- Islam granted several of these in the 7th century; Western law reached them in the 19th and 20th.

**Where they differ**

| Feminism (main secular strands) | Islam |
|---|---|
| Based on individual autonomy | Based on servanthood to Allah, for both sexes |
| Often seeks sameness of roles | Equal worth, complementary roles in the family, with freedom to work |
| Sees the family as a possible site of oppression | Sees the family as the basic unit to be protected, with justice inside it |
| Rights language | Rights with duties |
| Sexual freedom as liberation | Chastity for both sexes (An-Nur 24:30–31) |

**Islamic feminism / Muslim women's scholarship.** Some Muslim women scholars argue for women's rights from within the Quran and Sunnah, separating religion from patriarchal custom. Mention this as an existing trend without taking sides.

**Balanced conclusion.** The real problem for Muslim women is rarely Islamic law; it is the denial of rights that Islam already gives, through custom, poverty and weak enforcement. The answer is to implement those rights, not to import or reject a foreign ideology wholesale.

### 7. Women of achievement to cite

Khadijah (RA), merchant and first believer; Aisha (RA), jurist and narrator; Umm Salamah (RA), counsellor; Fatimah (RA); Sumayyah (RA), first martyr; Nusaybah bint Ka'b (RA), who defended the Prophet (PBUH) at Uhud; Rufaydah al-Aslamiyyah (RA), who ran a field hospital tent (Seerah works); al-Shifa bint Abdullah (RA), teacher; Fatimah al-Fihri, founder of al-Qarawiyyin in Fez (859 CE); in Pakistan, Fatima Jinnah and Benazir Bhutto.

### 8. Rights compared with other religions (asked 2021)

Keep this respectful and brief. Points to make:

1. Islam's rights are textual, detailed and legally enforceable: inheritance shares, dower, consent, divorce rights.
2. Spiritual equality is explicit (Al-Ahzab 33:35).
3. No doctrine of woman as the source of sin.
4. No celibacy as the ideal; marriage is honoured.
5. Rights of men are also defined: to respect, to lawful earnings, to obedience in what is right within the family, to good upbringing of children.
6. Acknowledge that all major religions teach compassion and that their modern followers have reformed many old customs. Then conclude that Islam's distinct contribution is the early legal form it gave to these rights.
