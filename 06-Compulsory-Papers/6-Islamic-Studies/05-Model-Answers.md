## MA-1. What is the difference between Deen and religion? Explain the importance of Deen in human life.

### 1. Introduction

The Quran does not call Islam a "religion" in the narrow sense of a private creed with a few rituals. It calls Islam *al-Din*: "The religion (din) in the sight of Allah is Islam" (Al Imran 3:19). The word *din* covers belief, worship, morals, law and the whole ordering of life under Allah. The modern word "religion", shaped by the Western separation of church and state, usually means a personal relationship with God that stays out of public affairs. The difference between the two is therefore one of scope. This answer first explains the two terms, then sets out the differences, and then shows why human beings, as individuals and as a society, cannot do without Deen.

### 2. Meaning of the terms

**Religion.** From the Latin *religio*. In common usage it means a set of beliefs about the divine and the acts of worship that go with them. In secular thought it is a private matter of conscience.

**Deen.** The Arabic word carries several linked meanings in the Quran:

- Judgment and recompense: "Master of the Day of Recompense" (Al-Fatihah 1:4).
- Obedience and sincere submission (Al-Bayyinah 98:5).
- Law and system: Yusuf (AS) could not detain his brother "under the king's law (din)" (Yusuf 12:76).
- A complete way of life (Al Imran 3:19; Al-Ma'idah 5:3).

Maulana Maududi, studying these uses, concluded that *din* means a whole system in which a supreme authority is acknowledged, obeyed, and looked to for reward and punishment. In Islam that authority is Allah alone.

### 3. Differences between Deen and religion

| Aspect | Religion (narrow sense) | Deen |
|---|---|---|
| Scope | Belief and ritual | All of life |
| Sphere | Private | Private and public |
| Law | Few rules of worship | A complete code, the Shariah |
| State and economy | Left to worldly authority | Guided by revelation |
| World and Hereafter | Often one at the cost of the other | Both, in balance |
| Clergy | Often a priestly class | No priesthood; direct access to Allah |
| Aim | Personal salvation | Personal salvation and a just society |

Three points deserve explanation.

**(a) Completeness.** Allah commands: "O you who believe, enter into Islam completely" (Al-Baqarah 2:208), and declares: "This day I have perfected for you your religion" (Al-Ma'idah 5:3). The Prophet (PBUH) is told to say: "My prayer, my sacrifice, my living and my dying are for Allah, Lord of the worlds" (Al-An'am 6:162).

**(b) Unity of the spiritual and the worldly.** The verse on righteousness joins belief, charity, prayer, keeping promises and patience in a single definition (Al-Baqarah 2:177). The Quran legislates on trade and debt (Al-Baqarah 2:275, 2:282), inheritance (An-Nisa 4:11), judging (An-Nisa 4:58) and war and peace (Al-Anfal 8:61).

**(c) A living model.** The Prophet (PBUH) was at once teacher, head of family, judge, head of state and commander, and the Quran presents his whole life as the example (Al-Ahzab 33:21).

In ordinary English, Islam is also called a religion, and there is no harm in that. The distinction matters only to prevent Islam from being reduced to private ritual.

### 4. Importance of Deen in human life

#### A. For the individual

**(i) It answers the basic questions of existence.** Who made me, and why? "I did not create jinn and mankind except to worship Me" (Adh-Dhariyat 51:56). "Did you think that We created you without purpose?" (Al-Mu'minun 23:115). Without an answer, life has no direction.

**(ii) It satisfies human nature.** Faith is part of the nature on which Allah created people (Ar-Rum 30:30). The Prophet (PBUH) said: "Every child is born on the natural disposition" (Sahih al-Bukhari; Sahih Muslim).

**(iii) It gives peace of heart.** "Truly, in the remembrance of Allah hearts find rest" (Ar-Ra'd 13:28). Wealth and technology have not removed anxiety and loneliness from modern life.

**(iv) It provides a fixed standard of right and wrong.** Human opinion shifts with interest and fashion. "This Quran guides to what is most upright" (Al-Isra 17:9).

**(v) It builds character through inner accountability.** A believer knows that Allah is "nearer to him than his jugular vein" (Qaf 50:16) and that "whoever does an atom's weight of good will see it" (Az-Zalzalah 99:7). This restrains him where no law can reach.

**(vi) It balances body and soul.** "Seek the home of the Hereafter through what Allah has given you, and do not forget your share of this world" (Al-Qasas 28:77). Islam rejects both monasticism and materialism.

**(vii) It gives patience and hope.** The believer meets loss with the words "We belong to Allah and to Him we return" (Al-Baqarah 2:156) and never despairs of His mercy (Az-Zumar 39:53).

#### B. For society

**(i) Human equality and unity.** All people come from one soul (An-Nisa 4:1), and "the most honoured of you before Allah is the most righteous" (Al-Hujurat 49:13).

**(ii) Justice.** Messengers were sent with the Book and the Balance "so that people may uphold justice" (Al-Hadid 57:25).

**(iii) Protection of life, property and honour.** The Farewell Sermon declared them sacred (Sahih al-Bukhari).

**(iv) A stable family.** Marriage is built on "love and mercy" (Ar-Rum 30:21), and kindness to parents is placed next to the worship of Allah (Al-Isra 17:23).

**(v) A fair economy.** Zakat, the ban on riba and the rule that wealth must not "circulate only among the rich" (Al-Hashr 59:7).

**(vi) Accountable government.** Authority is a trust to be handed to those fit for it (An-Nisa 4:58), and "each of you is a shepherd and each of you is responsible for his flock" (Sahih al-Bukhari).

**(vii) Brotherhood and mutual care.** "The believers are but brothers" (Al-Hujurat 49:10).

#### C. For civilization

Knowledge and power need a moral direction. The twentieth century showed what science without values can do: two world wars, nuclear weapons, damage to the planet. The Quran links the fate of nations to their moral state: "Allah does not change the condition of a people until they change what is in themselves" (Ar-Ra'd 13:11). Ibn Khaldun made the same point in the *Muqaddimah*: injustice and luxury ruin civilizations.

### 5. Views of scholars

Shah Waliullah, in *Hujjatullah al-Balighah*, showed that the commands of the Shariah serve human welfare. Allama Iqbal held that religion gives the individual an inner anchor that science cannot supply. Muhammad Asad, in *Islam at the Crossroads*, described Islam as a programme of life and not one compartment of it.

### 6. Contemporary relevance

Pakistan was created so that Muslims could order their collective life by Islam. The Objectives Resolution and Article 31 of the Constitution state this aim. Yet corruption, dishonesty in trade, intolerance and injustice persist beside crowded mosques. The cause is that many treat Islam as "religion", a set of rituals, and not as Deen that governs the office, the market and the court. At the global level, moral confusion over the family, wealth and new technologies shows the cost of confining faith to private life.

### 7. Conclusion

Religion in the narrow sense touches one corner of life; Deen claims the whole of it. Islam is Deen: a complete, balanced and final guidance. The individual needs it for purpose, peace and character; society needs it for justice, unity and welfare; civilization needs it for direction. The Quran's summons remains: "Enter into Islam completely" (Al-Baqarah 2:208).

## MA-2. What are the rational arguments for the existence of Allah? Explain the effects of belief in Tawhid on human life.

### 1. Introduction

Belief in one God is the first article of Islam and the foundation of everything else in it. The Quran does not ask for blind faith. It repeatedly addresses "people who reason" and "people of understanding" and offers arguments from the universe, from the human self and from history. *Tawhid* means to hold that Allah is One in His being, His attributes and His right to be worshipped: "Say: He is Allah, the One; Allah, the Eternal Refuge; He neither begets nor is born; and there is none equal to Him" (Al-Ikhlas 112:1–4). This answer first gives the rational arguments for His existence and oneness, and then explains what this belief does to the individual and to society.

### 2. Rational arguments for the existence of Allah

**(i) The argument from causation.** Nothing comes into being without a cause. The universe exists and had a beginning, so it has a cause beyond itself. The Quran puts it in three short questions: "Were they created by nothing, or were they their own creators? Or did they create the heavens and the earth?" (At-Tur 52:35–36). Each alternative is impossible, so a Creator remains.

**(ii) The argument from design and order.** The universe is not chaos. The sun and moon keep their courses; night follows day; rain revives dead land. "You see no flaw in the creation of the Most Merciful. Look again: do you see any gap?" (Al-Mulk 67:3). "In the creation of the heavens and the earth and the alternation of night and day are signs for people of understanding" (Al Imran 3:190). Order points to knowledge, and purpose points to will.

**(iii) The argument from human nature.** In every age and land people have turned to a higher power, above all in danger. The Quran calls this the *fitrah* on which Allah created people (Ar-Rum 30:30) and recalls the covenant: "Am I not your Lord?" They said: "Yes" (Al-A'raf 7:172).

**(iv) The argument from the self.** "On the earth are signs for those with certainty, and in yourselves. Do you not see?" (Adh-Dhariyat 51:20–21). The development of a human being from a drop to a thinking, moral person is itself evidence.

**(v) The argument from the unity of the system.** One set of laws runs through the whole universe. Two independent wills would have clashed: "Had there been gods besides Allah in the heavens and the earth, both would have fallen into ruin" (Al-Anbiya 21:22). This proves not only that God exists but that He is One.

**(vi) The moral argument.** Every person knows a sense of right and wrong (Ash-Shams 91:7–8) and demands justice. In this world justice is often incomplete. A moral law implies a Lawgiver, and the demand for final justice implies a Judge.

**(vii) The argument from contingency.** Everything in the world depends on something else and might not have existed. The chain of dependent things must end in a Being who exists by Himself. Muslim thinkers such as Ibn Sina and Imam al-Ghazali developed this reasoning. The Quran states the fact: "O mankind, you are in need of Allah, and Allah is the Self-Sufficient" (Fatir 35:15).

**(viii) The testimony of the prophets and of revelation.** Truthful men in every nation brought one message: "Can there be doubt about Allah, Creator of the heavens and the earth?" (Ibrahim 14:10). The Quran challenges anyone who doubts its source to produce a chapter like it (Al-Baqarah 2:23).

**(ix) The reasoning of Ibrahim (AS).** He looked at the star, the moon and the sun, saw each set, and concluded that what fades cannot be Lord (Al-An'am 6:75–79).

Modern science, which finds that the universe had a beginning and that its laws are finely balanced for life, agrees with these arguments. It serves as illustration.

### 3. Effects of Tawhid on the individual

**(i) Dignity and freedom.** One who bows to Allah alone bows to no one else. Iqbal expressed it in verse: the one prostration that a person finds heavy frees him from a thousand others.

**(ii) Freedom from fear and superstition.** "If Allah touches you with harm, none can remove it but He" (Yunus 10:107). The believer does not fear rulers, stars or omens.

**(iii) Humility.** Every gift is from Allah, so there is no ground for pride.

**(iv) Courage and trust.** "Whoever puts his trust in Allah, He is sufficient for him" (At-Talaq 65:3).

**(v) Peace and contentment.** "In the remembrance of Allah hearts find rest" (Ar-Ra'd 13:28).

**(vi) Responsibility.** Allah is "nearer to him than his jugular vein" (Qaf 50:16). Hidden sin is not hidden from Him.

**(vii) Hope.** "Do not despair of the mercy of Allah" (Az-Zumar 39:53).

**(viii) Unity of purpose.** Life has one aim, the pleasure of Allah (Al-An'am 6:162), so the believer is not torn between many masters.

**(ix) Broad vision.** The Lord of all the worlds is not the God of one tribe or nation; the believer's sympathy extends to all creation. Maulana Maududi lists these effects in *Towards Understanding Islam*.

### 4. Effects of Tawhid on society

**(i) Equality.** One Creator means one human family (An-Nisa 4:1). "The most honoured of you before Allah is the most righteous" (Al-Hujurat 49:13). Bilal (RA), a freed slave, gave the call to prayer from the roof of the Ka'bah.

**(ii) End of man's lordship over man.** No king, priest or class holds divine status. Rulers are trustees (An-Nisa 4:58).

**(iii) Rule of law.** The Prophet (PBUH) said that if his own daughter stole he would apply the penalty (Sahih al-Bukhari). All stand under one law because all stand under one Lord.

**(iv) Unity.** One God, one Book, one qiblah bind people of every race: "Hold firmly to the rope of Allah all together" (Al Imran 3:103).

**(v) Justice and accountability.** Since power is a trust from Allah, its holder answers for it: "Each of you is a shepherd" (Sahih al-Bukhari).

**(vi) Growth of knowledge.** Nature is creation, not deity. It can be studied and used: "He has made subject to you all that is in the heavens and on the earth" (Al-Jathiyah 45:13). This outlook helped Muslims develop the experimental sciences.

**(vii) A moral public life.** The same God is obeyed in the mosque, the market and the court.

### 5. Contemporary relevance

The idols of today are not made of stone. Wealth, status and consumer goods are worshipped in practice. Nationalism and racism raise the group to the place of God. Extremists treat their own opinion as divine and kill for it. Tawhid corrects each of these. In Pakistan, where shrines are sometimes misused, where people fear the powerful more than Allah, and where sect and tribe divide the community, a return to the real meaning of Tawhid would restore both courage and unity.

### 6. Conclusion

Reason leads to the existence of Allah by many roads: causation, design, human nature, the unity of the cosmos and the moral law. Revelation confirms it and adds what reason cannot reach. The belief that results is not a dry doctrine. It frees the individual from fear and servility and gives society equality, justice and unity. As the Quran says of every messenger's call: "There is no god but Me, so worship Me" (Al-Anbiya 21:25).

## MA-3. What are the Quranic arguments for life after death? Discuss the effects of this belief on the individual and collective life of human beings.

### 1. Introduction

Belief in the Hereafter (*Akhirah*) is the third fundamental of Islam, after belief in Allah and in His messengers. It means that death is not the end: every person will be raised, shown the record of his deeds and judged with perfect justice, and will then enter Paradise or Hell. "Every soul will taste death, and you will be paid your full reward only on the Day of Resurrection" (Al Imran 3:185). The Makkan chapters of the Quran return to this theme again and again because the Arabs of the time denied it: "They say: there is nothing but our worldly life" (Al-Jathiyah 45:24). The Quran answers them with arguments drawn from nature, reason and the moral sense. This belief then shapes the whole conduct of the believer and of his society.

### 2. Quranic arguments for life after death

**(i) The argument from the first creation.** The denier asks: "Who will give life to bones when they have decayed?" The reply: "He who produced them the first time will give them life" (Ya-Sin 36:78–79). To make again is no harder than to make at first: "He begins creation and then repeats it, and that is easier for Him" (Ar-Rum 30:27).

**(ii) The argument from the greater creation.** "Is not He who created the heavens and the earth able to create the like of them?" (Ya-Sin 36:81). The One who made the galaxies can remake a human being.

**(iii) The argument from the revival of dead earth.** Every year barren land comes to life after rain. "You see the earth lifeless, then when We send down rain upon it, it stirs and swells ... He who gives it life is the Giver of life to the dead" (Fussilat 41:39; see also Al-Hajj 22:5–7).

**(iv) The argument from the stages of human growth.** Man passes from dust to a drop, to a clot, to a formed body, to birth, strength and old age (Al-Hajj 22:5). Each stage is a new creation. One more stage after death is not strange.

**(v) The argument from purpose.** A wise Creator does not act in vain. "Did you think that We created you without purpose and that you would not be returned to Us?" (Al-Mu'minun 23:115). "Does man think he will be left unaccountable?" (Al-Qiyamah 75:36). If death ended everything, human life, with all its moral striving, would be pointless.

**(vi) The argument from justice.** In this world the tyrant often dies in comfort and the honest man in hardship. "Do those who commit evil think We shall make them like those who believe and do good, equal in life and death? Evil is their judgment" (Al-Jathiyah 45:21). "Shall We treat those who believe and do good like those who spread corruption on earth?" (Sad 38:28). Allah's justice requires a Day when accounts are settled.

**(vii) The argument from Allah's power and knowledge.** "Does man think We shall not assemble his bones? Yes, We are able to restore even his fingertips" (Al-Qiyamah 75:3–4).

**(viii) The witness of conscience.** Allah swears "by the self-reproaching soul" (Al-Qiyamah 75:2). The inner voice that blames a person for wrong is a small court pointing to the great one.

**(ix) Demonstration to the prophets.** Ibrahim (AS) asked to be shown how the dead are raised, to satisfy his heart, and was shown (Al-Baqarah 2:260).

### 3. What the belief includes

Death; the interval of *barzakh* (Al-Mu'minun 23:100); the Hour; resurrection; the record of deeds, which "leaves out nothing small or great" (Al-Kahf 18:49); the scales of justice (Al-Anbiya 21:47); the witness of one's own limbs (Ya-Sin 36:65); and the final reward or punishment.

### 4. Effects on the individual

**(i) A living sense of accountability.** "Whoever does an atom's weight of good will see it, and whoever does an atom's weight of evil will see it" (Az-Zalzalah 99:7–8). The Prophet (PBUH) said that a person's feet will not move on the Day of Resurrection until he is asked about his life, his knowledge, his wealth, how he earned and spent it, and his body (Jami at-Tirmidhi).

**(ii) Self-restraint in private.** "As for him who feared standing before his Lord and restrained the self from desire, Paradise will be his home" (An-Nazi'at 79:40–41). No policeman is needed.

**(iii) Purpose and seriousness.** Life is a test: "He created death and life to test which of you is best in deed" (Al-Mulk 67:2).

**(iv) Patience and courage.** Loss in this world is not final. The believer says: "We belong to Allah and to Him we return" (Al-Baqarah 2:156).

**(v) Freedom from greed.** "The Hereafter is better and more lasting" (Al-A'la 87:17). Wealth becomes a means, not an end.

**(vi) Sincerity.** Good is done without wish for thanks: "We feed you only for the sake of Allah" (Al-Insan 76:9).

**(vii) Constant self-examination.** "Let every soul look to what it has sent ahead for tomorrow" (Al-Hashr 59:18).

### 5. Effects on collective life

**(i) Honest dealing.** The Quran warns the fraudulent trader with the question: "Do they not think that they will be raised for a mighty Day?" (Al-Mutaffifin 83:4–5).

**(ii) Just and humble rulers.** Authority is a charge to be answered for. Umar (RA) is reported to have feared that he would be questioned even about an animal that came to harm on a distant road. The Prophet (PBUH) warned that a ruler who dies cheating his people is barred from Paradise (Sahih al-Bukhari; Sahih Muslim).

**(iii) Reliable justice.** Judge and witness know of a higher court: "Stand firm for justice, as witnesses for Allah, even against yourselves" (An-Nisa 4:135).

**(iv) Care for the weak.** The Quran links denial of the Recompense with harshness to the orphan and neglect of the poor (Al-Ma'un 107:1–3). Belief produces the opposite.

**(v) Low crime and low corruption.** The law catches a few offenders after the act. Belief prevents the act. The "bankrupt" on the Day of Judgment is one who prayed and fasted but wronged people; his good deeds are handed to his victims (Sahih Muslim).

**(vi) Sacrifice for the common good.** People give wealth and life for a reward they will not see here.

**(vii) Social peace.** The wronged can bear patiently, knowing that no injustice will go unanswered.

### 6. Contrast with the material view

Where people believe only in this life, the practical rule becomes: take what you can and avoid being caught. Laws multiply, and so do ways around them. Much of the corruption, tax evasion, adulteration and misuse of office in our own society comes from this weakness of inner accountability, even among those who profess the faith.

### 7. Contemporary relevance

Pakistan has many anti-corruption laws and institutions, yet the problem remains. The missing element is the conviction that every file signed and every rupee taken will be presented in a court where no influence works. Education and training of public servants should revive this conviction beside the legal controls. At the world level, belief in the Hereafter answers the despair and aimlessness that accompany material plenty.

### 8. Conclusion

The Quran argues for life after death from the first creation, the revival of the earth, the purposefulness of the Creator and the demand for justice. The belief turns each person into his own watchman and gives society honest markets, fair courts and accountable rulers. Without it, morality has no firm footing; with it, a person lives by the prayer the Quran teaches: "Our Lord, give us good in this world and good in the Hereafter" (Al-Baqarah 2:201).

## MA-4. Explain the doctrine of Prophethood and its importance in human life. Why is Prophethood final with Muhammad (PBUH)?

### 1. Introduction

Prophethood (*Risalat*) is the channel through which Allah guides mankind. Reason and nature tell a person that God exists; they do not tell him how to worship, what is lawful, or what lies beyond death. For this Allah chose human beings, gave them revelation and sent them to their peoples. "We sent Our messengers with clear proofs, and sent down with them the Book and the Balance, so that people may uphold justice" (Al-Hadid 57:25). The line began with Adam (AS) and ended with Muhammad (PBUH), "the Messenger of Allah and the Seal of the Prophets" (Al-Ahzab 33:40). Belief in all the prophets, and in the finality of the last, is a fundamental of Islam.

### 2. Meaning

*Risalah* means message; a *rasul* is one sent with a message. A *nabi* is one who receives news from Allah. Many scholars explain that a messenger brings a new law or scripture, while a prophet may continue the law of an earlier messenger; every messenger is a prophet, but not every prophet is a messenger.

### 3. The need for Prophethood

**(i) Limits of reason.** The mind cannot discover by itself the details of worship, the unseen world or a complete moral code.

**(ii) Justice of Allah.** He does not hold people to account without first informing them: "We never punish until We have sent a messenger" (Al-Isra 17:15); messengers were sent "so that people have no argument against Allah" (An-Nisa 4:165).

**(iii) Need for a living example.** A book alone is not enough. People need to see the teaching lived: "In the Messenger of Allah you have an excellent example" (Al-Ahzab 33:21).

**(iv) Universality of guidance.** "We sent to every nation a messenger: worship Allah and shun false gods" (An-Nahl 16:36). "There was no nation but a warner had passed among them" (Fatir 35:24).

### 4. Qualities of the prophets

- **Chosen by Allah.** Prophethood is a gift; it cannot be earned by effort.
- **Human.** "I am only a human being like you; it is revealed to me" (Al-Kahf 18:110). Being human, they can be followed.
- **Truthful and trustworthy.** Even before his mission Muhammad (PBUH) was known as *al-Sadiq* and *al-Amin*.
- **Protected from error in delivering the message.** "He does not speak from his own desire" (An-Najm 53:3–4).
- **They conveyed the whole message** and taught it in their people's language (Ibrahim 14:4).
- **One faith.** All taught the same creed (Al-Anbiya 21:25), and a believer makes "no distinction between any of His messengers" in belief (Al-Baqarah 2:285).

### 5. The mission of the Prophet Muhammad (PBUH)

The Quran states four tasks: he recites Allah's verses, purifies people, teaches them the Book, and teaches them wisdom (Al-Jumu'ah 62:2). He is described as "a witness, a bringer of good news, a warner, one who calls to Allah, and a lamp giving light" (Al-Ahzab 33:45–46).

His prophethood has features that set it apart:

1. **Universal:** "We have not sent you except to all mankind" (Saba 34:28). He said: "Every prophet was sent to his own people, and I have been sent to all mankind" (Sahih al-Bukhari).
2. **Complete:** "This day I have perfected for you your religion" (Al-Ma'idah 5:3).
3. **Preserved:** the Quran is guarded by Allah (Al-Hijr 15:9), and his Sunnah is recorded in detail.
4. **Merciful:** "We have not sent you except as a mercy to the worlds" (Al-Anbiya 21:107).
5. **A full model:** his life covers every role, private and public.

### 6. Finality of Prophethood

**Evidence**

- Quran: "Muhammad is not the father of any of your men, but the Messenger of Allah and the Seal of the Prophets" (Al-Ahzab 33:40).
- Hadith: "My example among the prophets before me is like a man who built a fine house but left the place of one brick ... I am that brick, and I am the Seal of the Prophets" (Sahih al-Bukhari; Sahih Muslim).
- Hadith: he told Ali (RA): "You are to me as Harun was to Musa, except that there is no prophet after me" (Sahih al-Bukhari; Sahih Muslim).
- Consensus: the Companions, under Abu Bakr (RA), fought those who claimed prophethood after him. The whole Muslim community, in all its schools, has held this belief ever since.

**Reasons why it ended**

1. The message was completed; nothing remained to be added.
2. The scripture is preserved; earlier scriptures had been lost or altered, which had made new prophets necessary.
3. The message is universal; no people is left outside it.
4. Humanity had reached an age of writing, travel and record; the teaching could now reach everyone.
5. The task of conveying it passed to the Ummah: "You are the best community raised for mankind: you enjoin right and forbid wrong" (Al Imran 3:110).
6. New situations are met by *ijtihad* within the final law, and by renewers of the faith in every century (Sunan Abi Dawud).

Allama Iqbal saw in finality a sign that mankind had come of age: revelation having given its final guidance, man must now use reason and experience in its light. In Pakistan the Constitution defines a Muslim as one who believes in the absolute finality of the Prophethood of Muhammad (PBUH) (Article 260).

### 7. Importance of Prophethood in human life

**For the individual**

1. **Certain knowledge** of Allah, of right and wrong and of the Hereafter.
2. **A perfect example** in every situation of life.
3. **Purification of character:** the Prophet (PBUH) said he was sent to perfect good character (Muwatta Malik).
4. **Love that lifts the soul:** "None of you believes until I am dearer to him than his father, his child and all people" (Sahih al-Bukhari).
5. **A sure way to Allah's love:** "If you love Allah, follow me; Allah will love you" (Al Imran 3:31).

**For society**

1. **One law and one model** for a community of many races, which is the basis of its unity.
2. **Authority for the legal order:** "Whoever obeys the Messenger has obeyed Allah" (An-Nisa 4:80); disputes are referred to him (An-Nisa 4:65).
3. **Justice** as the declared aim of the prophetic mission (Al-Hadid 57:25).
4. **Respect for other faiths' prophets,** which opens the door to dialogue with Jews and Christians.
5. **Protection from false claimants and personality cults,** since no one after him can claim divine authority.
6. **A mission for the Ummah:** to carry the message to mankind by word and example.

### 8. Contemporary relevance

The modern world offers many guides: ideologies, celebrities, the market. None gives certainty about the purpose of life. The Seerah supplies a tested model for the family, the economy, government and international relations. For Muslims, the answer to attacks on the Prophet's (PBUH) honour is to make his character visible in their own conduct, while defending his honour by lawful and dignified means. The belief in finality also unites all schools of Muslims and guards them against division.

### 9. Conclusion

Prophethood is Allah's mercy to mankind: guidance delivered through chosen, truthful human beings. It reached its completion in Muhammad (PBUH), whose message is universal, whose Book is preserved and whose life is the pattern for all time. To believe in him is to obey and follow him: "Whatever the Messenger gives you, take it; whatever he forbids you, refrain from it" (Al-Hashr 59:7).

## MA-5. Write a comprehensive note on the Zakat system of Islam and its spiritual, moral and social effects. How can it reduce poverty?

### 1. Introduction

Zakat is the third pillar of Islam and the main institution of its economic and welfare system. The word means both "purification" and "growth". In law it is a fixed share of a Muslim's wealth, due each year once his wealth reaches a set minimum, and payable to categories of people named by the Quran. The Quran joins it with prayer in many places: "Establish prayer and give zakat" (Al-Baqarah 2:43). Prayer is the right of Allah; zakat is the right of His servants. Abu Bakr (RA) fought those who refused to pay it, saying he would not allow prayer and zakat to be separated (Sahih al-Bukhari). It is at once an act of worship and a public financial system.

### 2. Obligation

- "Take from their wealth a charity by which you cleanse and purify them" (At-Tawbah 9:103).
- "Islam is built on five ... giving zakat" (Sahih al-Bukhari; Sahih Muslim).
- The Prophet (PBUH) instructed Mu'adh (RA), on sending him to Yemen, to tell the people that Allah had made obligatory "a charity taken from their rich and given to their poor" (Sahih al-Bukhari).
- Warning: those who hoard gold and silver and do not spend it in Allah's way are promised a painful punishment (At-Tawbah 9:34–35).

### 3. Main rules

| Wealth | Minimum (nisab) | Rate |
|---|---|---|
| Gold, silver, cash, trade goods | 87.48 g gold or 612.36 g silver, or equal value, held one lunar year | 2.5% |
| Farm produce (ushr) | About five *awsuq* by the majority view | 10% rain-fed; 5% irrigated |
| Livestock | Fixed scales | For example, one goat for 40 goats |
| Buried treasure and minerals | — | 20% |

### 4. The eight heads of expenditure

"Zakat is only for the poor, the needy, those employed to collect it, those whose hearts are to be reconciled, freeing captives, those in debt, the cause of Allah, and the traveller: a duty from Allah" (At-Tawbah 9:60).

Two features stand out. The state collects and distributes it through paid officers, so it is an organised system and not private alms. And the heads cannot be changed, so the money cannot be diverted to the general expenses of government.

### 5. Spiritual effects

**(i) Purification of the soul.** The verse itself names this purpose (9:103). Giving breaks the hold of wealth on the heart.

**(ii) Gratitude.** It is thanks for Allah's gifts, and "if you are grateful, I will give you more" (Ibrahim 14:7).

**(iii) Nearness to Allah and His reward.** "Those who spend in the way of Allah are like a grain that grows seven ears, each with a hundred grains" (Al-Baqarah 2:261).

**(iv) Blessing in wealth.** "Charity does not decrease wealth" (Sahih Muslim). "What you give in zakat, seeking Allah's pleasure, is multiplied" (Ar-Rum 30:39).

**(v) Protection from punishment.** Wealth on which zakat was not paid will become a torment for its owner (Sahih al-Bukhari).

### 6. Moral effects

**(i) Generosity in place of greed and miserliness.**

**(ii) Compassion.** The giver learns to feel the need of others.

**(iii) Honesty.** A person assesses his own wealth before Allah.

**(iv) Humility.** The Quran says the poor have a *right* in the wealth of the rich (Adh-Dhariyat 51:19). The giver does no favour, and he is forbidden to follow his gift with reminders and hurt (Al-Baqarah 2:264).

**(v) Self-respect of the receiver.** He takes his due from a public fund. He need not beg.

### 7. Social and economic effects

**(i) Circulation of wealth.** The Quran's rule is that wealth must not "circulate only among the rich among you" (Al-Hashr 59:7). Zakat moves it every year from those who have a surplus to those who lack.

**(ii) Social security.** The poor, the indebted, the stranded traveller and the captive each have a claim. It is a system of social insurance fourteen centuries old.

**(iii) Discouragement of hoarding.** Idle savings shrink by 2.5% a year. The owner is pushed to invest, which creates work.

**(iv) Greater demand and employment.** Money in the hands of the poor is spent on basic goods, which stimulates production.

**(v) Narrowing of class differences.** Envy and resentment fall; crime born of want falls with them.

**(vi) Brotherhood.** Rich and poor are tied by a bond of duty and gratitude: "The believers are but brothers" (Al-Hujurat 49:10).

**(vii) Relief of debt.** The debtor's burden is a named head; Islam also urges creditors to give time (Al-Baqarah 2:280).

**(viii) Strength of the community.** The head "in the cause of Allah" and "reconciling hearts" serve the wider interests of the Muslim society.

### 8. Zakat and the reduction of poverty

1. **It is targeted.** Unlike general taxes, it goes directly to the poor and needy.
2. **It is regular and permanent,** not dependent on a government's mood.
3. **It can build self-reliance.** Classical jurists allowed giving a poor person enough to set up a trade or buy tools, so that he would not need zakat again. The Prophet (PBUH) praised earning by one's own hand and taught that the upper hand is better than the lower (Sahih al-Bukhari).
4. **It works with the rest of the system:** the ban on riba, the law of inheritance, voluntary charity, endowments and interest-free loans.
5. **History supports it.** In the time of Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz, officials in some provinces are reported to have found few people in need of zakat.

Zakat differs from tax. Tax is fixed by the state for general needs and can change; zakat is worship, with rates and recipients fixed by revelation. Paying one does not cancel the other.

### 9. The position in Pakistan

The Zakat and Ushr Ordinance 1980 introduced deduction at source from bank deposits on the first of Ramadan, and a structure of zakat committees. Its weaknesses are well known: a narrow base, since most wealth lies outside savings accounts; widespread exemption declarations; leakage and political use in distribution; poor collection of ushr; and public distrust, so that most people pay privately. Pakistanis are estimated to give very large sums in charity each year, but without coordination the effect on poverty is limited.

**Recommendations**

1. Transparent, digital collection and payment, with published accounts.
2. Use of the national socio-economic registry to identify the deserving.
3. Spending on skills, tools and small enterprise, so that this year's receiver becomes next year's payer.
4. Proper assessment of ushr and of zakat on trade and shares.
5. Partnership with trusted welfare organisations.
6. Education from the pulpit on zakat as a right of the poor.

### 10. Conclusion

Zakat purifies the giver's soul, trains him in generosity, and gives the poor a guaranteed share in society's wealth. It is the oldest working system of social security. If collected honestly and spent wisely, it can remove destitution, as it once did. Its neglect is one cause of the gap between rich and poor in Muslim societies today. The Quran's description of true believers still applies: "in their wealth there is a right for the one who asks and the deprived" (Adh-Dhariyat 51:19).

## MA-6. "The Prophet Muhammad (PBUH) was the greatest peacemaker." Discuss with reference to his dealings with the pagans, Jews and Christians. Was peace the real purpose of his military expeditions?

### 1. Introduction

The Prophet Muhammad (PBUH) was born into a society where tribal wars lasted for generations and revenge was a duty. Within twenty-three years he left Arabia united and at peace, and he did it with remarkably little bloodshed. The Quran sums up his mission: "We have not sent you except as a mercy to the worlds" (Al-Anbiya 21:107). The very name of his religion comes from the root of *salam*, peace, and he defined a Muslim as "one from whose tongue and hand people are safe" (Sahih al-Bukhari). He taught that making peace between people ranks above optional prayer, fasting and charity (Sunan Abi Dawud). This answer examines his peacemaking before his mission, among his own followers, and with pagans, Jews and Christians, and then shows that even his battles served peace.

### 2. Peacemaker before Prophethood

**(i) The Pact of the Virtuous (*Hilf al-Fudul*).** As a young man he joined a league of Makkan clans sworn to defend any wronged person. He later said he would answer such a call in Islam also.

**(ii) The Black Stone.** When the Ka'bah was rebuilt, the clans nearly came to blows over who would set the Black Stone. He had them lift it together on a cloth and placed it himself. A war was avoided by wisdom.

### 3. Peace within the Muslim community

**(i) Aws and Khazraj.** These two tribes of Madinah had fought for decades. He made them brothers: "You were enemies and He joined your hearts, and by His grace you became brothers" (Al Imran 3:103).

**(ii) Brotherhood of Emigrants and Helpers.** He paired each refugee from Makkah with a host in Madinah. The Helpers shared their homes and produce (Al-Hashr 59:9). Friction between locals and newcomers never arose.

**(iii) Personal mediation.** He went in person to reconcile a quarrelling clan (Sahih al-Bukhari).

**(iv) Ending tribal cries.** When a dispute revived the old slogans, he said: "Leave it, for it is foul" (Sahih al-Bukhari).

### 4. Peace with the pagans of Makkah

**(i) Thirteen years of non-violence.** In Makkah he and his followers were mocked, boycotted, tortured and killed. He ordered patience and forbade retaliation.

**(ii) Ta'if.** Stoned until he bled, he refused the offer to destroy the town: "I hope that Allah will bring from their descendants people who worship Allah alone" (Sahih al-Bukhari; Sahih Muslim).

**(iii) Migration instead of civil war.** He left his home so that conflict would not tear Makkah apart.

**(iv) The Treaty of Hudaybiyyah (6 AH).** With 1,400 followers ready to fight, he accepted terms that looked humiliating: to go back without the pilgrimage, and to return fugitives from Makkah while Makkah kept those who fled to it. He even agreed to remove the title "Messenger of Allah" from the document (Sahih Muslim). The Quran called this peace "a clear victory" (Al-Fath 48:1). In the two years of peace that followed, more people accepted Islam than in all the years before.

**(v) The Conquest of Makkah (8 AH).** He entered with ten thousand men and declared a general amnesty. "Whoever enters the house of Abu Sufyan is safe; whoever lays down his arms is safe; whoever shuts his door is safe" (Sahih Muslim). When a commander cried that it was a day of slaughter, he took the standard from him. Those who had persecuted him for twenty years were told they were free. He forgave Wahshi, who had killed his uncle Hamzah (RA), and Hind, who had mutilated the body.

**(vi) The Farewell Sermon.** He cancelled all blood-claims of the age of ignorance, beginning with a claim of his own family (Sahih Muslim), and so ended the cycle of revenge.

### 5. Peace with the Jews

**(i) The Charter of Madinah.** On arrival he drew up a written pact that made the Jewish tribes part of one political community with the Muslims: "The Jews have their religion and the Muslims have theirs." It gave them security, equal protection and a share in defence.

**(ii) Daily relations.** He traded with Jewish merchants; at his death his armour was pledged to one for barley (Sahih al-Bukhari). He visited a sick Jewish boy (Sahih al-Bukhari). When a funeral passed and he was told it was a Jew's, he stood: "Was it not a soul?" (Sahih al-Bukhari).

**(iii) Khaybar.** After its fall the people were left on their land on a share-cropping agreement.

Action against particular tribes followed their breach of the pact in time of war, as the Seerah sources record. It was not directed at their faith.

### 6. Peace with the Christians

**(i) Abyssinia.** He sent his persecuted followers to a Christian king "in whose land no one is wronged", and later prayed the funeral prayer for him (Sahih al-Bukhari).

**(ii) The delegation of Najran.** Sixty Christians visited Madinah. He lodged them in his mosque and, according to Ibn Ishaq, let them pray there. The treaty that followed guaranteed their lives, property, churches and clergy.

**(iii) Letters to Christian rulers.** His letter to Heraclius was courteous and quoted the Quran's invitation: "O People of the Book, come to a word common between us and you" (Al Imran 3:64).

**(iv) The principle.** "Allah does not forbid you from being kind and just to those who have not fought you over religion nor driven you from your homes" (Al-Mumtahanah 60:8).

### 7. Were his military expeditions for peace?

**(i) War was permitted only after long patience, and only for defence.** "Permission is given to those who are fought against, because they were wronged" (Al-Hajj 22:39). "Fight in the way of Allah those who fight you, but do not transgress" (Al-Baqarah 2:190).

**(ii) The major battles were defensive.** Badr, Uhud and the Trench were fought at or near Madinah against armies that had marched from Makkah.

**(iii) Peace was accepted whenever offered.** "If they incline to peace, incline to it also" (Al-Anfal 8:61). Hudaybiyyah proves it.

**(iv) He disliked war.** "Do not wish to meet the enemy; but when you meet them, be steadfast" (Sahih al-Bukhari).

**(v) Strict limits protected the innocent.** He forbade the killing of women and children (Sahih al-Bukhari) and forbade treachery and mutilation (Sahih Muslim).

**(vi) Casualties were very low.** Dr Hamidullah estimated that in ten years, during which most of Arabia came under Madinah, only a few hundred people died on both sides.

**(vii) No one was forced to believe.** "There is no compulsion in religion" (Al-Baqarah 2:256).

**(viii) The result was security.** A land where caravans were robbed and feuds never ended became safe from one end to the other, as he had foretold (Sahih al-Bukhari).

The expeditions removed aggression and persecution. They were a means; peace was the end.

### 8. Principles of his peacemaking

1. Justice as the basis of peace: "Do not let hatred of a people lead you to be unjust" (Al-Ma'idah 5:8).
2. Forgiveness when in power.
3. Faithful keeping of treaties (Al-Ma'idah 5:1).
4. Patience and long-term vision.
5. Respect for human life and dignity (Al-Ma'idah 5:32).
6. Repelling evil with good (Fussilat 41:34).

### 9. Relevance today

The world faces wars, refugee flows, religious hatred and terrorism. His model offers: written pacts among different communities (Madinah); negotiated settlement even on hard terms (Hudaybiyyah); amnesty and reconciliation after conflict (Makkah); protection of minorities (Najran); and rules of war that anticipate modern humanitarian law. For Pakistan, torn by sectarian and political hatred, his way of joining hearts is the remedy. Those who kill in his name contradict everything he stood for.

### 10. Conclusion

The Prophet (PBUH) made peace by character before he had power, and by forgiveness when he had it. He reconciled tribes, made pacts with Jews and Christians, preferred a hard treaty to an easy battle, and conquered his own city without revenge. Western historians such as Montgomery Watt and Karen Armstrong have acknowledged this record. He remains, in the Quran's words, "a mercy to the worlds" (Al-Anbiya 21:107).

## MA-7. Discuss the Prophet Muhammad (PBUH) as a military strategist and commander.

### 1. Introduction

The Prophet Muhammad (PBUH) had no military training and commanded no army until he was past fifty. Yet in ten years at Madinah he defended a small city-state against far stronger enemies and brought most of the Arabian peninsula under one authority, at a cost in lives that historians find astonishingly small. He led about twenty-seven expeditions in person, of which fighting occurred in about nine, and sent out many more detachments. The Quran presents him as the example in all things (Al-Ahzab 33:21), and that includes leadership in war. His generalship rested on clear aims, careful intelligence, consultation, discipline, surprise and, above all, moral restraint.

### 2. The framework: his concept of war

**(i) War only for a just cause.** "Permission is given to those who are fought against, because they were wronged" (Al-Hajj 22:39).

**(ii) Limits.** "Fight in the way of Allah those who fight you, but do not transgress" (Al-Baqarah 2:190).

**(iii) Readiness as deterrence.** "Prepare against them whatever force you can" (Al-Anfal 8:60).

**(iv) Peace when possible.** "If they incline to peace, incline to it also" (Al-Anfal 8:61).

**(v) Attitude.** "Do not wish to meet the enemy; but when you meet them, be steadfast" (Sahih al-Bukhari).

### 3. Elements of his strategy

**(i) Clear and limited objectives.** He sought to end aggression and secure freedom of belief. He did not seek the destruction of the enemy. This is why Makkah could be taken almost without a fight and its people won over.

**(ii) Intelligence.** Before Badr his scouts learned the enemy's strength from the number of camels slaughtered each day. At the Trench he sent Hudhayfah (RA) into the enemy camp at night. Allies in Makkah kept him informed.

**(iii) Secrecy and surprise.** When setting out he would let people think he was going elsewhere (Sahih al-Bukhari). The march on Makkah was hidden until the army was camped outside it. He said: "War is deception" (Sahih al-Bukhari), meaning stratagem against the enemy in the field, not treachery or breach of treaty.

**(iv) Consultation.** The Quran commanded him: "Consult them in the matter" (Al Imran 3:159). At Badr he moved the camp to the wells on a Companion's advice, as the Seerah records. At Uhud he accepted the majority's wish to fight outside the city. At the Trench he adopted the proposal of Salman al-Farisi (RA).

**(v) Choice of ground.** At Badr he held the water and the firm sand. At Uhud he put the mountain behind him and archers on the one pass through which cavalry could turn his flank. At the Trench he closed the only open side of Madinah.

**(vi) Innovation.** A defensive trench was unknown in Arabia. Fighting in ordered ranks, which the Quran praises (As-Saff 61:4), replaced the loose tribal charge.

**(vii) Economic pressure.** His early patrols threatened the Makkan trade route to Syria, which forced Quraysh to react.

**(viii) Alliances and division of the enemy.** Treaties with the tribes around Madinah isolated Quraysh. During the siege of Madinah a new convert, Nu'aym ibn Mas'ud (RA), was allowed to sow distrust between the besiegers and their allies inside. The Treaty of Hudaybiyyah separated Quraysh from Khaybar so that each could be dealt with in turn.

**(ix) Speed and mobility.** Rapid marches repeatedly caught opponents unprepared.

**(x) Morale.** He shared every hardship. At the Trench he dug with his own hands, hungry like his men (Sahih al-Bukhari). He spent the night before Badr in prayer.

**(xi) Discipline and chain of command.** For Mu'tah he named three commanders in order of succession (Sahih al-Bukhari).

**(xii) Psychological effect.** Outside Makkah he had every man light a fire, so that the city saw ten thousand flames. After the setback at Uhud he marched out the next day in pursuit to show that the Muslims were unbroken.

**(xiii) Merit in command.** Khalid ibn al-Walid (RA), a recent convert, was given high command for his ability. The young Usamah (RA) was placed over veterans.

**(xiv) Personal courage.** At Hunayn, when a sudden ambush scattered the army, he stood firm and called the men back (At-Tawbah 9:25–26).

### 4. The strategy seen in his battles

**Badr (2 AH).** 313 poorly armed men faced about a thousand. Intelligence, control of water, disciplined ranks and high morale gave victory. "Allah gave you victory at Badr when you were weak" (Al Imran 3:123).

**Uhud (3 AH).** The plan was sound, and the first phase was won. Most of the archers then left their post against orders, and the enemy cavalry came through the gap. The Quran draws the lesson of obedience (Al Imran 3:152). He rallied the survivors on the high ground and denied the enemy a decisive result.

**The Trench (5 AH).** A coalition of about ten thousand besieged three thousand. The trench neutralised their cavalry; patience and diplomacy broke the coalition; a storm ended the siege (Al-Ahzab 33:9). He then said that from now on the Muslims would take the initiative.

**Hudaybiyyah (6 AH).** A strategic victory without battle: the main enemy was neutralised by treaty.

**Khaybar (7 AH).** A fast march achieved surprise; the forts fell one by one; the settlement kept the land productive.

**Makkah (8 AH).** Secrecy, four converging columns, overwhelming numbers and a promise of safety produced a nearly bloodless conquest.

**Hunayn (8 AH).** Overconfidence in numbers led to a near-disaster in an ambush; steadiness at the centre turned it into victory.

**Tabuk (9 AH).** Thirty thousand men marched to the Byzantine frontier in great heat. No battle took place; the show of strength secured the north and brought border tribes into treaty.

### 5. Ethics of war

His conduct in war is what most sets him apart from other commanders.

1. **Non-combatants protected.** Seeing a woman killed in one campaign, he forbade the killing of women and children (Sahih al-Bukhari).
2. **No treachery, no mutilation, no killing of children** (Sahih Muslim).
3. **No needless destruction.** His first successor repeated his rules to the army: no cutting of fruit trees, no killing of animals except for food, no harm to the aged or to monks (Muwatta Malik).
4. **Humane treatment of prisoners.** The captives of Badr were fed before their captors (compare Al-Insan 76:8); the literate could earn freedom by teaching children; the Quran lays down "generosity or ransom" (Muhammad 47:4).
5. **Treaties kept** even at a cost.
6. **Mercy in victory.** The amnesty at Makkah turned enemies into supporters. After Hunayn he freed thousands of captives.

### 6. Results

In one decade: the survival of the Muslim community; the end of Makkan hostility; the unification of Arabia; secure borders; and an army whose commanders soon afterwards defeated the two great empires of the age. The human cost was low, and the defeated were reconciled, so the peace lasted.

### 7. Relevance today

1. **Deterrence and preparedness** remain the basis of defence policy (Al-Anfal 8:60).
2. **Intelligence, unity of command and consultation** are the principles taught in every staff college.
3. **His rules of conduct** anticipated the Geneva Conventions by thirteen centuries.
4. **The line between lawful war and terrorism** is clear from his practice: war is declared by lawful authority, fought openly against combatants, and bound by ethics.
5. **Winning the peace** matters more than winning the battle.

### 8. Conclusion

As a commander the Prophet (PBUH) joined foresight with courage and firmness with mercy. He prepared thoroughly, consulted widely, struck where the enemy was weak, and stopped as soon as the purpose was served. His aim was never conquest for its own sake; it was the removal of oppression so that people could live and believe in safety. He is the model not only of how to fight, but of when to fight and when to forgive.

## MA-8. Analyse the Charter of Madinah (Mithaq al-Madinah) as a social contract.

### 1. Introduction

When the Prophet Muhammad (PBUH) arrived in Madinah in 622 CE he found a city without a government. The Arab tribes of Aws and Khazraj were exhausted by a long civil war. Three Jewish tribes lived beside them, allied to one or the other. To these were now added the Emigrants from Makkah. Within months he drew up a written document that bound all these groups into one political community with defined rights and duties. It is preserved by Ibn Ishaq and by Abu Ubayd in *Kitab al-Amwal*. Dr Muhammad Hamidullah, who divided it into 52 clauses, called it "the first written constitution in the world". Because it created a political society by agreement, it may properly be studied as a social contract.

### 2. The idea of a social contract

In political theory a social contract is the agreement by which individuals leave a condition of insecurity, form a society, and accept a common authority in return for protection and rights. Hobbes, Locke and Rousseau developed the idea in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. Their contracts were theories about how states might have begun. The Charter of Madinah was an actual, written, negotiated agreement, concluded about a thousand years earlier.

### 3. Background

- No central authority; blood-feuds between Aws and Khazraj.
- A mixed population: Muslims, Jews, and Arabs who had not yet accepted Islam.
- Refugees without homes or income.
- An external threat from Quraysh of Makkah.

The city needed internal peace, a system of justice, and a common defence.

### 4. Main provisions

**(i) One community.** The believers of Quraysh and Yathrib, and those who follow and join them, "are one community (*ummah*) apart from other people".

**(ii) Inclusion of the Jews.** "The Jews of Banu Awf are a community along with the believers. The Jews have their religion and the Muslims have theirs." The same applied to the other Jewish clans named.

**(iii) Freedom of religion** for each group.

**(iv) Existing social arrangements kept.** Each clan remained responsible for its own blood-money and for ransoming its captives, "with fairness".

**(v) Mutual help.** The believers shall not leave anyone among them burdened with debt without help.

**(vi) Rule of law.** The believers shall be against anyone who rebels, or seeks to spread injustice or corruption, "even if he is the son of one of them". No one shall shelter a wrongdoer.

**(vii) Individual responsibility.** A person is not liable for the wrong of his ally.

**(viii) Equal protection.** "The protection of Allah is one"; protection granted by the least of them binds all.

**(ix) Common defence.** All parties shall help one another against any attack on Yathrib. Each bears its own costs.

**(x) No separate dealings with the enemy.** No protection is to be given to Quraysh or their helpers.

**(xi) War and peace are indivisible.** No group makes a separate peace.

**(xii) Madinah a sanctuary.** The valley of Yathrib is sacred for the people of this document.

**(xiii) Good neighbourliness.** The neighbour is as oneself, not to be harmed.

**(xiv) Final authority.** "Whatever you differ about is to be referred to Allah and to Muhammad."

### 5. The Charter as a social contract

**(i) It created a political society by consent.** The tribes agreed to its terms. The Prophet (PBUH) did not conquer Madinah; he was invited, and the document was accepted by its parties.

**(ii) It replaced blood-ties with citizenship.** For the first time in Arabia, membership of a community rested on agreement and residence, not on descent. This was a revolution in a tribal world.

**(iii) It established a central authority.** Disputes went to one final judge. The Prophet (PBUH) became head of state, chief justice and commander by the terms of the pact.

**(iv) It defined rights and duties on both sides.** The parties gained security of life, property and religion. In return they owed loyalty, defence of the city and obedience to its law. This exchange is the core of any social contract.

**(v) It limited private vengeance.** The right to punish passed from the clan to the community. This is exactly the step that Hobbes and Locke describe as the beginning of civil society.

**(vi) It was pluralist.** Muslims and Jews were equal parties with freedom of religion. Citizenship did not depend on creed.

**(vii) It balanced unity with autonomy.** The clans kept their internal affairs; defence, foreign relations and final justice were common. This resembles a federal arrangement.

**(viii) It was written.** Rights did not depend on memory or the goodwill of the strong.

**(ix) It set a moral basis.** The contract was made under Allah, which gave it a sanction beyond force.

### 6. Comparison with Western theories

| Aspect | Hobbes / Locke / Rousseau | Charter of Madinah |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | Theory | Historical fact |
| Date | 17th–18th century | 622 CE |
| Sovereign | An absolute ruler (Hobbes); the people (Locke, Rousseau) | Allah; the Prophet (PBUH) as final referee under revelation |
| Parties | Abstract individuals | Real tribes and religious communities |
| Religion | Largely left out | Freedom of religion guaranteed |
| Rights | Life, liberty, property | Life, property, religion, justice, mutual aid |
| Moral basis | Self-interest and reason | Faith, justice and covenant |

### 7. Results and significance

1. The feud between Aws and Khazraj ended.
2. Madinah became a state with a recognised head.
3. The refugees were absorbed.
4. A united front was formed against outside attack.
5. A model of coexistence between faiths was set.
6. The principle that government rests on a written covenant entered Muslim political thought.

Later, according to the Seerah sources, the Jewish tribes were dealt with one after another when they were held to have broken the pact in time of war. This shows that the covenant was treated as binding on all parties. It does not lessen the value of its principles.

### 8. Lessons for today

1. **Constitutionalism.** A plural society needs a written, agreed charter.
2. **Minority rights.** Non-Muslims were full parties with religious freedom. Pakistan's Constitution echoes this in Articles 20 and 36, and the Quaid-e-Azam expressed the same spirit on 11 August 1947.
3. **National unity above tribe, sect and ethnic group.**
4. **Rule of law.** No one shelters a wrongdoer, even a relative.
5. **Collective security and a single foreign policy.**
6. **Dialogue and agreement** as the way to build a state. The idea of the "state of Madinah" has meaning only if these principles are applied.

### 9. Conclusion

The Charter of Madinah turned a divided city into an organised community by free agreement, under a written text, with rights and duties for people of different faiths. It contains the essential elements of a social contract, and it joined them to a moral foundation. It stands as proof of the Prophet's (PBUH) statesmanship, and as a lasting guide for building a just and plural society.

## MA-9. Give a comparative and critical analysis of human rights in Islam and in the Universal Declaration of Human Rights. Refer to the Farewell Sermon.

### 1. Introduction

Human rights are the basic entitlements that belong to every person simply because he or she is human. The modern world dates their international recognition from the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR), adopted by the United Nations General Assembly on 10 December 1948. Islam proclaimed such rights in the seventh century. The Quran declares: "We have honoured the children of Adam" (Al-Isra 17:70). The Prophet (PBUH) summed them up before the largest gathering of his life: "Your blood, your property and your honour are sacred to one another, like the sanctity of this day, in this month, in this city" (Sahih al-Bukhari; Sahih Muslim). This answer outlines the Islamic concept, compares it with the UDHR, and evaluates both.

### 2. The Islamic concept of human rights

**(i) Source.** Rights are granted by Allah. No ruler, majority or parliament can withdraw them.

**(ii) Basis.** The dignity of man as Allah's honoured creature and vicegerent (Al-Isra 17:70; Al-Baqarah 2:30), and the common origin of all people (An-Nisa 4:1).

**(iii) Rights joined to duties.** Islamic law speaks of the "rights of people" (*huquq al-ibad*), which are other people's duties. Violating them is a sin that Allah does not forgive until the victim does.

**(iv) Sanction.** They are enforced by courts and also by conscience and the Hereafter. The Prophet (PBUH) described as "bankrupt" the one who comes on the Day of Judgment with prayer and fasting but has wronged others (Sahih Muslim).

**(v) Aim.** Imam al-Ghazali and al-Shatibi showed that the whole Shariah protects five essentials: religion, life, intellect, family and property.

### 3. The Farewell Sermon as a charter

Delivered at Arafat on 9 Dhul Hijjah, 10 AH (632 CE), it laid down:

1. **Sanctity of life, property and honour** (Sahih al-Bukhari).
2. **Abolition of blood revenge,** starting with a claim of his own clan (Sahih Muslim).
3. **Abolition of usury,** starting with that owed to his own uncle (Sahih Muslim).
4. **Rights of women:** "Fear Allah concerning women" (Sahih Muslim).
5. **Equality of mankind:** no superiority of Arab over non-Arab or white over black except by piety (reported in Musnad Ahmad). Compare Al-Hujurat 49:13.
6. **Individual responsibility:** no one is punished for another's offence (Jami at-Tirmidhi).
7. **Supremacy of law:** hold fast to the Book of Allah (Sahih Muslim).
8. **Duty to convey:** "Let the one present inform the one absent" (Sahih al-Bukhari).

It came six centuries before Magna Carta and thirteen before the UDHR, and its author began by applying it to his own family.

### 4. The UDHR in brief

Thirty articles, adopted after the horrors of the Second World War. It is a declaration, not a treaty, but it became the basis of the two International Covenants of 1966. It covers civil and political rights (life, liberty, fair trial, expression, religion, participation) and economic and social rights (work, education, an adequate standard of living).

### 5. Comparison: common ground

| Right | UDHR | Islam |
|---|---|---|
| Dignity and equality | Art. 1, 2 | Al-Isra 17:70; Al-Hujurat 49:13 |
| Life and security | Art. 3 | "Whoever kills a person ... it is as if he had killed all mankind" (Al-Ma'idah 5:32) |
| Freedom from slavery | Art. 4 | Allah is the opponent of one who enslaves a free person (Sahih al-Bukhari); freeing slaves is a high virtue (Al-Balad 90:13) |
| No torture | Art. 5 | Mutilation forbidden (Sahih Muslim) |
| Equality before law; fair trial | Art. 7, 10, 11 | "Judge with justice" (An-Nisa 4:58); the noble thief not spared (Sahih al-Bukhari); the burden of proof is on the claimant (Jami at-Tirmidhi) |
| Privacy | Art. 12 | "Do not spy" (Al-Hujurat 49:12); permission before entering homes (An-Nur 24:27) |
| Asylum | Art. 14 | At-Tawbah 9:6 |
| Property | Art. 17 | An-Nisa 4:29 |
| Freedom of religion | Art. 18 | "There is no compulsion in religion" (Al-Baqarah 2:256) |
| Expression | Art. 19 | "The best jihad is a word of truth before a tyrant ruler" (Sunan Abi Dawud) |
| Participation in government | Art. 21 | Consultation (Ash-Shura 42:38) |
| Social security | Art. 22, 25 | Zakat (At-Tawbah 9:60); the right of the needy (Adh-Dhariyat 51:19) |
| Work and fair wages | Art. 23 | "Give the worker his wage before his sweat dries" (Sunan Ibn Majah) |
| Education | Art. 26 | "Read" (Al-Alaq 96:1); seeking knowledge is a duty (Sunan Ibn Majah) |

On most matters the two agree. Islam can claim priority in time.

### 6. Comparison: differences

| Aspect | UDHR | Islam |
|---|---|---|
| Source | Agreement among states | Divine revelation |
| Legal force | Declaratory; depends on states | Religious and legal duty |
| Rights and duties | Rights stressed; duties in one article (29) | Rights and duties inseparable |
| Philosophy | Secular, centred on the individual | Centred on God; individual and community balanced |
| Range | Mainly the individual against the state | Also rights of parents, neighbours, orphans, travellers, enemies in war, animals |
| Limits | "Morality, public order and general welfare" as each state defines | Limits set by the Shariah |
| Sanction | Law and opinion | Law, conscience and the Hereafter |

### 7. Areas of debate

1. **Freedom to change religion (Art. 18) and marriage without religious limits (Art. 16).** Classical Islamic law has its own rules here. Contemporary Muslim scholars differ on how those rules apply in modern conditions.
2. **Equality of the sexes in family law.** Islam gives men and women equal worth and reward (Al Imran 3:195; Al-Ahzab 33:35) with some different rules, such as inheritance shares, tied to different financial duties.
3. **Limits of expression** concerning what is sacred.
4. **Some fixed punishments** and the modern definition of cruel punishment.

Because of these points, Muslim bodies issued the Universal Islamic Declaration of Human Rights (1981) and the Cairo Declaration on Human Rights in Islam (1990), which restate rights within the framework of the Shariah.

### 8. Critical evaluation

**Of the UDHR**

- *Strengths:* a common standard for all nations; a tool for the weak against the state; detailed on civil liberties.
- *Weaknesses:* drafted when most Muslim and African countries were under colonial rule; no binding force of its own; applied selectively by powerful states. The people of Palestine and Kashmir have been denied life, liberty and self-determination for decades while the Declaration's sponsors look on. Its moral basis is thin: it does not say why human beings have dignity.

**Of the Islamic system**

- *Strengths:* a transcendent basis for dignity; rights that cannot be suspended by any government; an inner sanction; attention to economic justice and to duties.
- *Weakness lies in practice, not principle.* Many Muslim states fall short on fair trial, freedom from arbitrary detention, minority protection, women's inheritance and workers' rights. Custom is often passed off as religion.

**Assessment.** The two are not enemies. The shared core is large. Muslims can support universal standards while holding that their own source is revelation, and should recognise that the first task is to implement at home the rights Islam already guarantees.

### 9. Relevance and recommendations

1. Teach human rights from the Quran and the Seerah, so that people see them as part of faith.
2. Reform the justice system to make rights enforceable. Pakistan's Constitution lists fundamental rights in Articles 8–28.
3. Protect minorities and women in practice.
4. Use the OIC and the UN to press for equal application of human rights, including to Muslims under occupation and to victims of Islamophobia.
5. Dialogue between Islamic and Western scholars on the points of difference.

### 10. Conclusion

Islam gave mankind a charter of rights at Arafat in 632 CE: life, property, honour, equality, justice and the protection of the weak, grounded in the dignity that Allah gave to every child of Adam. The UDHR, thirteen centuries later, repeats most of these in secular language. The Islamic concept is deeper in foundation and wider in scope; the modern system is stronger in machinery. The challenge for Muslims is to match their practice to their principles, so that the world can see what the Quran means by "We have honoured the children of Adam" (Al-Isra 17:70).

## MA-10. Discuss the status and rights of women in Islam in the context of the modern feminist movement.

### 1. Introduction

When Islam came, a woman in Arabia could be inherited like property, and in some tribes a baby girl could be buried alive. Elsewhere in the world of that time, women were legal minors under father or husband. Islam declared her an equal human being before Allah, gave her a legal personality, and fixed her rights in the text of revelation: "I do not let the work of any worker among you be lost, male or female. You are of one another" (Al Imran 3:195). The modern feminist movement arose in the West in the nineteenth century to win rights that Western law had denied to women. The two share much ground and differ in foundations. This answer sets out the status Islam gives to women, then compares it with feminism.

### 2. Women before Islam

- **Arabia:** female infanticide (At-Takwir 81:8–9); grief at a daughter's birth (An-Nahl 16:58–59); widows inherited by the husband's kin (An-Nisa 4:19); no fixed share in inheritance; unlimited polygamy and divorce.
- **Other societies:** in Roman law she was under perpetual guardianship; in England a married woman's property passed to her husband until the Married Women's Property Acts of 1870 and 1882.

### 3. Spiritual and human equality

1. **Same origin:** "He created you from a single soul, and from it created its mate" (An-Nisa 4:1).
2. **Same reward:** "Whoever does good, male or female, and is a believer, We shall give a good life" (An-Nahl 16:97).
3. **Same duties and virtues:** the Quran lists believing men and believing women side by side in ten qualities (Al-Ahzab 33:35).
4. **Partners in public duty:** "Believing men and believing women are allies of one another; they enjoin right and forbid wrong" (At-Tawbah 9:71).
5. **No doctrine of woman as the origin of sin:** the Quran holds Adam and his wife both responsible, and both were forgiven (Al-A'raf 7:20–23).
6. The Prophet (PBUH) said: "Women are the counterparts of men" (Sunan Abi Dawud).

### 4. Rights in each role

**As a daughter.** Her life is sacred. To raise daughters well is a path to Paradise: "Whoever brings up two girls until they reach maturity, he and I will come on the Day of Resurrection like this," and he joined his fingers (Sahih Muslim). Parents must be just among their children (Sahih al-Bukhari). She cannot be married without her consent: "A virgin is not to be married until her permission is sought" (Sahih al-Bukhari). When a woman complained that her father had married her against her will, the Prophet (PBUH) cancelled the marriage (Sahih al-Bukhari).

**As a wife.** Marriage is a bond of "love and mercy" (Ar-Rum 30:21). The dower is hers alone (An-Nisa 4:4). Her maintenance is her husband's duty, whatever her own wealth. "Live with them in kindness" (An-Nisa 4:19). "The best of you are the best to their wives" (Jami at-Tirmidhi). "Women have rights similar to those held over them" (Al-Baqarah 2:228). She may seek dissolution of an unhappy marriage, as the wife of Thabit ibn Qays did (Sahih al-Bukhari).

**As a mother.** "Who is most deserving of my good company?" "Your mother," said three times, "then your father" (Sahih al-Bukhari; Sahih Muslim). The Quran recalls her hardship in bearing and nursing (Luqman 31:14).

### 5. Economic rights

1. **Ownership and earning:** "For men is a share of what they earn, and for women a share of what they earn" (An-Nisa 4:32). She manages her property without anyone's permission. Khadijah (RA) was a leading merchant.
2. **Inheritance:** "For women is a share of what parents and near relatives leave ... a fixed share" (An-Nisa 4:7).
3. **Dower and maintenance.**
4. **No duty to spend on the household.**

*Why is a daughter's share half a son's?* The ratio applies in some cases only; in others the shares are equal, as with the mother and father of a deceased who leaves children. The son must pay dower, maintain his family and support needy female relatives. The daughter keeps her share for herself and is maintained by others. The system must be judged as a whole.

### 6. Educational, social and political rights

1. **Education.** Seeking knowledge is a duty on every Muslim (Sunan Ibn Majah). Women asked for a day of teaching and were given it (Sahih al-Bukhari). Aisha (RA) became one of the greatest authorities on the Sunnah.
2. **Worship.** "Do not prevent the women servants of Allah from the mosques of Allah" (Sahih al-Bukhari).
3. **Political voice.** Women gave the pledge of allegiance (Al-Mumtahanah 60:12). A woman's complaint brought down revelation (Al-Mujadilah 58:1). Umm Salamah's (RA) advice resolved the crisis at Hudaybiyyah (Sahih al-Bukhari).
4. **Work and service.** Women nursed the wounded in battle (Sahih al-Bukhari).
5. **Protection of honour.** False accusation against a chaste woman is a crime with a fixed penalty (An-Nur 24:4).

### 7. Points often questioned

- **Guardianship of men (An-Nisa 4:34).** The word *qawwam* means maintainer and protector. It is a duty of support within the family, linked in the verse to spending. It is not a licence for domination, and family matters are to be settled by consultation (Al-Baqarah 2:233).
- **Polygamy (An-Nisa 4:3).** Islam restricted an unlimited custom to four and tied it to justice: "if you fear you will not be just, then only one."
- **Modesty.** Men are told first to lower their gaze, then women (An-Nur 24:30–31). The aim is dignity and protection.

On the details of these matters jurists have differed, and I have given the mainstream view.

### 8. Islam and feminism

**The movement.** The first wave (to the early twentieth century) sought legal identity, property and the vote. The second wave (1960s onward) sought equality at work and in family law. Later waves stress identity, autonomy and action against harassment. In Pakistan the debate has been sharpened by the annual women's marches.

**Common ground**

- Education, property and inheritance.
- Consent in marriage; the right to leave a harmful marriage.
- Protection from violence and harassment.
- Equal pay for equal work.
- Participation in public life.

Islam granted most of these in law more than twelve centuries before Western states did.

**Differences**

| Secular feminism (main strands) | Islam |
|---|---|
| The autonomous individual is the starting point | Both sexes are servants of Allah |
| Often aims at identical roles | Equal worth; complementary duties in the family; freedom to learn, earn and serve |
| Tends to see the family as a place of oppression | Treats the family as the foundation of society, to be made just |
| Rights language | Rights matched with duties |
| Sexual freedom as liberation | Chastity required of men and women alike |

**Assessment.** Feminism arose from real injustice and has won real gains. Its more extreme forms set women against men and weaken the family, with costs visible in Western societies. Islam aims at justice between the sexes, not rivalry. Some Muslim women scholars today argue for women's rights from the Quran and Sunnah themselves, separating the religion from patriarchal custom.

### 9. The real problem and its remedy

In Pakistan and other Muslim societies women are denied many rights that Islam gave them: inheritance is taken by brothers; girls are kept from school; marriages are forced or arranged by exchange; "honour" killings continue; dowry is demanded. These are customs, and Islam condemns them. The remedy is to enforce existing law (the Muslim Family Laws Ordinance 1961; the law against depriving women of inheritance, 2011; the Enforcement of Women's Property Rights Act 2020), to educate girls, to use the pulpit and media, and to make courts accessible to women.

### 10. Conclusion

Islam raised woman from an object of inheritance to an heir, from a burden to a path to Paradise, and gave her spiritual equality, property, consent and dignity by divine law. Where Muslim women suffer today, it is because that law is ignored. The answer to feminism is neither to reject its just demands nor to adopt its philosophy whole, but to implement the Prophet's (PBUH) instruction in his last sermon: "Fear Allah concerning women" (Sahih Muslim).

## MA-11. Explain the concept of self-purification (tazkiyah) and ihsan in the light of the Quran and Sunnah. Why is self-purification essential for the development of society?

### 1. Introduction

Every reform of society that Islam proposes begins inside the human being. The Arabic word for this inner reform is *tazkiyah*: to cleanse the soul of false beliefs and evil traits and to make good qualities grow in it. The Quran names it among the purposes for which the Prophet (PBUH) was sent: "He sent among the unlettered a Messenger from themselves, reciting His verses to them, purifying them, and teaching them the Book and wisdom" (Al-Jumu'ah 62:2). It also makes it the measure of success: "He has succeeded who purifies it, and he has failed who corrupts it" (Ash-Shams 91:9–10). Its highest fruit is *ihsan*, worship and conduct of the best quality. Self-purification is listed in the syllabus among the characteristics of Islamic civilization because a just society cannot be built from corrupt individuals.

### 2. Meaning

- **Literal:** *tazkiyah* means purification and growth, like weeding a field so that the crop can flourish.
- **Technical:** freeing the heart from polytheism, hypocrisy, pride, envy, greed and the like, and adorning it with faith, sincerity, patience, gratitude, humility and love of Allah, so that outward conduct follows His guidance.

### 3. Self-purification in the Quran

**(i) A task of the prophets.** Ibrahim (AS) prayed for a messenger who would purify his people (Al-Baqarah 2:129), and Allah answered with Muhammad (PBUH) (Al Imran 3:164).

**(ii) The condition of success.** "He has succeeded who purifies himself, remembers the name of his Lord and prays" (Al-A'la 87:14–15).

**(iii) The soul has two possibilities.** Allah "inspired it with its wickedness and its righteousness" (Ash-Shams 91:8). Man must choose.

**(iv) Three states of the self.** The self that urges evil (Yusuf 12:53); the self that reproaches itself (Al-Qiyamah 75:2); and the self at peace (Al-Fajr 89:27–30). Purification is the journey from the first to the last.

**(v) Only a sound heart counts in the end.** "The Day when neither wealth nor children will benefit, only one who comes to Allah with a sound heart" (Ash-Shu'ara 26:88–89).

**(vi) Paradise for the one who restrains himself** (An-Nazi'at 79:40–41).

### 4. Self-purification in the Sunnah

1. "In the body is a piece of flesh: if it is sound, the whole body is sound; if it is corrupt, the whole body is corrupt. It is the heart." (Sahih al-Bukhari; Sahih Muslim)
2. "Allah does not look at your bodies or your wealth; He looks at your hearts and your deeds." (Sahih Muslim)
3. "The one who strives is he who strives against his own self." (Jami at-Tirmidhi)
4. "The strong man is not the one who throws others down; the strong man is the one who controls himself when angry." (Sahih al-Bukhari; Sahih Muslim)
5. "Fear Allah wherever you are, follow a bad deed with a good one to wipe it out, and treat people with good character." (Jami at-Tirmidhi)

### 5. Ihsan

In the Hadith of Jibril the Prophet (PBUH) explained the three levels of the religion: Islam, Iman and Ihsan. Of the last he said: "It is to worship Allah as though you see Him; and if you do not see Him, He sees you" (Sahih Muslim). Ihsan is therefore constant awareness of Allah, and from it comes excellence in every act. "Allah has prescribed excellence in everything" (Sahih Muslim). "Allah commands justice and ihsan" (An-Nahl 16:90): justice is to give what is due; ihsan is to give more than is due. "Allah is with those who fear Him and those who do good" (An-Nahl 16:128).

Tazkiyah is the process; ihsan is the result.

### 6. Means of self-purification

1. **Sound belief and sincere intention.** "Actions are judged by intentions" (Sahih al-Bukhari).
2. **The prescribed worship.** Prayer "restrains from indecency and wrongdoing" (Al-Ankabut 29:45). Zakat cleanses: "Take from their wealth a charity by which you cleanse and purify them" (At-Tawbah 9:103). Fasting builds restraint (Al-Baqarah 2:183). Hajj trains patience and humility (Al-Baqarah 2:197).
3. **Remembrance of Allah and the Quran.** "In the remembrance of Allah hearts find rest" (Ar-Ra'd 13:28).
4. **Repentance.** "Do not despair of the mercy of Allah" (Az-Zumar 39:53).
5. **Self-accounting.** "Let every soul look to what it has sent ahead for tomorrow" (Al-Hashr 59:18).
6. **Good company.** "Be with the truthful" (At-Tawbah 9:119).
7. **Voluntary worship.** Allah says: "My servant keeps drawing near to Me with voluntary deeds until I love him" (Sahih al-Bukhari).
8. **Lawful earning and service to others.**
9. **Remembering death and the Hereafter.**

Imam al-Ghazali built his great work *Ihya Ulum al-Din* on this plan: half of it treats the vices that destroy and the virtues that save. Shah Waliullah counted ihsan as a branch of religious knowledge beside creed and law. In Muslim history the discipline of *tasawwuf* grew around this aim; at its best it produced people of great character who spread Islam across South Asia by their conduct.

### 7. Effects on the individual

- Peace of mind and freedom from the slavery of desire.
- Sincerity: the same conduct in public and in private.
- Self-control in anger, greed and lust.
- Humility and gratitude.
- Courage and steadiness in hardship.
- A living conscience.

### 8. Why self-purification is essential for society

**(i) Society is made of individuals.** "Allah does not change the condition of a people until they change what is in themselves" (Ar-Ra'd 13:11). Laws and institutions are worked by people; if the people are corrupt, the best system fails.

**(ii) Honesty in dealings.** A purified conscience does not need an inspector. Markets, offices and courts depend on it.

**(iii) Control of crime and corruption.** Law punishes after the act and only when it is discovered. Fear of Allah prevents the act. The "bankrupt" in the hadith is the worshipper who wronged others (Sahih Muslim).

**(iv) Trustworthy leadership.** The Rightly Guided Caliphs held vast power and lived more simply than their subjects. Umar (RA) patrolled the streets at night to learn the needs of the people. Their accountability came from within.

**(v) Social harmony.** Most quarrels come from pride, envy, suspicion and loose tongues, which the Quran forbids (Al-Hujurat 49:11–12). Remove these from hearts and peace follows.

**(vi) Compassion and welfare.** The Helpers of Madinah "preferred others over themselves, even when they themselves were in need" (Al-Hashr 59:9).

**(vii) Strong families.** Patience, kindness and fidelity begin in the heart.

**(viii) Sound use of wealth and knowledge.** Material progress without moral restraint produces exploitation and destruction.

**(ix) Protection from extremism.** The extremist is certain of his own purity and others' guilt. Tazkiyah teaches the opposite: to suspect oneself and think well of others.

**(x) Divine help.** "Had the people of the towns believed and been mindful of Allah, We would have opened for them blessings from heaven and earth" (Al-A'raf 7:96).

The first Muslim society proves the point. The same Arabs who had buried daughters and fought over camels became, after the Prophet's (PBUH) training, the teachers and just rulers of half the known world.

### 9. Contemporary relevance

Pakistan's gravest problems are moral before they are legal or economic: corruption, tax evasion, adulteration of food and medicine, false testimony, harassment, intolerance. New laws are passed every year with little effect, because the inner check is missing. Meanwhile mosques are full, which shows that ritual has been separated from character.

**Recommendations**

1. Character education built on the Seerah at every level of schooling.
2. Friday sermons on honesty, rights of people and self-control.
3. Leaders and officials to set the example of simple living.
4. Ethics as a real part of civil-service training.
5. Media to promote moral models in place of display and consumption.
6. Families to restore the home as the first school.

### 10. Conclusion

Self-purification is the root; a just and prosperous society is the fruit. The Quran ties success to it, the Prophet (PBUH) made it his mission, and Muslim history rose and fell with it. Reform that begins with systems and ignores souls does not last. The lasting formula is the Quran's: "He has succeeded who purifies it" (Ash-Shams 91:9).

## MA-12. What are the causes of extremism and terrorism in Pakistan? How can they be eliminated in the light of Islamic teachings?

### 1. Introduction

Extremism means going beyond the balanced limit in belief or action and refusing others the right to differ. When it turns to violence against civilians to spread fear, it becomes terrorism. Pakistan has suffered from both for more than two decades: tens of thousands of citizens and soldiers have been killed, including the schoolchildren of Peshawar in December 2014, and the economic loss has been immense. Some of the perpetrators claim to act in the name of Islam. Yet the Quran describes Muslims as "a middle community" (Al-Baqarah 2:143), forbids "excess in religion" (An-Nisa 4:171), and declares that "whoever kills a person ... it is as if he had killed all mankind" (Al-Ma'idah 5:32). Extremism is therefore a challenge to Islam, not a product of it. This answer states Islam's position, analyses the causes in Pakistan, and proposes remedies drawn from Islamic teachings.

### 2. Islam's position on extremism and violence

**(i) Moderation is commanded.** "Allah intends ease for you and does not intend hardship for you" (Al-Baqarah 2:185). The Prophet (PBUH) said: "The religion is easy; no one makes it hard on himself but it overcomes him" (Sahih al-Bukhari).

**(ii) Extremism is condemned.** "Beware of going to extremes in religion, for those before you were destroyed by going to extremes in religion" (Sunan an-Nasa'i; Sunan Ibn Majah). "Ruined are those who go to extremes," he said three times (Sahih Muslim).

**(iii) Life is sacred.** "Whoever kills a believer deliberately, his recompense is Hell" (An-Nisa 4:93). "The passing away of the world is lighter before Allah than the killing of a Muslim" (Sunan an-Nasa'i). "Whoever kills a person who has a covenant with the Muslims will not smell the fragrance of Paradise" (Sahih al-Bukhari).

**(iv) Suicide is forbidden.** "Do not kill yourselves" (An-Nisa 4:29); the one who kills himself will be punished with the same instrument (Sahih al-Bukhari).

**(v) Declaring Muslims unbelievers is forbidden.** "If a man says to his brother 'O unbeliever', it returns upon one of them" (Sahih al-Bukhari).

**(vi) Armed disorder is a grave crime.** The Quran prescribes severe penalties for those who "strive to spread corruption in the land" (Al-Ma'idah 5:33).

**(vii) The first extremists were disowned.** Of people like the Khawarij, who killed Muslims in the name of religion, the Prophet (PBUH) said that they would recite the Quran without it passing their throats (Sahih al-Bukhari; Sahih Muslim).

**(viii) Jihad is not terrorism.** Lawful fighting is defensive (Al-Baqarah 2:190), declared by the state, directed at combatants only, and bound by rules: no killing of women and children (Sahih al-Bukhari), no treachery or mutilation (Sahih Muslim).

### 3. Causes of extremism and terrorism in Pakistan

#### A. Religious and ideological

1. **Misreading of the texts.** Verses on fighting are quoted without their context and conditions.
2. **Takfir and sectarian hatred.** Preachers and pamphlets declare other Muslims unbelievers.
3. **Unqualified religious leadership.** People without learning issue rulings.
4. **Neglect of moral training.** Religion is taught as identity and ritual, not as character.

#### B. Historical and geopolitical

5. **The Afghan war of 1979–89.** Fighters were recruited, armed and praised by many states. The networks and weapons remained.
6. **The war after 2001.** Pakistan became a front-line state. Military operations, drone strikes and displacement produced anger and recruits.
7. **Foreign interference and proxy rivalries,** including support for militancy by hostile agencies.
8. **Injustice abroad.** Palestine and Kashmir are used by recruiters to stir feelings of humiliation.

#### C. Political

9. **Use of religious groups for political ends** by successive governments.
10. **Political instability and polarisation.** Opponents are treated as enemies; the language of hatred is normal.
11. **Denial of rights and development** in some regions, which feeds ethnic militancy.

#### D. Economic

12. **Poverty and unemployment** among a very large young population.
13. **Inequality** and the sense that the system serves only the powerful.

#### E. Educational and social

14. **Low literacy and three separate education systems,** which produce three mindsets.
15. **Curricula and teaching methods** that reward memorising and discourage questions.
16. **Intolerance in daily life;** mob violence on mere accusation.

#### F. Governance and justice

17. **Slow courts and low conviction rates.**
18. **Weak policing and ungoverned areas.**
19. **Corruption,** which destroys trust in the state.

#### G. Media and technology

20. **Online radicalisation** and unchecked hate speech.

### 4. Remedies in the light of Islamic teachings

#### A. Correcting ideas

1. **Teach the true concept of jihad** and the sanctity of life from the Quran and Sunnah.
2. **A united scholarly voice.** The declaration *Paigham-e-Pakistan* (2018), signed by more than 1,800 scholars of all schools, ruled that suicide attacks and armed action against the state are forbidden and that only the state may declare war. It should be taught in every seminary and school.
3. **End takfir.** Teach the ethics of disagreement practised by the Companions, who differed on details and remained brothers.
4. **Reform the pulpit.** Train and certify imams; enforce the ban on hate speech.

#### B. The Prophet's (PBUH) method

5. **Gentleness.** "By mercy from Allah you were gentle with them. Had you been harsh and hard-hearted they would have scattered from around you" (Al Imran 3:159).
6. **Wise preaching.** "Call to the way of your Lord with wisdom and good counsel" (An-Nahl 16:125).
7. **Forgiveness and reconciliation,** as at Ta'if and Makkah; the door of return kept open for those who lay down arms.
8. **A pluralist charter,** as in Madinah, giving every group security and rights.
9. **Firmness with those who persist in bloodshed,** as the law requires (Al-Ma'idah 5:33).
10. **Brotherhood in place of faction.** "The believers are but brothers, so make peace between your brothers" (Al-Hujurat 49:10).

#### C. Justice and governance

11. **Rule of law for all.** "When you judge between people, judge with justice" (An-Nisa 4:58). Speedy trials and reliable prosecution.
12. **Consultation and inclusion.** Political disputes to be settled in parliament and by dialogue (Ash-Shura 42:38).
13. **The state's monopoly of force.** No private armies of any colour.
14. **Full implementation of the National Action Plan** and coordination through the counter-terrorism authority.

#### D. Social and economic measures

15. **Economic justice.** Zakat, jobs and skills for the young. The Quran joins freedom from hunger with freedom from fear (Quraysh 106:3–4).
16. **Development of neglected areas** and a fair share of resources.

#### E. Education and media

17. **One curriculum of quality** with tolerance, civic sense and critical thinking; registration and modern subjects for seminaries.
18. **Verification of news.** "If a wrongdoer brings you news, verify it" (Al-Hujurat 49:6). Regulation of online hate.

#### F. For political extremism

19. Truthful and decent speech (Al-Ahzab 33:70); no mockery or insulting names (Al-Hujurat 49:11); justice even to opponents (Al-Ma'idah 5:8).

#### G. International

20. Active diplomacy for a just settlement of Palestine and Kashmir; cooperation among Muslim states; a joint stand against Islamophobia, which feeds on extremism and feeds it in turn.

### 5. Conclusion

Extremism in Pakistan grew from a mixture of distorted religious teaching, war on its borders, injustice, poverty and weak government. Force alone has contained it but has not uprooted it. The lasting cure lies in the religion that extremists misuse: its moderation, its respect for life, its ethics of disagreement, and its demand for justice. If the state provides justice and opportunity, and the scholars speak with one voice, the Quran's description can again fit this society: "Thus We have made you a middle community" (Al-Baqarah 2:143).

## MA-13. Explain the concept of accountability in Islam. Show with examples that the Rightly Guided Caliphs set the highest standard of accountability. How can Pakistan's system be reformed on these lines?

### 1. Introduction

In Islam every person who holds any charge, from the head of a household to the head of the state, is answerable for it. The Prophet (PBUH) said: "Each of you is a shepherd and each of you is responsible for his flock" (Sahih al-Bukhari; Sahih Muslim). Power is a trust (*amanah*) from Allah, and a trust is always followed by a reckoning. The Arabic terms are *muhasabah* (calling to account) and *hisbah*, the institution that enjoins what is right and forbids what is wrong in public life. The first four Caliphs applied this idea to themselves and their officers so strictly that their record is still the standard by which Muslim governments are judged.

### 2. Basis in the Quran and Sunnah

**Quran**

- "We shall question those to whom messengers were sent, and We shall question the messengers" (Al-A'raf 7:6). Even prophets are questioned.
- "By your Lord, We shall question them all about what they used to do" (Al-Hijr 15:92–93).
- "Allah commands you to hand over trusts to those entitled to them" (An-Nisa 4:58).
- "Whoever does an atom's weight of evil will see it" (Az-Zalzalah 99:8).
- "Let there be among you a group that calls to good, enjoins what is right and forbids what is wrong" (Al Imran 3:104).

**Sunnah**

- "No servant is given charge of subjects by Allah and dies while cheating them, but Allah forbids him Paradise" (Sahih al-Bukhari; Sahih Muslim).
- To Abu Dharr (RA), on public office: "It is a trust, and on the Day of Resurrection it will be disgrace and regret, except for one who takes it by right and fulfils its duty" (Sahih Muslim).
- "Whoever of you sees a wrong, let him change it with his hand; if he cannot, then with his tongue" (Sahih Muslim).
- "The best jihad is a word of truth before a tyrannical ruler" (Sunan Abi Dawud).

### 3. Levels of accountability

1. **Before Allah.** The final court, from which nothing is hidden.
2. **Before one's own conscience.** "Let every soul look to what it has sent ahead for tomorrow" (Al-Hashr 59:18).
3. **Before the people.** Citizens have the right and the duty to question rulers.
4. **Before the law.** The ruler has no immunity in court.
5. **Before institutions.** The market inspector (*muhtasib*), the court of grievances (*mazalim*), the judge and the auditor.

The first two are internal, the rest external. Islam's strength is that it joins them.

### 4. Principles

1. **No one is above the law.** A noblewoman was caught stealing and people asked for her to be spared. The Prophet (PBUH) said: "Those before you were destroyed because when a noble among them stole they let him go, and when a weak one stole they punished him. By Allah, if Fatimah the daughter of Muhammad stole, I would cut off her hand" (Sahih al-Bukhari).
2. **Accountability begins at the top.** In his last sermon he cancelled his own family's claims first (Sahih Muslim).
3. **Gifts to officials are suspect.** A collector returned and said: "This is for you, and this was given to me as a gift." The Prophet (PBUH) said from the pulpit: "Why did he not sit in the house of his father and mother and see whether gifts would come to him?" (Sahih al-Bukhari; Sahih Muslim).
4. **Public money is sacred.** One who hides even a needle from it will bring it on the Day of Resurrection (Sahih Muslim).
5. **Due process.** The burden of proof is on the accuser (Jami at-Tirmidhi); both sides must be heard (Sunan Abi Dawud); spying on private lives is forbidden (Al-Hujurat 49:12).
6. **No revenge.** "Do not let hatred of a people lead you to be unjust" (Al-Ma'idah 5:8).

The Prophet (PBUH) himself inspected the market. Finding damp grain hidden under dry, he said: "Whoever cheats is not one of us" (Sahih Muslim).

### 5. The Rightly Guided Caliphs: the highest standard

#### Abu Bakr (RA)

- **Invited public correction.** His first address: "I have been given authority over you and I am not the best of you. If I do right, help me; if I do wrong, set me right ... Obey me as long as I obey Allah and His Messenger; if I disobey them, you owe me no obedience" (reported by Ibn Hisham).
- **Kept his hands off the treasury.** He took a bare allowance and, the histories report, directed at his death that it be repaid from his own property.

#### Umar (RA)

- **Accountability of self.** He is reported to have said that if an animal came to harm on a far road, he feared Allah would question him about it. On his deathbed he hoped only to come out even, with nothing for him and nothing against him (Sahih Muslim), and he arranged for his debts to be paid from his family's property (Sahih al-Bukhari).
- **Open to challenge.** Well-known reports tell of an ordinary man questioning him in the mosque about the cloth of his garment, and of a woman correcting him on the limit of dowers. He accepted both. He held that there is no good in people who do not speak out and no good in rulers who do not listen.
- **Accountability of officials.** On appointment a governor's property was listed; an unexplained increase was taken for the treasury. Governors were bound to live simply and keep their doors open. Complaints were heard publicly at the Hajj. A special officer investigated charges on the spot. Even the great general Khalid ibn al-Walid (RA) was removed from command, and the governor of Kufa, Sa'd ibn Abi Waqqas (RA), was investigated on a complaint.
- **Equality.** When an Egyptian Copt complained that the governor's son had struck him, Umar is reported to have had the Copt take his due and to have said: "Since when have you enslaved people whose mothers bore them free?"
- **Family.** He allowed his own household no privilege.
- **Independent judges.** He separated the judiciary from the governors and himself appeared before a judge as a party.

#### Uthman (RA)

- He received delegations of complaint and, the histories record, wrote to the provinces inviting anyone wronged by an official to come forward at the Hajj.
- Besieged in his house, he refused to let the army shed the blood of citizens to protect him.

#### Ali (RA)

- **Before his own court.** The famous report tells that he took a dispute over a coat of armour to his own judge and accepted the decision against him for lack of proof.
- **Written rules for officials.** In his letter to Malik al-Ashtar, governor of Egypt, he ordered that officials be appointed after trial and not by favour, paid well, watched by honest observers, and punished for proven breach of trust. He told the governor to set a time to hear complaints in person without guards.
- **Rebuke for small lapses.** He censured a governor merely for attending a rich man's banquet (Nahj al-Balaghah, Letter 45).
- **Strict equality in the treasury.**

**Why this is the highest standard.** These men ruled a state stretching from Libya to Persia. They could have placed themselves above question. They did the reverse: they asked to be corrected, lived as the poorest lived, applied the law first to their own families, and dreaded the questioning of Allah more than any human court.

### 6. Institutions that grew from this practice

- **Hisbah:** supervision of markets, weights, prices and public conduct.
- **Mazalim:** a high court for complaints against the powerful, the forerunner of the ombudsman.
- **Independent judiciary.**
- **Audit of public accounts.**

### 7. Accountability in Pakistan and its reform

**Existing bodies:** the National Accountability Bureau, the Federal Investigation Agency, provincial anti-corruption departments, the Auditor General, the Public Accounts Committees, the Federal Ombudsman (*Wafaqi Mohtasib*), and the courts.

**Weaknesses:** accountability is widely seen as selective and timed to politics; trials are slow and convictions few; some classes are in practice exempt; bodies overlap; honest officers fear to take decisions.

**Reforms on the Islamic model**

1. **Across-the-board application,** with no exempt class.
2. **Start with the powerful,** including those in government at the time.
3. **Independence** of the accountability institutions from the executive.
4. **Due process and speed:** evidence, hearing, decision within a fixed time.
5. **Declaration and audit of assets** at entry, during service and after, as Umar (RA) practised.
6. **Clear rules on gifts and conflict of interest.**
7. **Recovery of public wealth.**
8. **Prevention:** merit in appointments, adequate pay, digital systems that remove discretion.
9. **Access to information and strong ombudsman offices,** the modern form of the grievance court.
10. **Protection for those who report wrongdoing.**
11. **Moral training,** so that the inner check is restored.

### 8. Conclusion

Accountability in Islam rests on two pillars: the believer's own fear of standing before Allah, and society's right to question those in authority. The Rightly Guided Caliphs joined the two more completely than any rulers before or since. Pakistan's institutions have the forms of accountability; what they lack is its impartiality and its spirit. The standard was set by the saying every Muslim official should keep before him: "Each of you is a shepherd and each of you is responsible for his flock."

## MA-14. Define Ijtihad. Explain its principles and its importance in the modern world. Can legislation by a parliament take the place of Ijma?

### 1. Introduction

Islam claims to guide mankind in every age. Yet the verses of the Quran and the sayings of the Prophet (PBUH) are limited in number, while new situations never end. The bridge between the fixed texts and changing life is *ijtihad*. Literally the word means to exert oneself to the utmost. In law it is the utmost effort of a qualified jurist to derive a ruling of the Shariah from its sources on a matter not settled by a definitive text. Allama Iqbal called it "the principle of movement in the structure of Islam". When the jurists of an age all reach the same conclusion, the result is *ijma*, consensus, the third source of law. This answer explains the basis, conditions and principles of ijtihad, shows why it is needed today, and examines Iqbal's proposal that a parliament may exercise it.

### 2. Basis of ijtihad

**Quran**

- "Had they referred it to the Messenger and to those in authority among them, those able to draw out its meaning would have known it" (An-Nisa 4:83).
- "Those who strive for Us, We shall guide them to Our ways" (Al-Ankabut 29:69).
- A group should stay behind "to gain understanding of the religion" (At-Tawbah 9:122).
- "If you differ over anything, refer it to Allah and the Messenger" (An-Nisa 4:59).

**Sunnah**

- "When a judge gives a ruling, having tried his best, and is right, he has two rewards; and when he tries his best and errs, he has one" (Sahih al-Bukhari; Sahih Muslim). Error in sincere ijtihad is rewarded, not punished.
- When Mu'adh ibn Jabal (RA) was sent to Yemen he said he would judge by the Book of Allah, then by the Sunnah, and then by his own reasoned opinion, and the Prophet (PBUH) approved (Sunan Abi Dawud; Jami at-Tirmidhi; the chain has been discussed, but jurists have accepted the report).
- After the Battle of the Trench he told the Companions to pray the afternoon prayer at Banu Qurayzah. Some prayed on the way, reading the order as a call to hurry; others delayed the prayer until they arrived. He blamed neither group (Sahih al-Bukhari).

**Practice of the Companions**

Abu Bakr (RA) decided to collect the Quran in one volume. Umar (RA) kept the conquered lands of Iraq as public property, suspended the penalty for theft in a year of famine, and stopped payments to "those whose hearts are to be reconciled" when the need had passed. Each was a fresh judgment guided by the purposes of the law.

### 3. Scope

There is no ijtihad against a text that is certain in both authenticity and meaning, such as the five prayers or the ban on usury. It operates in three fields: texts that bear more than one meaning; reports whose authenticity is probable; and matters on which the texts are silent. The third is the widest.

### 4. Qualifications of a mujtahid

1. Sound faith and upright character.
2. Mastery of Arabic.
3. Knowledge of the Quran, especially its legal verses and their contexts.
4. Knowledge of the Sunnah and the science of hadith.
5. Knowledge of the points on which consensus exists.
6. Command of the principles of jurisprudence, including analogy.
7. Understanding of the objectives of the Shariah (*maqasid*): the protection of religion, life, intellect, family and property. Al-Shatibi made this the chief condition.
8. Knowledge of the conditions and customs of his own time.

Today no single person can master all the sciences that modern questions involve. This is why collective ijtihad, in which jurists sit with economists, physicians and scientists, has become the preferred form.

### 5. Principles and tools of ijtihad

1. **Order of sources:** Quran, Sunnah, consensus, analogy.
2. **Analogy (*qiyas*):** extending a known ruling to a new case with the same cause. Wine is forbidden because it intoxicates; so every intoxicant is forbidden.
3. **Juristic preference (*istihsan*):** setting aside a strict analogy for a stronger consideration of fairness.
4. **Public interest (*maslahah*):** ruling for the common good where no text applies.
5. **Custom (*urf*)** that does not contradict a text.
6. **Presumption of continuity (*istishab*):** things remain permitted until proved otherwise.
7. **Blocking the means to harm.**
8. **Removal of hardship:** "Allah intends ease for you" (Al-Baqarah 2:185); "Allah does not burden a soul beyond its capacity" (Al-Baqarah 2:286); "No harming and no returning harm" (Sunan Ibn Majah).
9. **Necessity** makes the forbidden permissible to the extent required (Al-Baqarah 2:173).
10. **Change of ruling with change of circumstances,** in matters based on custom and interest, as Ibn al-Qayyim explained.

### 6. Kinds

- **By rank:** the independent mujtahid (the founders of the schools); the mujtahid within a school; the scholar who chooses between existing opinions.
- **By method:** interpreting a text; reasoning by analogy; reasoning from public interest.
- **By agent:** individual or collective.

### 7. History in brief

The first three centuries were an age of free ijtihad that produced the great schools of Abu Hanifah, Malik, al-Shafi'i and Ahmad ibn Hanbal, and the Ja'fari school. Later, scholars increasingly confined themselves to following these schools, and it came to be said that "the gate of ijtihad was closed". The reasons included fear of unqualified opinion, political decline and the destruction of Baghdad in 1258. Voices in every age rejected this: Ibn Taymiyyah, al-Suyuti, Shah Waliullah, and in modern times Muhammad Abduh and Iqbal, who called the closing of the gate "a pure fiction".

### 8. Importance in the modern world

**(i) New problems.** Banking and insurance, paper and digital currency, organ transplants, test-tube birth, genetic engineering, artificial intelligence, moon-sighting by calculation, DNA evidence, international treaties, the position of Muslim minorities in the West, climate change. None is mentioned by name in the texts.

**(ii) Universality of Islam.** A religion for all times must be able to answer.

**(iii) Protection from two errors.** Without ijtihad Muslims either cling to rulings framed for other conditions or abandon the Shariah for foreign law.

**(iv) Unity.** Collective ijtihad can narrow the differences between schools.

**(v) Revival.** The Prophet (PBUH) foretold renewers of the religion in every century (Sunan Abi Dawud).

The response to the COVID-19 pandemic, when scholars across the world quickly ruled on closing mosques and on vaccination, showed living ijtihad.

**Institutions:** the International Islamic Fiqh Academy of the OIC in Jeddah; the Fiqh Council of the Muslim World League; al-Azhar's research academy; the standards body for Islamic finance; and in Pakistan the Council of Islamic Ideology and the Federal Shariat Court.

### 9. Ijma and the legislature

**Ijma** is the agreement of all qualified jurists of the community in a period after the Prophet (PBUH) on a legal ruling. Its authority rests on verses such as An-Nisa 4:115, which warns against following "a way other than that of the believers", and on the report: "My community will not unite on misguidance" (Sunan Ibn Majah). Examples are the collection of the Quran and the grandmother's share in inheritance.

**Iqbal's view.** In the sixth lecture of his *Reconstruction* he argued that, with the Muslim world divided into states and schools, the only practical form of ijma today is a Muslim legislative assembly. He proposed transferring the power of ijtihad from individual scholars to such an assembly, where laymen with insight into affairs can also contribute. He saw the danger that members might not know Islamic law, and suggested that scholars sit in the assembly or guide it.

**The objection.** Classical ijma requires *qualified jurists* and *unanimity* across the *whole community*. A national parliament is elected by popular vote, decides by majority and speaks for one country.

**A balanced answer**

1. Legislation by a Muslim parliament, within the limits of the Quran and Sunnah, is valid collective ijtihad and is binding as the law of that state.
2. It is not ijma in the technical sense unless qualified scholars of the Ummah concur.
3. The gap is narrowed when parliament works with scholarly bodies. Pakistan's Constitution attempts this: no law may be repugnant to the Quran and Sunnah (Article 227); the Council of Islamic Ideology advises; the Federal Shariat Court reviews.
4. No assembly can alter a clear text.

### 10. Conclusion

Ijtihad is the means by which Islam's permanent principles meet a changing world. It requires learning, method and sincerity, and in our age it is best exercised collectively. Its neglect was a cause of Muslim decline; its revival, through reformed legal education and strong institutions working with elected legislatures, is a condition of recovery. The Prophet's (PBUH) promise remains an encouragement: the one who strives and errs still has a reward, and the one who strives and is right has two.

## MA-15. Describe the main principles of the Islamic economic system. What are the harms of concentration of wealth, and how does Islam prevent it and eliminate poverty?

### 1. Introduction

The Islamic economic system is that part of the Shariah which governs how wealth is earned, owned, exchanged, spent and shared. It rests on a simple belief: everything belongs to Allah, and human beings hold it as trustees. "Spend out of that in which He has made you successors" (Al-Hadid 57:7). From this follow its two aims: lawful prosperity for the individual, and justice in distribution for society. Its guiding rule on distribution is stated in the Quran: wealth must not "circulate only among the rich among you" (Al-Hashr 59:7). The system stands between capitalism, which leaves distribution to the market, and socialism, which abolishes private ownership. It is neither; it is a balanced order with its own moral foundation.

### 2. Main principles

**(i) Divine ownership and human trusteeship.** "To Allah belongs whatever is in the heavens and the earth" (Al-Baqarah 2:284). The earth's resources were created for all people (Al-Baqarah 2:29).

**(ii) Private ownership within limits.** Property lawfully earned or inherited is protected: "Do not consume one another's wealth wrongfully, but let there be trade by mutual consent" (An-Nisa 4:29). Ownership carries duties, and certain resources remain common: "Muslims are partners in three things: water, pasture and fire" (Sunan Abi Dawud).

**(iii) Lawful and unlawful.** Earning and spending must be halal (Al-Baqarah 2:168). Usury, gambling (Al-Ma'idah 5:90), fraud, bribery (Al-Baqarah 2:188), hoarding and trade in harmful things are forbidden.

**(iv) Prohibition of riba.** "Allah has permitted trade and forbidden riba" (Al-Baqarah 2:275). Those who persist are warned of "war from Allah and His Messenger" (2:279). The Prophet (PBUH) cursed the taker, the giver, the writer and the witnesses of usury (Sahih Muslim). Interest guarantees a return to the lender while the borrower bears all the risk, and so moves wealth steadily from the many to the few.

**(v) Zakat.** A compulsory annual transfer from the rich to eight named categories (At-Tawbah 9:60).

**(vi) Voluntary spending.** "They ask you what they should spend. Say: what is beyond your needs" (Al-Baqarah 2:219). Charity, endowments and interest-free loans are strongly urged (Al-Baqarah 2:261).

**(vii) Law of inheritance.** An estate is divided among many heirs by fixed shares (An-Nisa 4:11–12), so large fortunes break up in each generation.

**(viii) Free but fair market.** Honest weights (Al-Mutaffifin 83:1–3), no deception ("Whoever cheats is not one of us", Sahih Muslim), no hoarding ("No one hoards but a wrongdoer", Sahih Muslim), written contracts (Al-Baqarah 2:282), and state supervision.

**(ix) Dignity of labour and fair wages.** "No one has ever eaten better food than what he earns by the work of his own hand" (Sahih al-Bukhari). "Give the worker his wage before his sweat dries" (Sunan Ibn Majah).

**(x) Moderation in consumption.** "Eat and drink, but do not be excessive" (Al-A'raf 7:31). "The wasteful are brothers of the devils" (Al-Isra 17:27).

**(xi) Risk-sharing finance.** Partnership and profit-sharing replace lending at interest.

**(xii) Responsibility of the state** for the basic needs of those who cannot earn.

### 3. Comparison with other systems

| | Capitalism | Socialism | Islam |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ownership | Private, almost unlimited | State | Private with duties; some public |
| Interest | The basis of finance | — | Forbidden |
| Distribution | Market | State | Market, plus zakat, inheritance, charity |
| Freedom | High | Low | Balanced by moral limits |
| Motive | Profit | Plan | Lawful profit and the pleasure of Allah |

### 4. Harms of the concentration of wealth

**(i) Economic.** When money gathers in few hands, the majority cannot buy, demand falls, and unemployment rises. Capital is hoarded or used for speculation. Monopolies raise prices.

**(ii) Social.** Society splits into classes. Envy and resentment grow; so do begging and crime. The poor are shut out of education, health care and justice.

**(iii) Political.** The wealthy capture the state and shape the laws in their own favour. Corruption spreads.

**(iv) Moral.** Luxury and arrogance among the rich; despair among the poor. The Quran's example is Qarun, whose treasures made him insolent until the earth swallowed him (Al-Qasas 28:76–82). "Woe to every slanderer and mocker, who gathers wealth and counts it" (Al-Humazah 104:1–2).

**(v) Civilizational.** "When We intend to destroy a town, We command its affluent, but they defy the command in it" (Al-Isra 17:16). Shah Waliullah traced the fall of empires to the burden of an idle, luxurious class upon the workers.

**(vi) Spiritual.** "Rivalry in worldly increase distracts you until you reach the graves" (At-Takathur 102:1–2).

### 5. How Islam prevents concentration

| Measure | How it works |
|---|---|
| Zakat and ushr | A yearly levy on idle wealth and produce, paid to the poor |
| Ban on riba | Removes the main engine of unearned accumulation |
| Inheritance | Divides estates |
| Ban on hoarding and monopoly | "Those who hoard gold and silver ... give them news of a painful punishment" (At-Tawbah 9:34) |
| Ban on gambling and speculation | Stops gain without productive effort |
| Spending the surplus; endowments | Moves wealth voluntarily |
| Public ownership of essential resources | "No reserved pasture except for Allah and His Messenger" (Sahih al-Bukhari) |
| Fair wages; prompt payment | Gives labour its share |
| Ban on waste and display | Limits luxury consumption |
| State action | Collection and fair distribution; grants of unused land to those who will cultivate it (Sunan Abi Dawud) |

Precedent: the Prophet (PBUH) gave the lands of Banu Nadir mainly to the poor Emigrants. Umar (RA) refused to divide the conquered lands of Iraq among the soldiers and kept them for the whole community and future generations, citing the verses of Surah Al-Hashr.

### 6. How the system eliminates poverty

1. **Work first.** Earning is a duty; begging by the able is condemned. "It is better for one of you to take his rope and bring a bundle of firewood on his back than to beg" (Sahih al-Bukhari).
2. **Family support.** Relatives must maintain their needy kin.
3. **Zakat,** best used to make the poor self-supporting.
4. **Charity and endowments,** which for centuries paid for hospitals, schools and water supply in Muslim lands.
5. **Interest-free credit and partnership finance** for those without capital.
6. **Relief of debtors** (Al-Baqarah 2:280).
7. **State guarantee** of food, clothing and shelter from the public treasury. Umar (RA) fixed stipends for children, the old and the disabled, including non-Muslims.
8. **Removal of the causes:** usury, hoarding, exploitation and waste.

The poor have a *right* in the wealth of the rich (Adh-Dhariyat 51:19). Under Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz, the histories report, officials struggled to find people entitled to zakat.

### 7. Contemporary relevance

Global inequality is at extreme levels, and debt burdens poor nations. Interest-based finance was at the centre of the 2008 crisis. Islamic finance, built on real assets and shared risk, has grown into a worldwide industry. In Pakistan, wealth and land are concentrated, taxes fall mainly on consumption, and public debt consumes much of the budget. The Constitution requires the elimination of riba (Article 38), the Federal Shariat Court ruled against bank interest in 2022, and a constitutional amendment of 2024 set the beginning of 2028 as the target for ending it.

**Recommendations**

1. A firm, phased move to interest-free banking based on genuine profit-sharing.
2. A transparent zakat and ushr system linked to poverty data.
3. Fair, progressive taxation and action against cartels and hoarders.
4. Enforcement of inheritance law, especially women's shares.
5. Interest-free micro-finance and revival of endowments for health and education.
6. Simplicity by rulers and an end to wasteful state spending.

### 8. Conclusion

The Islamic economic system accepts private enterprise and the market, and binds them with moral limits and compulsory sharing. It attacks the concentration of wealth at its sources (usury, hoarding, monopoly) and corrects what remains through zakat, inheritance and charity. It treats poverty as a wrong that society must remove, and gives the poor a right, not a favour. Its aim is the Quran's own: that wealth should not "circulate only among the rich among you" (Al-Hashr 59:7).
