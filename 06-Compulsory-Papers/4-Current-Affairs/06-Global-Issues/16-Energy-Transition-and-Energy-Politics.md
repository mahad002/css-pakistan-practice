## 1. Background

- **Energy and power.** Control of fuel has shaped world politics: the 1973 Arab oil embargo; the 1979 Iranian revolution; the Gulf wars; Russia's gas leverage over Europe; the US shale boom that made America the largest oil and gas producer.
- **Institutions.** OPEC (1960; 12 members after Angola left in 2024 **[verify]**) and OPEC+ (with Russia, since 2016); the International Energy Agency (1974, consumers; emergency stocks); IRENA (2009, renewables); the Gas Exporting Countries Forum; the Energy Charter Treaty (many EU states have left).
- **Chokepoints.** Strait of Hormuz (about 20 million barrels a day, roughly a fifth of world oil consumption, and about a fifth of LNG trade), Malacca, Bab el-Mandeb and Suez, the Turkish Straits, Panama.

**Winners and losers (analysis):** exporters outside the Gulf (the US, Russia, Norway, Brazil) gained; Asian importers (India, Japan, South Korea, Pakistan, Bangladesh) and Europe lost; Gulf states' image as safe suppliers was damaged; China drew on stockpiles, overland supply from Russia and Central Asia, and its electric-vehicle fleet.

## 3. The energy transition

- **Meaning:** shifting from fossil fuels (about 80% of primary energy) to low-carbon sources, with electrification and efficiency.
- **Progress** (**[verify latest figures]**): clean-energy investment is roughly double fossil investment (IEA: over $2 trillion of $3.3 trillion in 2025); solar is the cheapest new power in most countries; renewables overtook coal in global electricity generation in 2025 (Ember); electric cars exceed a fifth of new sales; COP28 pledged to triple renewables and double efficiency gains by 2030.
- **Limits:** emissions are still at record highs; coal use remains high in Asia; grids and storage lag; AI data centres add demand; oil and gas companies and producer states resist a timetable (the COP31 collapse, November 2026).
- **New geopolitics:**
  1. **China dominates clean technology:** over 80% of solar manufacturing, most battery production, and most processing of lithium, cobalt, graphite and rare earths.
  2. **Critical minerals** are the new oil: DR Congo (cobalt), Chile and Australia (lithium), Indonesia (nickel); US minerals deals with Ukraine, Central Asia, Pakistan and others; China's export controls.
  3. **Petro-states** face long-run decline and diversify (Saudi Vision 2030).
  4. **Carbon border taxes** (the EU's CBAM, 2026) reshape trade.
  5. **US reversal:** withdrawal from Paris; "energy dominance" through oil, gas and LNG exports; cuts to clean-energy subsidies.
  6. **Energy security now argues for renewables:** sun and wind cannot be blockaded. The 2026 shock strengthened this case in importing countries.
- **Just transition:** who pays for poor countries? Just Energy Transition Partnerships (South Africa, Indonesia, Vietnam) delivered slowly.

## 4. Global energy governance (2023 question)

Fragmented: no single body covers producers and consumers, fossil and clean energy. The IEA speaks for rich consumers (China and India are only associates); OPEC+ manages supply; the UNFCCC handles emissions, not energy; the G20 coordinates loosely. Gaps: investment in poor countries (Africa has 60% of the best solar resource and about 2–3% of clean-energy investment **[verify]**), minerals governance, and emergency coordination for gas.

## 5. Pakistan

- Imports about a third or more of its energy; the fuel import bill is its largest (roughly $15–17bn a year **[verify FY26]**).
- 2026: record pump prices, austerity, inflation; LNG supply risk from Qatar.
- A grassroots solar boom (about 17 GW of panels imported in 2024 **[verify]**), surplus grid capacity and circular debt (domestic file `08`).
- Stalled pipelines: Iran–Pakistan, TAPI; CASA-1000 near completion.
- Assets: Thar coal, hydropower potential (about 60,000 MW), wind corridor (Gharo–Jhimpir), nuclear, sun.

## 6. Way forward

1. Importers: diversify suppliers and routes; build strategic stocks; accelerate renewables, storage and grids.
2. A producer–consumer dialogue on keeping chokepoints open; a Gulf security framework including Iran.
3. Finance and technology for the transition in developing countries.
4. Transparent, fair minerals supply chains.
5. Pakistan: an energy-security strategy that puts indigenous and renewable sources first, fixes the grid and its finances, and keeps regional import options open.
