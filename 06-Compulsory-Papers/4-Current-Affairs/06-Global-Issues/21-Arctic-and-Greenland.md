# The Arctic and Greenland

> **As of:** 23 November 2026. **Syllabus:** Global issues – environment; international security; energy politics. **Past papers:** none. Short-note material. Relevant in 2028 because of the 2026 Greenland crisis.

---

## 1. Basic facts

- **Arctic states (8):** Russia, United States (Alaska), Canada, Denmark (through Greenland), Norway, Iceland, Sweden, Finland. Seven are NATO members since Finland (2023) and Sweden (2024) joined. Russia holds about half the Arctic coastline.
- **Arctic Council** (1996, Ottawa Declaration): the eight states plus indigenous organisations; deals with environment and development, not military security; consensus-based; cooperation with Russia largely frozen since 2022. China, India, Japan and others are observers (China calls itself a "near-Arctic state", 2018).
- **Law:** no Arctic treaty comparable to the Antarctic Treaty (1959). The UN Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS) governs: 200-mile exclusive economic zones and continental-shelf claims (overlapping claims to the Lomonosov Ridge by Russia, Denmark and Canada). The US has not ratified UNCLOS. The Svalbard Treaty (1920) gives Norway sovereignty with access rights for others.

## 2. Why it matters

1. **Climate:** the Arctic is warming about four times faster than the global average; summer sea ice has shrunk by roughly 40% since 1979; Greenland's ice sheet holds enough water to raise sea level by about 7 metres; thawing permafrost releases methane.
2. **Shipping:** the Northern Sea Route along Russia's coast cuts the Asia–Europe distance by about 40% compared with Suez; the Northwest Passage through Canada; traffic is still small and seasonal.
3. **Resources:** an estimated 13% of the world's undiscovered oil and 30% of undiscovered gas (US Geological Survey, 2008); rare earths, zinc, nickel, fish.
4. **Military:** Russia's Northern Fleet and nuclear submarines on the Kola Peninsula; reopened Soviet bases; the GIUK gap (Greenland–Iceland–UK) for submarine tracking; US missile-warning radar at Pituffik (Thule) in Greenland; NATO exercises.
5. **China:** a "Polar Silk Road"; investment in Russian LNG (Yamal); research stations; joint patrols with Russia near Alaska.

## 3. The Greenland crisis (2025–26)

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| Background | Greenland: the world's largest island, about 57,000 people, mostly Inuit; a self-governing territory of Denmark (home rule 1979; self-government 2009 with a right to independence by referendum). The US has had a base there since the Second World War (1951 defence agreement). Trump first proposed buying it in 2019. |
| 2025 | The Trump administration pressed to acquire Greenland for "national security"; Vice President Vance visited Pituffik (March); an envoy was appointed (December). Greenland's March 2025 election produced a coalition favouring slow independence and rejecting US annexation. Denmark summoned the US ambassador. |
| January 2026 | After the Venezuela operation, Trump refused to rule out force and announced a 10% tariff on eight European countries from 1 February, rising to 25%, unless Denmark agreed to sell. The EU backed Denmark; Denmark and eight NATO allies sent forces to Greenland. On 21 January, after talks with NATO's Secretary-General Mark Rutte, Trump ruled out force and dropped the tariffs, citing a "framework". |
| July 2026 | At the NATO summit in Ankara, Trump repeated that US control would be better. |
| **18–22 Sept 2026** | **The US, Denmark and Greenland reached and signed a defence agreement** giving the US an expanded military presence and a larger security role, **without any transfer of sovereignty**. |

## 4. Assessment (analysis)

- **For the world order:** a NATO leader threatened an ally's territory, which weakened the norm against acquiring territory by coercion that the West invokes over Ukraine. The outcome (bases without sovereignty) shows both the limits of coercion among allies and how much it can still extract.
- **For Europe:** a push for "strategic autonomy" and Arctic defence spending.
- **For Russia and China:** propaganda value; an opening to present themselves as defenders of sovereignty; a harder NATO presence in the High North.
- **For Greenlanders:** leverage for more autonomy and investment; fear of being traded between powers.
- **Theory:** realism (great powers seek control of strategic space and minerals) against liberal alliance norms.

## 5. Issues ahead

1. Militarisation without an arms-control or security forum.
2. Shipping rules, oil-spill risk and the IMO's Polar Code.
3. Mining of rare earths in Greenland against environmental limits (a uranium ban since 2021).
4. Indigenous rights.
5. Whether the Arctic Council can function with Russia.
6. Feedback loops: ice loss accelerates global warming and sea-level rise that threaten low-lying states.

## 6. Relevance to Pakistan

- Indirect but real: sea-level rise threatens the Indus delta and Karachi; changes in the jet stream affect monsoon and heatwave patterns; the "third pole" (Hindu Kush–Karakoram–Himalaya glaciers) faces the same warming.
- Legal precedent: respect for sovereignty and UNCLOS matters to all small and middle states.
- Shipping: Arctic routes could, over decades, shift some trade away from the Indian Ocean.
