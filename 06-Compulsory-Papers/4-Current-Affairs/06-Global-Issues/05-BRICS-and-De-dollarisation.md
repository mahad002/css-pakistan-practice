## 1. Background

- **Origin.** "BRIC" was coined by Goldman Sachs economist Jim O'Neill (2001). First summit: Yekaterinburg, 2009. South Africa joined in 2010.
- **Institutions.** New Development Bank (NDB, Shanghai, 2014–15; president Dilma Rousseff) and a $100bn Contingent Reserve Arrangement.
- **Expansion.** Egypt, Ethiopia, Iran and the UAE joined on 1 January 2024; Indonesia in January 2025. Saudi Arabia was invited and is usually counted as a member. Argentina declined. A "partner country" tier was created at Kazan (2024) for states such as Belarus, Kazakhstan, Malaysia, Thailand, Cuba, Uganda, Uzbekistan, Nigeria and Vietnam **[verify list]**.
- **Weight.** Roughly 45% of world population and over 35% of world GDP at purchasing power parity, more than the G7 on that measure; major shares of oil output and of critical minerals **[verify figures]**.

## 3. De-dollarisation: what is and is not happening

**Why states want it**
1. US sanctions: freezing about $300bn of Russian reserves (2022) and cutting banks from SWIFT showed the dollar can be a weapon.
2. US debt above $36 trillion and political fights over it **[verify latest]**.
3. Tariff shocks and doubts about US policy stability.
4. China's wish to internationalise the yuan.

**What has changed**
- The dollar's share of disclosed foreign-exchange reserves has fallen from about 71% (1999) to about 56–58% **[verify latest IMF COFER]**.
- Central banks bought over 1,000 tonnes of gold a year in 2022–24; gold overtook the euro as the second-largest reserve asset by value (ECB, 2025) **[verify]**.
- Russia–China trade is settled mostly in roubles and yuan; India paid for Russian oil in rupees, dirhams and yuan; Saudi Arabia is open to non-dollar oil sales.
- Alternatives: China's CIPS payment system; the mBridge central-bank digital currency project; Russia's proposal of a "BRICS Bridge"; local-currency swap lines.

**What has not changed**
- The dollar is on one side of nearly 90% of foreign-exchange trades and dominates trade invoicing and international debt **[verify BIS figures]**.
- No rival offers deep, open capital markets: the yuan is not freely convertible; the euro lacks a single safe asset.
- BRICS has no common currency plan; India opposes one. Members' interests differ (India–China rivalry; Gulf currencies pegged to the dollar).

**Conclusion (analysis):** the trend is **diversification at the margins, not replacement**. The dollar's role erodes slowly. The faster change is the growth of parallel payment channels that reduce the reach of sanctions.

## 4. Does BRICS challenge US dominance? (2025 question)

| Yes | No |
|---|---|
| Economic mass and control of commodities | No treaty, secretariat or common strategy |
| Alternative lenders (NDB) without Western conditions | NDB lending is small beside the World Bank |
| A platform to resist sanctions and demand reform of the IMF and UN | Internal rivalries: India–China, Iran–UAE/Saudi, Egypt–Ethiopia |
| Attracts dozens of applicants: a signal of dissatisfaction | Several members are US security partners (India, UAE, Saudi Arabia, Egypt) |

**US counter-measures:** tariffs and secondary sanctions; courting India and the Gulf; critical-minerals deals; export controls; development-finance alternatives (the DFC, G7 infrastructure plans); and reform offers at the IMF and World Bank.

## 5. Pakistan

- Applied in 2023; supported by China and Russia; membership needs consensus, and India chairs or sits at every table.
- Interests: NDB membership and finance; local-currency trade (yuan settlement with China; barter with Iran, Afghanistan and Russia under the 2023 mechanism); less exposure to dollar shortages.
- Constraints: an IMF programme, a dollar-based debt stock and the US as the top export market.
- Sensible aim: partner-country status and NDB membership; more currency swap lines; no gesture that invites tariff penalties.
