## 1. Basic facts

| State | Capital | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Kazakhstan | Astana | Largest by area and economy; oil, uranium (world's top producer); president Kassym-Jomart Tokayev |
| Uzbekistan | Tashkent | Largest population (about 37 million); reformist president Shavkat Mirziyoyev since 2016; doubly landlocked |
| Turkmenistan | Ashgabat | Fourth-largest gas reserves; "permanent neutrality" |
| Kyrgyzstan | Bishkek | Hydropower; hosted the SCO summit in 2026; president Sadyr Japarov |
| Tajikistan | Dushanbe | Persian-speaking; hydropower; 1,350 km border with Afghanistan; president Emomali Rahmon since 1994 |

About 80 million people; five landlocked states between Russia, China, Iran and Afghanistan. Mackinder's "Heartland"; the nineteenth-century "Great Game" between Britain and Russia; today a "New Great Game" over energy, minerals and routes.

## 2. External powers

| Power | Interests and tools |
|---|---|
| **Russia** | Historic patron; military bases (Tajikistan, Kyrgyzstan); CSTO; Eurasian Economic Union; millions of migrant workers send remittances from Russia. Its influence has weakened since 2022; treatment of Central Asian migrants after the March 2024 Moscow attack caused friction. |
| **China** | Largest trading partner and lender; BRI (launched in Astana, 2013); gas pipelines from Turkmenistan; the China–Kyrgyzstan–Uzbekistan railway under construction; the China–Central Asia summit mechanism (Xi'an 2023; Astana 2025). |
| **United States** | The C5+1 format; a leaders' meeting in Washington (November 2025) focused on critical minerals; Kazakhstan announced it would join the Abraham Accords **[verify]**. |
| **European Union** | Global Gateway investment; the Trans-Caspian "Middle Corridor" to bypass Russia; first EU–Central Asia summit (Samarkand, April 2025). |
| **Türkiye** | Organisation of Turkic States (cultural and economic ties with four of the five). |
| **Iran, India, Pakistan, Gulf states** | Access routes (Chabahar, Bandar Abbas, Gwadar/Karachi); Gulf investment in energy and farms. |

**The region's own strategy:** "multi-vector" diplomacy: balance all powers, join none exclusively. Since 2018 the five presidents hold regular consultative meetings; long-running border disputes eased (the Kyrgyz–Tajik border treaty and a three-way agreement at Khujand in March 2025 **[verify]**).

## 3. Current issues (November 2026)

1. **SCO:** the Bishkek summit (31 August–1 September 2026) marked the organisation's 25th year, agreed a security coordination centre in Bishkek and passed the chair to Pakistan ([Tribune](https://tribune.com.pk/story/2626913/1); [Anadolu](https://www.aa.com.tr/en/world/sco-leaders-adopt-bishkek-declaration-expand-cooperation-on-security-trade-technology/4043993)).
2. **Afghanistan:** pragmatic engagement with the Taliban for trade and security; concern about ISKP (many recruits are Tajik and Uzbek); the Qosh Tepa canal threatens downstream water.
3. **Connectivity:** the Uzbekistan–Afghanistan–Pakistan railway (feasibility stage; Kazakhstan offered financing for a link in January 2026, per [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uzbekistan%E2%80%93Afghanistan%E2%80%93Pakistan_Railway_Project)); the Middle Corridor; the International North–South Transport Corridor through Iran. The Pakistan–Afghanistan war and the Iran war disrupted southern routes in 2026.
4. **Energy and minerals:** uranium, rare earths, lithium, copper draw US, EU and Chinese interest; Kazakhstan is building its first nuclear plant with Rosatom **[verify]**; TAPI and CASA-1000.
5. **Water and climate:** the Aral Sea disaster; shrinking glaciers; upstream–downstream tension (Rogun dam in Tajikistan).
6. **Sanctions spillover:** the region is a channel for re-exports to Russia and faces secondary-sanctions pressure.
7. **Governance:** authoritarian presidencies; unrest in Kazakhstan (January 2022) and Karakalpakstan (2022); succession questions.

## 4. Significance (analysis)

- **For China:** a secure land route west that avoids sea chokepoints, and a buffer for Xinjiang.
- **For Russia:** its last sphere of influence, now shared.
- **For the West:** minerals and a way to limit Russian and Chinese dominance.
- **For South Asia:** energy and markets, blocked by Afghanistan and by India–Pakistan hostility.
- **Theory:** a classic case of small states hedging in a multipolar setting.
