# IR Theories in Simple Terms

> **As of:** 23 November 2026. Theory does not go out of date. The examples do, so refresh them.
> **Why you need this:** The syllabus expects you to "interpret" events. A theory is a lens for interpretation. One or two sentences of theory in an answer show the examiner that you can analyse, not only report.

---

## 1. Three ideas to learn first

| Idea | Meaning | Software analogy |
|---|---|---|
| **Anarchy** | There is no world government above states. It does not mean chaos. | A peer-to-peer network with no central server |
| **Sovereignty** | Each state is the final authority inside its borders. | Root access on your own machine |
| **National interest** | What a state tries to secure: survival first, then wealth, influence and values. | The system's top-priority process |

Every theory below answers one question: *in a world without a central authority, why do states act as they do?*

---

## 2. Realism: power and survival

**Core claims**
1. States are the main actors.
2. The system is anarchic, so each state must protect itself ("self-help").
3. States seek power or security.
4. Morality and law matter less than capabilities.

**Versions**

| Version | Thinker | One-line idea |
|---|---|---|
| Classical realism | Hans Morgenthau, *Politics Among Nations* (1948) | Human nature seeks power, so states do too. |
| Neorealism (structural, "defensive") | Kenneth Waltz, *Theory of International Politics* (1979) | The structure of the system (anarchy, distribution of power) forces states to seek security. |
| Offensive realism | John Mearsheimer, *The Tragedy of Great Power Politics* (2001) | Great powers try to maximise power and become regional hegemons. |

**Key realist concepts:** balance of power, security dilemma, deterrence, alliances, spheres of influence, zero-sum thinking.

**Examples you can use**
- India and Pakistan arming against each other is a **security dilemma**: one side's defence looks like offence to the other.
- The Saudi–Pakistan defence agreement (September 2025) and the Makkah alliance with Türkiye (August 2026) are **external balancing**: weaker states pool power against threats.
- The US–Israel strikes on Iran (February 2026) fit offensive realism: a great power preventing a rival from gaining a decisive capability.
- Mearsheimer's much-quoted argument that NATO expansion provoked Russia is a realist reading of the Ukraine war. Liberals reject it.

**Weakness:** Realism explains conflict well but explains cooperation (EU, WTO, climate treaties) poorly. It also ignores what happens inside states.

## 3. Liberalism: cooperation is possible

**Core claims**
1. States are important, but so are institutions, companies and people.
2. Anarchy can be softened by rules, trade and shared institutions.
3. States can achieve **absolute gains** (everyone wins something) and do not only compare themselves with rivals.

**Versions**

| Version | Thinker | One-line idea |
|---|---|---|
| Democratic peace | Immanuel Kant; Michael Doyle | Democracies rarely fight each other. |
| Commercial liberalism | Richard Cobden; Norman Angell | Trade makes war too costly. |
| Neoliberal institutionalism | Robert Keohane, Joseph Nye | Institutions reduce cheating and build trust. "Complex interdependence" links states through many channels. |

**Key liberal concepts:** interdependence, international regimes, collective security, soft power (Nye), multilateralism, rules-based order.

**Examples**
- The Indus Waters Treaty (1960) survived three wars. Liberals say this shows institutions can work between enemies. Its "abeyance" by India since April 2025 tests that claim, and the August 2026 Court of Arbitration award shows a rule-based forum still functioning.
- CPEC and the BRI are sold in liberal language: connectivity and mutual gain.
- The IMF programme shows institutions shaping a state's domestic policy.

**Weakness:** Interdependence can be turned into a weapon (sanctions, tariffs, export controls, closing a strait). The 2025–26 tariff wars and the Hormuz closure are examples.

## 4. Constructivism: ideas and identities shape interests

**Core claims**
1. Interests are not fixed. They come from identity, history and shared beliefs.
2. Anarchy has no single logic. Alexander Wendt: "Anarchy is what states make of it" (1992).
3. Norms (for example, the taboo on using nuclear weapons, or sovereignty) guide behaviour and can change.

**Examples**
- The US does not fear Britain's nuclear weapons but fears Iran's. The weapons are similar. The relationship and identity differ.
- Pakistan–India hostility is sustained by national narratives on both sides (partition, Kashmir), not only by military balance.
- "Ummah", "Global South" and "Indo-Pacific" are constructed ideas that organise policy. The label "Indo-Pacific" replaced "Asia-Pacific" to bring India into a US-led frame.
- Islamophobia and the 15 March International Day to Combat Islamophobia (UN, 2022) show norm-building.

**Weakness:** It explains *why* preferences exist better than it predicts *what* a state will do next.

## 5. Quick comparison

| Question | Realism | Liberalism | Constructivism |
|---|---|---|---|
| Main actors | States | States, institutions, firms, individuals | States and societies as carriers of ideas |
| What drives behaviour | Power, survival | Interests, rules, mutual gain | Identity, norms, beliefs |
| View of institutions | Tools of the powerful | Independent force for cooperation | Places where norms are formed |
| View of conflict | Normal | Avoidable | Depends on how actors see each other |
| Typical policy advice | Arm, ally, deter | Trade, negotiate, join institutions | Change narratives, build trust, people-to-people links |

## 6. Other approaches (one paragraph each)

- **Marxism / dependency / world-systems theory (Immanuel Wallerstein):** The world economy is divided into a rich core and a poor periphery. The structure keeps the periphery dependent. Use for debt, IMF conditionality, unequal trade and climate injustice.
- **English School (Hedley Bull, *The Anarchical Society*):** States form an "international society" with shared rules (diplomacy, law, balance of power) even without a government.
- **Feminist IR (Cynthia Enloe, J. Ann Tickner):** Asks where women are in war, peace and the economy, and widens "security" to include people.
- **Critical and post-colonial theory (Robert Cox; Edward Said):** "Theory is always for someone and for some purpose." Asks whose interests the "rules-based order" serves.
- **Geopolitics:** Mackinder's Heartland (control of Eurasia), Mahan's sea power, Spykman's Rimland. Use for the BRI, the Indo-Pacific and Gwadar. Tim Marshall's *Prisoners of Geography* (asked as an MCQ in 2026) is a popular modern version.
- **Clash of Civilizations (Samuel Huntington, 1993/1996)** versus **End of History (Francis Fukuyama, 1989/1992):** Two famous post-Cold War predictions. Know the author and the one-line claim of each.
- **Thucydides Trap (Graham Allison, *Destined for War*, 2017):** A rising power (China) and a ruling power (US) tend towards war. Use in every US–China answer.
- **Power transition theory (A. F. K. Organski):** War is most likely when a challenger approaches parity with the dominant state.

## 7. Levels of analysis (Kenneth Waltz's "three images")

| Level | Looks at | Example: May 2025 India–Pakistan conflict |
|---|---|---|
| Individual | Leaders' beliefs and personalities | Decisions of Modi, Munir, Sharif; Trump's personal mediation |
| State | Regime type, domestic politics, economy | Indian domestic pressure after Pahalgam; Pakistan's civil–military structure |
| System | Distribution of power, alliances | Nuclear deterrence; China's support for Pakistan; US interest in avoiding war |

A strong answer touches at least two levels.

---

## 8. How to use theory in a 20-mark answer

1. **One sentence in the introduction:** "From a realist view, the Saudi–Pakistan pact is balancing behaviour by two states facing an uncertain US security guarantee."
2. **One heading in the analysis:** "A classic security dilemma".
3. **A contrast in the critical section:** "Liberals would reply that the Indus Waters Treaty proves institutions can restrain rivals. Events since April 2025 weaken that case, but the 2026 award shows the legal track is still alive."
4. **Do not write a theory essay.** Two or three sentences in total are enough. Name the thinker once.

**Common errors:** calling realism "pessimism" and liberalism "optimism" without content; quoting thinkers you cannot place; using theory in place of facts.
