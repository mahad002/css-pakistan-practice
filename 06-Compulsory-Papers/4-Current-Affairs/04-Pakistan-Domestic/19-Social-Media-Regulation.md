## 1. Background

- **Constitution.** Article 19 guarantees freedom of speech and of the press, subject to "reasonable restrictions" for the glory of Islam, security, friendly relations with foreign states, public order, decency, contempt of court and incitement. Article 19A gives a right to information.
- **Main law.** The Prevention of Electronic Crimes Act (PECA) 2016 covers hacking, fraud, harassment, hate speech and online defamation. Section 37 lets the telecom regulator (PTA) block content.
- **Earlier attempts to tighten:** the 2020–21 social media rules; the February 2022 ordinance on online defamation, struck down by the Islamabad High Court in April 2022.
- **Platform bans:** YouTube (2012–16), TikTok (several short bans), Wikipedia (briefly, 2023), and **X/Twitter, blocked from February 2024 after the general election** and restored during the May 2025 conflict **[verify date]**.
- **"Firewall":** a national web-management system upgraded in 2024 was blamed for slow internet and VPN disruption.

## 3. The debate

| Case for stronger regulation | Case against the present approach |
|---|---|
| Real harms: disinformation, deepfakes, blasphemy accusations that lead to mob violence, online harassment of women, terrorist propaganda, financial fraud. | "Fake news" is undefined; the state decides what is true. The law is used mainly against critics and journalists. |
| Foreign platforms ignore local law and have no offices in Pakistan. | Regulators are appointed and controlled by the executive; no independent oversight. |
| Hybrid warfare: hostile states run influence campaigns (EU DisinfoLab documented an India-linked network in 2020). | Shutdowns and throttling cost the IT sector and freelancers heavily and deter investment. |
| Democracies also regulate (EU Digital Services Act, UK Online Safety Act, Germany's NetzDG). | Those laws target platforms' systems, with courts and independent regulators, not individual speech with prison terms. |

## 4. Challenges

1. Balancing Article 19 with public order.
2. Weak media literacy; rumours travel faster than corrections.
3. Polarisation and organised trolling by all political camps.
4. Self-censorship in mainstream media pushes debate onto unregulated platforms.
5. No data-protection law; surveillance concerns.
6. Generative AI lowers the cost of fabrication.
7. Platforms' poor Urdu and regional-language moderation.

## 5. Way forward

1. Define offences narrowly (incitement, hate speech, child abuse, fraud, non-consensual images). Remove or tightly define "fake news"; make defamation a civil matter.
2. An independent regulator appointed through parliament, with judicial review in the high courts.
3. Transparency: publish every blocking order and its legal basis; platform transparency reports.
4. Co-regulation: require platforms to appoint local grievance officers and meet response deadlines, in exchange for clear, lawful procedures.
5. No blanket shutdowns; any restriction to be necessary, proportionate and time-limited.
6. Pass the data-protection law.
7. Media and digital literacy in schools; support independent fact-checkers.
8. Protect journalists (enforce the 2021 Protection of Journalists Act).
9. Label AI-generated content; election-period rules on deepfakes.

## 7. Model answer outline (Question 1)

- **Intro:** over 100 million users **[verify]**; real harms and real over-reach. Thesis: regulate platforms' conduct through an independent, transparent system; do not criminalise vague categories of speech.
- **Background:** Article 19; PECA 2016; bans; 2025 amendment.
- **Harms that justify regulation;** **problems in the current law.**
- **Comparative table:** EU DSA (systemic duties, fines, independent coordinators) against PECA 2025 (content offences, executive regulator).
- **Diagram:** a balance scale: on one side security, order, dignity; on the other speech, information, economy; with "necessity and proportionality" as the pivot.
- **Way forward:** nine points.
- **Conclusion:** trust in information comes from credible institutions, not from control.
