# Afghan Refugees Policy

> **As of:** 23 November 2026. **Syllabus:** Domestic – social/political; external – Afghanistan; global issues – human rights. **Past papers:** not asked alone; relevant to 2022 and 2025 Afghanistan questions. Read with `06-Global-Issues/13`.

---

## 1. Background

- **Four waves:** after the Soviet invasion (1979; over 3 million by the late 1980s), the civil war of the 1990s, the US intervention (2001), and the Taliban takeover (August 2021; an estimated 600,000 or more new arrivals).
- **Legal position.** Pakistan has not signed the 1951 Refugee Convention or its 1967 Protocol and has no national refugee law. Afghans stayed under administrative arrangements with UNHCR.
- **Categories before 2023:**

| Category | Approx. number | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Proof of Registration (PoR) card holders | 1.3–1.4 million | Registered refugees |
| Afghan Citizen Card (ACC) holders | About 0.8 million | Documented but not refugees |
| Undocumented | Estimated 1 million or more | No legal status |

- **Contribution and cost.** Pakistan hosted one of the world's largest refugee populations for over four decades with limited international help. Many Afghans were born in Pakistan and have never lived in Afghanistan.

## 2. Current situation (November 2026)

| Date | Step | Source |
|---|---|---|
| October 2023 | "Illegal Foreigners Repatriation Plan": undocumented foreigners told to leave by 1 November 2023. | UNHCR–IOM updates |
| April 2025 | Second phase: ACC holders. | Same |
| 30 June 2025 | PoR cards expired and were not renewed; a 1 September 2025 deadline was set for holders to leave. | [UNHCR documents](https://data.unhcr.org/en/documents/download/124778) |
| 2025–26 | Refugee villages closed; arrests and expulsions reported, including of registered refugees. | UNHCR |

**Numbers.**
- About **2.66 million** Afghans returned from Pakistan between September 2023 and 1 August 2026, of whom about 358,000 were deported ([UNHCR–IOM flash update](https://www.ecoi.net/en/file/local/2142871/UNHCR%20-%20IOM%20Flash%20Update%20103-v3.pdf)).
- In 2026 alone (to 31 October): about **1 million returns, including 155,000 deportations** (UNHCR key figures).
- Iran also expelled large numbers in 2025–26, so Afghanistan received several million returnees in two years.

**Positions.**

| Government of Pakistan | UNHCR, rights groups, Kabul |
|---|---|
| Every state may remove foreigners without legal status. | Forced return to a country with a humanitarian crisis and restrictions on women risks breaching *non-refoulement*, a customary rule that binds all states. |
| Security: officials link Afghan nationals to a share of suicide attacks and to TTP facilitation. | Collective punishment; most refugees have no link to militancy. |
| Economic strain; smuggling; pressure on services. | Many were born in Pakistan; families and livelihoods are being broken. |
| Forty years of hospitality with shrinking donor support. | Returns should be voluntary, gradual and dignified. |

**Context.** The policy hardened with the Pakistan–Afghanistan war (October 2025; February 2026 onwards). Kabul treats the expulsions as pressure; Islamabad treats them as sovereign policy.

## 3. Challenges

1. **Humanitarian:** winter returns, loss of property, women and girls returning to bans on education and work, people at risk (former officials, journalists, musicians).
2. **Legal vacuum:** no asylum law means decisions are administrative and inconsistent.
3. **Security pay-off is unproven:** mass returns may create resentment that militants exploit.
4. **Economic effects:** loss of labour and traders in KP and Balochistan; disruption of transit trade.
5. **Diplomatic cost:** criticism from the UN; strain with Kabul; third-country resettlement cases stranded after the US paused admissions in 2025.
6. **Absorption in Afghanistan:** drought, aid cuts and a weak economy mean returnees may try to come back.

## 4. Way forward

1. Enact a national refugee and asylum law; consider acceding to the 1951 Convention with reservations.
2. Screen individually: protect those at risk; regularise long-term residents, spouses of Pakistanis and Pakistan-born Afghans through visas or residence permits.
3. Open legal channels: work, study, medical and business visas at scale; a digital border regime.
4. Phase returns with UNHCR and IOM; avoid winter; allow people to sell assets and carry savings.
5. Seek burden-sharing: a donor conference for host areas and for reintegration inside Afghanistan.
6. Separate counter-terrorism (intelligence-led, individual) from refugee policy (group-based).
7. Use the issue as part of a wider settlement with Kabul on the TTP, trade and border management.

## 5. Likely exam questions

1. Critically evaluate Pakistan's policy of repatriating Afghan nationals since 2023.
2. Does mass repatriation serve Pakistan's security interests?
3. Discuss Pakistan's obligations towards refugees under international law despite not signing the 1951 Convention.

## 6. Model answer outline (Question 1)

- **Intro:** 2.66 million returns since September 2023; thesis: the state has a right to regulate entry, but a blanket, hurried policy carries legal, security and diplomatic costs that a selective policy would avoid.
- **Background:** four waves; documents; no law.
- **The policy and its reasons;** the numbers.
- **Evaluation:** security claim; economy; humanitarian law (*non-refoulement*); effect on relations with Kabul.
- **Diagram:** timeline 1979–2026 with refugee numbers, or a flow diagram of the three categories and deadlines.
- **Way forward:** seven points.
- **Conclusion:** an orderly, lawful exit from the refugee era protects both security and Pakistan's reputation.
