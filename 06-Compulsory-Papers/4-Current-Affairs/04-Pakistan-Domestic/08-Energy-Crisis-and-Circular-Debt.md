# Energy Crisis and Circular Debt

> **As of:** 23 November 2026. **Syllabus:** Domestic affairs – economic; global energy politics. **Past papers:** 2018 (Iran–Pakistan pipeline), 2019 (energy policy 2013–18), 2023 (energy and geopolitics). Read with `06-Global-Issues/16`.

## 1. Background

- **From shortage to surplus.** Until about 2015 the problem was load-shedding of up to 12 hours. Under the 2013–18 policy and CPEC, large plants were added (coal, LNG, nuclear, hydro). Installed capacity is now about 46,000 MW against a summer peak near 30,000 MW and a winter demand of 10,000–12,000 MW **[verify latest NEPRA figures]**.
- **The new problem is cost.** Private plants (IPPs) were built on "take-or-pay" contracts: the state pays a fixed **capacity charge**, indexed to the dollar, whether or not it buys the power. When demand fell and the rupee lost value, capacity charges came to dominate bills.
- **Circular debt, in one line:** distribution companies (DISCOs) do not recover the full cost of electricity, so they cannot pay the central buyer, which cannot pay generators, who cannot pay fuel suppliers. The unpaid stock is the circular debt.

## 4. Causes (analysis)

1. **Contract design:** dollar-indexed, take-or-pay capacity with guaranteed returns.
2. **Planning error:** capacity was built for demand that did not arrive; industry and households then left the grid for solar, raising the fixed cost for those who remain (the "utility death spiral").
3. **Losses and theft; poor bill recovery** in several DISCOs.
4. **Transmission bottlenecks:** cheap southern power cannot reach northern load centres.
5. **Imported fuel dependence** and exchange-rate exposure.
6. **Pricing politics:** uniform national tariff, cross-subsidies and delayed tariff notifications.
7. **Fragmented governance:** more than a dozen federal bodies; weak regulator.

## 5. Way forward

1. **Grow demand on the grid:** marginal-cost tariffs for industry and electric vehicles, winter packages, electrification of heating and cooking.
2. **Finish IPP reform lawfully:** convert remaining take-or-pay to take-and-pay, re-profile Chinese IPP debt by agreement.
3. **Fix DISCOs:** privatise or give long management concessions; smart meters; feeder-level accountability.
4. **Open the market:** implement the competitive wholesale market (CTBCM) and allow wheeling at fair charges.
5. **Invest in transmission** (south–north lines) and battery storage to absorb solar.
6. **Shift to local sources:** hydro, Thar coal with emission controls, wind, solar, nuclear.
7. **Targeted subsidies** through BISP data in place of blanket cross-subsidies.
8. **Energy security plan** after the Hormuz shock: strategic petroleum reserves (currently about 20 days of cover **[verify]**), diversified LNG, Central Asian electricity (CASA-1000).

## 7. Model answer outline (Question 1)

- **Intro:** surplus capacity with unaffordable bills. Thesis: the crisis is one of contracts, governance and planning, not of megawatts.
- **Background:** 1994 and 2002 power policies; 2013–18 capacity drive; CPEC plants.
- **Causes:** seven headings above.
- **Diagram:** the circular-debt chain (consumer → DISCO → CPPA-G → IPP → fuel supplier → government), with leak points marked (see `09-Maps-and-Diagrams.md`, item 13).
- **Effects:** de-industrialisation, export competitiveness, fiscal cost, public anger.
- **Remedies:** eight points.
- **Conclusion:** the cheapest unit is the one sold on existing capacity; reform must raise use of the grid while cutting its fixed costs.
