## 1. Background

- **Constitutional position.** Article 243: the federal government has control and command of the armed forces; supreme command vests in the President. Article 244: officers swear to uphold the Constitution and not to take part in politics. Article 245: the forces defend the country and aid civil power when called. Article 6: abrogating the Constitution by force is high treason.
- **Practice.** Three coups (1958, 1977, 1999) and long periods of indirect influence. Courts validated takeovers under the "doctrine of necessity" (Dosso 1958, Nusrat Bhutto 1977, Zafar Ali Shah 2000) and later rejected it (Asma Jilani 1972; Sindh High Court Bar Association 2009).
- **Why the military became dominant (standard scholarly explanations):**
  1. A security threat from India from the first day, giving defence priority over institution-building.
  2. Weak political parties and early deaths of founding leaders.
  3. Cold War alliances that strengthened the army's organisation and budget.
  4. A large corporate and welfare economy run by military foundations (Ayesha Siddiqa, *Military Inc.*).
  5. Repeated invitations by civilians to intervene against rivals.
- **Key authors:** Hasan Askari Rizvi (*Military, State and Society in Pakistan*), Aqil Shah (*The Army and Democracy*), Ayesha Jalal (*The State of Martial Rule*), Stephen Cohen (*The Idea of Pakistan*), Shuja Nawaz (*Crossed Swords*).

## 3. The debate

| Supporters of the present arrangement say | Critics say |
|---|---|
| A unified command (CDF) fits modern multi-domain war, as May 2025 showed. Many states have a chief of defence. | The reform concentrates command in one service and one person, and was passed in days without debate. |
| Civil–military "harmony" delivered economic stabilisation, investment pledges and diplomatic gains. | Harmony means civilian subordination. Elected bodies have less say over security, foreign and economic policy. |
| Parliament passed the changes with a two-thirds majority. | The majority rests on a disputed election and a reversed court ruling; lifetime immunity conflicts with equality before law. |
| Security emergencies (TTP, BLA, India, Afghanistan, the Gulf) need quick decisions. | Militarised policy has not ended insurgency in KP and Balochistan; political solutions are missing. |

## 4. Challenges (analysis)

1. **Accountability gap.** Parliamentary oversight of defence spending and intelligence is thin.
2. **Mission expansion.** Economy (SIFC), mining, agriculture schemes, internal security, diplomacy.
3. **Erosion of civilian capacity.** When the military does the job, civilian bodies do not learn to.
4. **Politicisation risk for the forces.** Public controversy can harm cohesion and public standing.
5. **Federal strain.** Security-first policy in KP and Balochistan fuels grievance.
6. **Succession risk.** Long tenures and personalised arrangements make transitions uncertain.

## 5. Way forward

1. **Use the constitutional forums:** National Security Committee and the Cabinet Committee on Defence should meet on a schedule, with minutes.
2. **Strengthen parliamentary committees** on defence and national security with staff, in-camera budget scrutiny and intelligence oversight by law.
3. **Define SIFC's limits** by statute: a sunset clause, transparency of deals, audit by the Auditor General.
4. **Build civilian capacity:** a professional Ministry of Defence, police and civil armed forces able to lead internal security, and a foreign service that leads diplomacy.
5. **Political parties must stop seeking unelected support** against opponents. This needs an inter-party compact.
6. **Judicial clarity** on military trials of civilians and on immunity clauses.
7. **Gradualism.** Comparative cases (Türkiye before 2016, Indonesia after 1998, Chile) show civilian control grows through performance and consensus, not confrontation.

## 7. Model answer outline (Question 1)

- **Intro:** Articles 243–245 set civilian supremacy; practice differs. Thesis: imbalance has structural causes and can be corrected only by stronger civilian institutions and agreed rules.
- **Background:** three coups; doctrine of necessity; 2008–18 transfer of power; 2023–26 formalisation of the military's role.
- **Causes:** threat environment; weak parties; alliance history; military economy; civilian invitations.
- **Recent trends:** SIFC; tenure extension; 27th Amendment; diplomacy.
- **Diagram:** a spectrum line from "civilian control" to "military rule" with Pakistan's position by decade (1950s, 1977, 1990s, 2008–18, 2023–26).
- **Effects:** on democracy, federation, economy, foreign policy.
- **Counter-view:** efficiency and unity of command; reply with accountability.
- **Way forward:** seven points above.
- **Conclusion:** security and democracy are complementary when each institution works inside its constitutional field.
