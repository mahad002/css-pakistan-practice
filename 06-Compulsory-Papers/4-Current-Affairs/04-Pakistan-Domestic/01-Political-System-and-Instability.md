# Political System and Political Instability

> **As of:** 23 November 2026. **Syllabus:** Domestic affairs – political. **Past papers:** 2022 (design a political system), 2026 (multi-crisis state). Related: 2019 (governance).
> Political topics are contested. This note sets out the main positions. In the exam, argue from the Constitution and institutions, not for a party.

---

## 1. Background

- **System.** Federal parliamentary republic under the 1973 Constitution. Bicameral parliament: National Assembly (336 seats: 266 general, 60 for women, 10 for non-Muslims) and Senate (96 seats). The Prime Minister is chief executive; the President is head of state.
- **Record.** No prime minister has completed a five-year term. Direct military rule covered 1958–71, 1977–88 and 1999–2008. Assemblies completed full terms in 2013, 2018 and 2023, but with changes of prime minister inside each term.
- **Recent sequence.**
  - April 2022: Imran Khan removed by a vote of no confidence, the first successful one in Pakistan's history.
  - 9 May 2023: his arrest led to riots and attacks on military sites. Thousands were detained; some civilians were tried in military courts.
  - 5 August 2023: Khan jailed. He remains in Adiala Jail, Rawalpindi.
  - 8 February 2024: general election. PTI lost its election symbol, so its candidates ran as independents and won the most seats. PML-N and PPP formed a coalition under Shehbaz Sharif; Asif Ali Zardari became President. PTI says the result was rigged. The government and the Election Commission reject that.
  - October 2024 and November 2025: the 26th and 27th Amendments changed the judiciary and the military command (see file `03`).

## 4. Why instability persists (analysis)

1. **Civil–military imbalance.** Elected governments lack control over security and, increasingly, economic policy (file `02`).
2. **Contested elections.** Every election since 1977 except 1970 and arguably 2008 has been disputed. Losers do not accept results, so parliament lacks legitimacy.
3. **Weak parties.** Dynastic or personality-led, with little internal democracy. Parties invite unelected help against rivals.
4. **Zero-sum politics.** Accountability laws are used against whoever is out of power. Each party has been both victim and beneficiary.
5. **Institutional conflict.** Parliament, judiciary and executive contest each other's limits. The 26th and 27th Amendments settled this by reducing judicial power, which critics see as a new source of illegitimacy.
6. **Centre–province friction.** PTI in KP versus the centre; PPP's defence of provincial shares; Balochistan's alienation; unrest in AJK.
7. **Economic stress.** Austerity under the IMF narrows the room to buy consent.
8. **Polarised information space.** Social media mobilisation on one side; restrictions and prosecutions on the other.

**Costs.** Policy discontinuity, low investment (about 13–14% of GDP **[verify]**), brain drain, and weak response to terrorism because provinces and the centre do not coordinate.

## 5. Two readings of the present order

| View | Argument |
|---|---|
| **Government and supporters** | The "hybrid" arrangement brought economic stabilisation (reserves above $21bn, IMF reviews passed), foreign-policy gains in 2025–26 and firm action against 9 May violence. Constitutional changes were passed by parliament. |
| **Opposition, bar associations, rights groups** | The 2024 mandate was denied. Amendments were rushed, courts were restructured to suit the executive, the main opposition leader is jailed and a province's elected government has been removed. Stability is therefore shallow. |

## 6. Way forward

1. **A new political compact** (a "Charter of Democracy II") among all major parties on rules of the game: election conduct, accountability, and no use of unelected power against rivals.
2. **Electoral reform:** an Election Commission appointed by consensus, transparent results (publishing polling-station forms at once), and settled rules on party symbols and reserved seats.
3. **Dialogue with the opposition,** including on the fair trial and medical rights of imprisoned leaders. Courts, not street power or executive orders, should settle cases.
4. **Restore provincial self-government in KP** within the constitutional time limit and through the assembly.
5. **Empower parliament:** committee scrutiny, regular National Security Committee meetings, pre-budget debate.
6. **Local government** with constitutional protection, to widen the base of politics (file `06`).
7. **Internal party democracy** enforced through the Elections Act.
8. **An economic minimum agenda** shared across parties (tax, energy, privatisation) so that reforms outlast governments.

## 8. Model answer outline (Question 1)

- **Intro:** definition of political stability; one-line record (no PM has completed a term); thesis: instability comes from unsettled rules among institutions and parties, not from the parliamentary form.
- **Background:** 1958, 1977, 1999; 2008–18 transition; 2022–26 sequence.
- **Causes (headings as claims):** civil–military imbalance; disputed elections; weak parties; politicised accountability; judicial–executive conflict; federal tensions (KP, Balochistan, AJK); economic crisis.
- **Diagram:** a cycle: disputed election → weak government → reliance on unelected support → confrontation with opposition → crisis → next disputed election.
- **Costs:** economy, security, foreign policy credibility.
- **Counter-view:** the current order has delivered macro-stability and diplomatic gains; reply: durability needs consent.
- **Way forward:** the eight points above.
- **Conclusion:** stability is a product of agreed rules and fair competition.
