## Answer 1

**Q. Discuss the causes and consequences of the 2026 war on Iran for the Middle East and for global energy security. Critically evaluate Pakistan's role as a mediator.**

### Introduction
On 28 February 2026 the United States and Israel launched large-scale strikes on Iran while nuclear talks were still under way. Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei was killed, Iran struck Israel, American bases and several Gulf states, and the Strait of Hormuz was closed. Nine months later the region is in a state of "no war, no peace": large-scale fighting has paused, no settlement exists and the strait remains shut. Pakistan, a neighbour of Iran and a treaty partner of Saudi Arabia, emerged as the principal go-between in the spring of 2026. This answer argues that the war has broken the old regional balance without creating a new one, that it produced the gravest energy shock on record, and that Pakistan's mediation was a real diplomatic gain whose limits were set by its lack of leverage over either side's core demands.

### Background
The dispute dates from the 2015 nuclear deal, which the United States left in 2018. Iran then enriched uranium to 60 per cent. A twelve-day Israeli–Iranian war in June 2025 ended with American strikes on Fordow, Natanz and Isfahan. Sanctions returned, protests inside Iran were suppressed in January 2026, and Washington assembled its largest regional force since 2003.

### Causes of the war
**1. The nuclear question was unresolved.** Israel and the United States judged that Iran could rebuild its programme. They chose prevention over containment.

**2. Iran's regional shield had collapsed.** Hamas and Hezbollah were weakened after 2023–24 and the Assad government fell in December 2024. Iran looked vulnerable, which lowered the perceived cost of attack.

**3. Domestic politics on all sides.** An Israeli government facing elections, an American president who prizes decisive action, and an Iranian leadership that could not concede under pressure.

**4. Failure of diplomacy.** Talks mediated by Oman were under way when the strikes began, which destroyed trust for later rounds.

From a realist view, this was a preventive war by stronger powers against a weaker rival approaching a decisive capability. In law, most states saw no imminent armed attack that would justify force under Article 51 of the UN Charter.

### Consequences for the Middle East
**1. Leadership change in Tehran.** Mojtaba Khamenei succeeded his father. The system survived, hardened by war.

**2. The Gulf became a battlefield.** Iran hit Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Qatar, Kuwait and Bahrain. The image of the Gulf as a safe hub was damaged. Gulf states sought new protectors: Saudi Arabia invoked its 2025 defence agreement with Pakistan, and in August 2026 signed the Makkah defence pact with Pakistan and Türkiye.

**3. Wider fronts.** Fighting resumed in Lebanon, where Israel occupied parts of the south, and the Houthis attacked Saudi airports in the autumn.

**4. Political costs in the aggressor states.** Israel's October 2026 election left no bloc with a majority. In the United States, Democrats won both houses of Congress on 3 November 2026 amid anger over fuel prices.

**5. Institutional failure.** The Security Council was paralysed. The SCO condemned the attack; BRICS, with Iran and the UAE both inside, could only call for restraint.

### Consequences for global energy security
The Strait of Hormuz carries about a fifth of the world's oil and of its LNG trade. Its closure caused what observers called the largest supply disruption in the history of the oil market. Daily transits fell from about 130 ships to a handful. The IMF's October 2026 outlook assumes oil at about $96 a barrel for the year and world inflation of 4.7 per cent. Europe's gas benchmark briefly tripled in October. Qatar's Ras Laffan complex was struck. For importers such as Pakistan the effect was immediate: petrol reached Rs458 a litre in April, a four-day working week was ordered, inflation returned to about 10 per cent and the State Bank raised its policy rate to 12 per cent. The lesson drawn in many capitals is that domestic and renewable energy is a security asset, because sunlight cannot be blockaded.

Mark the narrow shipping lanes, Fujairah (UAE) outside the strait, and arrows for oil flowing to Asia and Europe. Add Gwadar to the east to show Pakistan's proximity.

### Pakistan's mediation: what happened
Pakistan condemned both the American–Israeli strikes and Iran's attacks on Gulf states. It offered Islamabad as a venue on 23 March, delivered an American fifteen-point plan to Tehran on 25 March and issued a five-point initiative with China on 31 March. A two-week ceasefire took effect on 8 April on Pakistan's proposal. On 11–12 April Vice President Vance, Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner met Foreign Minister Araghchi and Speaker Ghalibaf in Islamabad, the highest-level direct contact between the two states in decades. The talks failed, and a US naval blockade followed. A second effort produced the Islamabad Memorandum of 17 June, which suspended hostilities for sixty days. It collapsed in early July after attacks on shipping. Since August, Qatar and Oman have carried most of the mediation.

### Critical evaluation
**Strengths.** Pakistan had access to both capitals: trust in Tehran as a neighbour that opposed the strikes, and personal links to the Trump White House built in 2025. It does not recognise Israel, which reassured Iran. It had China's backing. It delivered two ceasefires that saved lives and gave markets relief. Its diplomatic standing rose sharply.

**Limits.** Pakistan could carry messages but could not change them. Iran demanded an end to the blockade and sanctions, reparations and a say over Hormuz. Washington demanded an end to enrichment. Israel was outside the process and distrusted the mediator. Pakistan's defence commitment to Saudi Arabia, and the arrival of Pakistani aircraft in the Kingdom in April, sat uneasily with neutrality. Oman, which borders the strait, was better placed for the technical talks.

**Risks.** A mediator who fails can be blamed by both sides. Domestic opinion is sharply anti-war: protesters died outside the US consulate in Karachi on 1 March. The economy bore heavy costs.

### Way forward
1. Keep the Islamabad process available and coordinate with Qatar and Oman instead of competing with them.
2. Press for a first-step deal: reopening of the strait in exchange for lifting the blockade, followed by nuclear talks with IAEA verification and phased sanctions relief.
3. Keep Saudi commitments strictly defensive and explain them to Tehran.
4. Propose, with China and Türkiye, a Gulf security dialogue that includes Iran.
5. At home, build strategic fuel reserves, speed up solar and local energy, and protect sectarian harmony.

### Conclusion
The 2026 war weakened Iran, exposed the Gulf, shook the oil market and showed that force could not produce a settlement. Pakistan's mediation proved that a middle power with relationships on all sides can open doors that great powers cannot. It also proved that opening a door is different from making the parties walk through it. Islamabad's interest lies in remaining a bridge, which requires that it stay out of the fighting and put its own economic house in order.

## Answer 2

**Q. Why has terrorism resurged in Pakistan since 2021? Critically evaluate Pakistan's policy towards Afghanistan, including the military campaign of 2026, and suggest a way forward.**

### Introduction
The Global Terrorism Index 2026 ranked Pakistan the country most affected by terrorism in the world, for the first time. It recorded 1,139 deaths and 1,045 attacks in 2025, the worst year since 2013, with the Tehreek-i-Taliban Pakistan (TTP) responsible for more than half the deaths. In 2026 the violence continued: September alone saw 225 attacks, the highest monthly figure in twelve years by one count. Since late February 2026 Pakistan has also been in what its Defence Minister called an "open war" with the Taliban government in Kabul. This answer argues that the resurgence has both external and internal causes, that air power has not removed the threat, and that only a combination of regional diplomacy, calibrated force and domestic reform can do so.

### Background
Military operations after the 2014 Army Public School attack, together with the National Action Plan, reduced terrorism deaths by more than 80 per cent by 2019. The trend reversed when the Afghan Taliban returned to power in August 2021. A ceasefire with the TTP brokered by Kabul in 2021–22 allowed fighters to resettle in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and then collapsed.

### Causes of the resurgence
**1. Sanctuary across the border.** UN monitors estimate that 6,000 to 6,500 TTP fighters are based in Afghanistan. The Afghan Taliban share ideology and wartime bonds with them and have not acted against them.

**2. Better weapons.** Arms left behind in 2021, including night-vision equipment, reached militants.

**3. An unfinished National Action Plan.** Points on madrassa reform, terror financing, criminal justice and a strong NACTA were never completed.

**4. Neglect of the merged districts.** The development funds and civil institutions promised at the 2018 merger did not arrive.

**5. Balochistan's political grievance.** Baloch separatist groups carried out mass-casualty attacks, including the bombing of a military train in Quetta in May 2026. Enforced disappearances and disputed elections feed recruitment.

**6. A third actor.** Islamic State Khorasan claimed the Islamabad mosque bombing of 6 February 2026, which killed 31 worshippers.

**7. Division at home.** The federal government and the provincial government in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa disagreed on military operations. Governor's rule was imposed on the province in October 2026.

**8. Weak policing and prosecution,** which leave the army as the first responder.

### Pakistan's Afghan policy since 2021
**Phase one: engagement.** Islamabad urged the world to work with the Taliban and expected cooperation on the TTP.

**Phase two: pressure.** From 2023 Pakistan expelled undocumented Afghans, tightened transit trade and carried out occasional strikes. More than 2.6 million Afghans have returned since September 2023.

**Phase three: force.** In October 2025 the heaviest border fighting in years ended with a ceasefire signed in Doha under Qatari and Turkish mediation. Talks in Istanbul failed. After the Islamabad bombing, Pakistan struck camps on 21–22 February 2026; the Taliban attacked border posts; Pakistan hit Kabul and Kandahar and launched Operation Ghazab lil-Haq. Truces were observed at both Eids and China hosted talks in Urumqi in April. Strikes resumed in June, September and October after major attacks.

Mark Torkham and Chaman crossings, Peshawar, Quetta, Kabul, Kandahar, Khost and Nangarhar, and shade the former tribal districts. Add arrows for militant infiltration and a symbol for the strikes on Kabul.

### Critical evaluation of the 2026 campaign
**The case for it.** A state has the right to defend itself against attacks launched from foreign soil when the host is unwilling or unable to act. Diplomacy had been tried for four years. The strikes imposed a cost on Kabul and showed resolve.

**The case against relying on it.**
1. *It has not reduced attacks.* The third quarter of 2026 saw violence-related deaths rise by 83 per cent over the previous quarter.
2. *Civilian harm.* UNAMA counted at least 358 Afghan civilians killed and about 115,000 displaced. A strike on a Kabul rehabilitation centre in March, whose details are disputed, damaged Pakistan's standing.
3. *It unites Afghans behind the Taliban* and helps militant recruitment.
4. *Economic self-harm.* Closing Torkham and Chaman cut a billion-dollar export market and the route to Central Asia.
5. *Diplomatic costs.* Kabul turned to India, Russia, which recognised it in 2025, and Iran.
6. *The Taliban's dilemma is real.* Acting against the TTP risks driving its fighters to Islamic State.

The honest conclusion is that force can punish but cannot, by itself, change the Taliban's calculation or fix the weaknesses inside Pakistan.

### Way forward
**External**
1. A verification mechanism guaranteed by third parties (China, Qatar, Türkiye, Saudi Arabia): relocation of TTP fighters away from the border, joint monitoring and intelligence exchange.
2. Use the SCO chair in 2026–27 to build a common regional position that ties recognition, trade and aid to counter-terrorism action.
3. Keep trade and transit open and separate from security disputes.
4. Limit strikes to clearly identified militant targets away from cities.
5. A humane, phased refugee policy.

**Internal**
6. A revised National Action Plan owned by all parties and provinces, with NACTA as the hub.
7. Police first: strengthen counter-terrorism departments in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and Balochistan.
8. Deliver the merged districts' development package and local governments.
9. Open a political track in Balochistan.
10. Reform prosecution and courts so that convictions do not depend on military trials.

### Conclusion
Terrorism returned to Pakistan because a hostile sanctuary met an unreformed home front. The policy of the past year has leaned heavily on air power. It has shown resolve but not results. Security will come when Kabul faces united regional pressure and real incentives, and when the Pakistani state is present in its border regions as a provider of justice and services, not only as a striking force.

## Answer 3

**Q. Has the May 2025 conflict changed the rules of deterrence in South Asia? Evaluate the prospects for peace between Pakistan and India.**

### Introduction
In May 2025 India and Pakistan fought their most serious military exchange since 1971. It lasted four days, was fought mainly with missiles, drones and long-range air combat, and reached targets deep inside both countries before a ceasefire on 10 May. Eighteen months later there is no dialogue, the Indus Waters Treaty is held "in abeyance" by India, and both arsenals are growing. This answer argues that nuclear deterrence still prevents all-out war, but that the conflict lowered the threshold for conventional strikes, shortened decision time and added water to the list of core disputes. Peace is therefore more necessary and, for now, less likely.

### Background
The two states have fought over Kashmir since 1947. Since their 1998 nuclear tests each crisis (Kargil 1999, the 2001–02 standoff, Mumbai 2008, Pulwama–Balakot 2019) has tested how much force can be used under the nuclear shadow. India's revocation of Kashmir's special status in August 2019 froze relations.

### What happened in May 2025
On 22 April gunmen killed 26 civilians at Pahalgam. India blamed Pakistan-based militants; Pakistan denied involvement and offered a neutral inquiry. India suspended the water treaty and closed the border. On 7 May it struck nine sites in Punjab and Azad Kashmir. Pakistan said it shot down several Indian aircraft, a claim India partly conceded without numbers. After drone exchanges, India hit Pakistani air bases on 10 May and Pakistan struck Indian bases in Operation Bunyan-um-Marsoos. A ceasefire followed that afternoon. President Trump claimed mediation; India insists the truce was arranged between the two armies.

### How the rules of deterrence changed
**1. The geographic limit fell.** In 2019 India struck one site in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa. In 2025 it struck Punjab and air bases near Rawalpindi, close to nuclear command facilities.

**2. India declared a "new normal".** Any major terrorist attack will be treated as an act of war, and no distinction will be drawn between militants and their alleged state sponsors. This removes the pause for investigation and diplomacy.

**3. Pakistan demonstrated conventional denial.** Its air force, using Chinese J-10C aircraft and PL-15 missiles, imposed losses. Islamabad concluded that it can deter with conventional means as well as nuclear ones, which in theory raises its own nuclear threshold.

**4. New weapons compress time.** Drones, cruise missiles and stand-off munitions allow strikes without crossing borders. Warning times are measured in minutes.

**5. The stability–instability paradox deepened.** Because both sides believe nuclear weapons prevent total war, each is more willing to fight below that level.

**6. Third parties returned.** The ceasefire came after American intervention, which contradicts India's doctrine of bilateralism and encourages both sides to play to outside audiences.

**7. Water became a weapon.** Suspension of a treaty that had survived three wars signalled that no agreement is safe.

Rungs from the bottom: (1) terrorist attack; (2) diplomatic and economic measures; (3) fire across the LoC; (4) limited air strike (2019); (5) missile and drone strikes on the mainland and air bases (2025); (6) large-scale conventional war; (7) tactical nuclear use; (8) strategic exchange. Mark where 2019 and 2025 stopped, and write "shrinking gap" between rungs 5 and 7.

### Counter-view: deterrence worked
Some analysts argue that the crisis proves stability. The fighting stopped in four days, neither side sent ground forces across the border, and outside powers intervened precisely because nuclear weapons exist. This is true as far as it goes. But stopping at rung five once does not guarantee stopping there next time, especially when each side believes it won.

### Prospects for peace: the obstacles
1. **No channel.** High commissioners are absent; only the military hotline works.
2. **Kashmir.** India treats the question as closed; Pakistan cannot accept that.
3. **Water.** The Court of Arbitration ruled on 31 August 2026 that "abeyance" has no legal basis and the treaty remains in force. India rejects the award.
4. **Terrorism and proxies.** Each accuses the other: India over Lashkar-e-Taiba and Jaish-e-Mohammad, Pakistan over the BLA and TTP.
5. **Domestic politics and media** reward hard lines in both countries.
6. **Arms racing.** SIPRI's 2026 estimates give India about 190 warheads and Pakistan about 170, with India reported to keep some mated to missiles in peacetime.
7. **Alignments.** India's partnerships with the United States, France and Israel; Pakistan's with China, and since 2025–26 with Saudi Arabia and Türkiye.

### Prospects for peace: the openings
1. **Quiet contacts exist.** A handshake between senior figures in Dhaka at the end of 2025, track-two meetings in Colombo and Bangkok, and reported back-channel talks in several capitals during 2026.
2. **Both economies need calm.** Each wants investment and growth.
3. **Legal rulings** give India a face-saving route back to technical water talks.
4. **Shared threats:** smog, floods, heat.
5. **Multilateral rooms:** both sit in the SCO, which Pakistan chairs in 2026–27.

### Way forward
1. **Crisis management first.** Restore envoys; create a political hotline between national security advisers; reaffirm the 2021 Line of Control ceasefire.
2. **Nuclear risk reduction.** Extend the 1988 non-attack agreement to command and control sites; pre-notify cruise missile tests; agree rules on drones.
3. **Water.** Resume the Permanent Indus Commission and data-sharing while legal positions are reserved.
4. **Terrorism.** A joint or neutral mechanism to investigate attacks; visible action by Pakistan against proscribed groups under its own law.
5. **Kashmir.** Press for restoration of statehood and rights; keep alive the 2004–07 back-channel ideas for a later day.
6. **Small steps.** Kartarpur, pilgrim visas, release of fishermen and prisoners, limited trade in medicines and food.

### Conclusion
May 2025 did not overturn nuclear deterrence. It moved the line beneath it. South Asia now has two nuclear neighbours willing to exchange missile strikes on each other's heartland and a doctrine on one side that makes the next exchange nearly automatic. Peace in the full sense is distant. The realistic goal for the coming years is managed rivalry: channels that work in a crisis, restraint agreements suited to new weapons, and the patient rebuilding of minimum trust. That goal is modest. Given what the alternative has become, it is also urgent.

## Answer 4

**Q. Evaluate the legal and strategic implications of India's decision to hold the Indus Waters Treaty "in abeyance". What options does Pakistan have?**

### Introduction
The Indus Waters Treaty of 1960 is often described as the most successful water-sharing agreement in the world. It survived the wars of 1965, 1971 and 1999. On 23 April 2025, the day after the Pahalgam attack, India announced that it would hold the treaty "in abeyance" until Pakistan ended support for cross-border terrorism. On 31 August 2026 a Court of Arbitration sitting in The Hague ruled unanimously that this step has no basis in the treaty or in international law and that the treaty remains fully in force. India rejects the ruling. This answer examines the legal position, the strategic meaning of India's move and the choices before Pakistan. It argues that Pakistan holds the stronger legal case but that law alone will not secure its water: it needs diplomacy abroad and reform at home.

### Background
The treaty, signed in Karachi by Nehru and Ayub Khan with the World Bank as broker, gave the three eastern rivers (Ravi, Beas, Sutlej) to India and the three western rivers (Indus, Jhelum, Chenab) to Pakistan. India may use the western rivers for domestic needs, limited agriculture and run-of-river hydropower within design limits. A Permanent Indus Commission handles routine matters. Disputes go to a Neutral Expert or a Court of Arbitration. The treaty has no exit clause: Article XII allows change only by a new treaty agreed by both.

### The legal implications
**1. Unilateral suspension is not provided for.** The treaty contains no clause on suspension or abeyance. The 2026 award confirmed that the term has no technical meaning in international law.

**2. The law of treaties offers India little.** Under the Vienna Convention, a treaty may be suspended for material breach of that treaty or a fundamental change of circumstances. Alleged support for terrorism is not a breach of a water treaty, and the tribunal did not accept changed circumstances.

**3. Customary law protects the lower riparian.** The principles of equitable use and of not causing significant harm apply even outside the treaty.

**4. The enforcement gap.** India calls the Court illegally constituted and has stopped taking part in the Neutral Expert process, which continues without it and is expected to conclude around July 2027. International awards cannot be enforced by force. Their value lies in legitimacy, in shaping the positions of the World Bank and other states, and in raising the reputational cost of defiance.

**5. A precedent for others.** India is itself a lower riparian to China on the Brahmaputra and an upper riparian to Bangladesh on the Ganges. A norm that treaties can be shelved at will could be used against it.

### The strategic implications
**1. Water has been securitised.** India has linked a technical treaty to its terrorism demands, giving itself a new lever.

**2. Near-term physical limits.** India lacks the storage to stop the western rivers. It cannot "turn off the tap" in the coming years.

**3. Near-term real risks.** India can change the timing of flows by flushing and refilling reservoirs, and it can withhold flood and flow data. Both hurt Pakistan at sowing time and in the monsoon. The 2025 floods showed the danger of missing warnings.

**4. Long-term risk.** Free of treaty design limits, India could build storage and diversion works on the Chenab and Jhelum over a decade or more.

**5. Escalation.** Pakistan has said that diversion of its water would be an act of war. A country in which agriculture uses over 90 per cent of water and employs more than a third of workers cannot treat the matter as routine.

**6. Internal effect.** Scarcity sharpens disputes between Punjab and Sindh, as the 2025 protests over new canals showed.

Add the Jhelum and Chenab joining from the east (label "western rivers: Pakistan"), and the Ravi, Beas and Sutlej further east (label "eastern rivers: India"). Mark the Line of Control, Kishenganga and Ratle projects, Baglihar, Tarbela and Mangla dams, and Sukkur and Kotri barrages.

### Pakistan's own water weakness
The treaty dispute lands on a fragile system. Per capita availability has fallen below the scarcity line of 1,000 cubic metres. Storage covers roughly a month of flow. Canal losses are high, water is nearly free, groundwater is unregulated and water-hungry crops dominate. A country that manages its own water poorly magnifies every external shock.

### Options for Pakistan
**A. Legal and diplomatic**
1. Build on the award. Seek statements of support for the treaty from the World Bank, the UN Secretary-General, the OIC, China and others. Raise non-compliance in the General Assembly.
2. See the Neutral Expert process through and keep a record of every deviation in flows.
3. Consider an advisory opinion from the International Court of Justice on the obligations of upper riparians.
4. Offer technical talks without prejudice: resumption of the Permanent Indus Commission, data-sharing and climate adaptation under the treaty.

**B. Strategic**
5. Keep water out of the nuclear rhetoric while making clear that physical diversion would cross a red line.
6. Work with China and Bangladesh on shared norms for transboundary rivers.
7. Open talks with Kabul, when possible, on the Kabul River to avoid a second front.

**C. Domestic (the most important)**
8. Conservation: lining of canals, drip and sprinkler systems, laser levelling, crop zoning.
9. Storage at every scale: complete Mohmand and Diamer-Bhasha, build small dams and recharge aquifers with floodwater.
10. Price water and license groundwater.
11. Install telemetry on all barrages to rebuild trust between provinces under the 1991 Accord.
12. A flood early-warning system that does not depend on Indian data: radar, satellites and upstream gauges in Azad Kashmir.

### Critical note
Some in India argue that the treaty is outdated, gives Pakistan about 80 per cent of the system's water and ignores climate change and India's energy needs. A reasonable response is that the treaty itself provides for modification by mutual consent. Pakistan can agree to discuss adaptation inside the treaty. What it cannot accept is renegotiation under coercion.

### Conclusion
India's "abeyance" is unlawful, as an international tribunal has now said. It is also a strategic signal that water will be used as leverage. Pakistan's answer should have three parts: hold the legal high ground and widen international support, keep a door open for technical cooperation, and above all repair its own water management so that the country is less exposed to any upstream decision. The treaty protected Pakistan for sixty-five years. The next sixty-five will depend as much on what Pakistan does with its water as on what India does with its dams.

## Answer 5

**Q. Why does Pakistan's economic stability remain fragile despite repeated IMF programmes? Suggest structural reforms for sustainable growth.**

### Introduction
By the usual measures Pakistan's economy is more stable in late 2026 than at any time since the near-default of 2023. The State Bank's reserves stand above $21 billion, four IMF reviews have been cleared, remittances reached a record $41.6 billion in the last fiscal year and the budget shows a primary surplus. Yet growth was only 3.7 per cent, poverty rose to 28.9 per cent, inflation climbed back to about 10 per cent after the Gulf oil shock and the policy rate was raised to 12 per cent in October. Pakistan is in at least its twenty-fourth IMF programme. This answer argues that stability is fragile because programmes treat the symptoms (deficits and reserves) while the causes (a narrow tax base, low exports, low investment, a costly energy system and weak institutions) remain. It then proposes reforms.

### Background: the boom–bust pattern
Pakistan's growth is driven by consumption and imports. Whenever growth passes about 5 per cent, imports surge, reserves fall and the rupee comes under pressure. The government then turns to the IMF, compresses demand and stabilises, until the next push for growth repeats the cycle. This has happened in 2008, 2013, 2018 and 2022–23.

### Why stability is fragile
**1. A narrow and unfair tax system.** Tax revenue is roughly a tenth of GDP. Retail, real estate and agriculture pay little. Salaried workers and documented industry carry the burden. Heavy reliance on indirect taxes hurts the poor.

**2. Debt that feeds on itself.** Public debt was Rs86.7 trillion at end-June 2026, about 68 per cent of GDP. Interest payments of about Rs8.1 trillion take over 42 per cent of the federal budget and exceed the federation's net revenue after provincial transfers. New loans repay old ones.

**3. Exports that do not grow.** Goods exports have stayed near $30–32 billion for over a decade, dominated by low-value textiles and a few markets. The current account returned to deficit in FY26 as soon as imports recovered.

**4. Dependence on remittances.** They finance the trade gap but are exposed to events in the Gulf, as the 2026 war showed.

**5. Low savings and investment.** Investment is about 13–14 per cent of GDP, roughly half the regional norm. Policy reversals and slow courts deter investors.

**6. An energy system that taxes production.** Capacity payments, circular debt of about Rs1.6–1.7 trillion and high tariffs make industry uncompetitive.

**7. Loss-making state enterprises.** The sale of PIA in December 2025 was a first step; power distributors are next.

**8. Weak human capital.** Education and health spending are each below 1 per cent of GDP, and 20 to 26 million children are out of school.

**9. Exposure to shocks.** Floods in 2022 and 2025, US tariffs, and the 2026 oil price spike each knocked growth off target.

**10. Political instability and elite capture.** Reforms that hurt powerful groups are diluted; governments rarely last long enough to see them through.

In the centre write "root causes: low tax, low exports, low investment, costly energy".

### Have IMF programmes helped or harmed?
**Helped:** they averted default in 2023, rebuilt reserves about five-fold, cut inflation from 38 per cent and imposed fiscal discipline, including a pact that made provinces tax agricultural income.

**Limits:** programmes focus on short-term targets. Revenue has been raised by taxing those already in the net and by energy price increases, which slow growth and raise poverty. Structural benchmarks are often met on paper. The deeper fault lies less with the lender than with the borrower's failure to use the breathing space.

### Structural reforms
**Fiscal**
1. Broaden the base: enforce agricultural income tax, value property at market rates, bring retail and wholesale into the net with digital tools, and remove exemptions.
2. Restructure spending: close duplicate ministries after devolution, target subsidies through BISP data, and agree a fiscal pact with provinces.
3. Manage debt actively: lengthen maturities, swap costly debt, use debt-for-climate arrangements.

**External**
4. An export-led strategy: lower tariffs on inputs, competitive energy for exporters, diversification into IT services (about $4 billion and growing fast), engineering goods, processed food and minerals.
5. Productive use of remittances: diaspora bonds and investment vehicles.
6. Foreign investment through predictable rules and contract enforcement, with the SIFC acting as a lawful one-window facilitator.

**Real economy**
7. Finish energy reform: privatise or concession distributors, open the wholesale market, invest in transmission, shift to local and renewable sources.
8. Agriculture: seed, water efficiency, mechanisation and markets to raise yields.
9. Privatise or restructure state enterprises transparently.

**People and institutions**
10. Raise education and health spending in steps; expand skills training.
11. Elected local governments to deliver services.
12. A cross-party charter of the economy so that core reforms survive changes of government.
13. Plan the exit from the IMF in 2027 with a home-grown programme approved by parliament.

### Critical note
Some argue that growth must come first and that austerity is self-defeating. The experience of 2017–18 and 2021–22 shows that growth without reform ends in crisis within two years. Others argue that reform is politically impossible. Yet the tax on agricultural income, the sale of PIA and the renegotiation of power contracts show that steps once thought impossible can be taken when the alternative is default.

### Conclusion
Pakistan has achieved stabilisation without transformation. Reserves and ratings have improved, but the engine has not been rebuilt, so every external shock threatens a return to crisis. Lasting stability requires the state to tax fairly, spend wisely, sell abroad more than it buys, and invest in its people. None of this is technically difficult. All of it requires political agreement. The current programme ends in 2027; whether it is the last will depend on choices made before then.

## Answer 6

**Q. Critically evaluate the Saudi–Pakistan Strategic Mutual Defence Agreement (2025) and the Makkah Joint Defence Agreement (2026). Are they an opportunity or an entanglement for Pakistan?**

### Introduction
Within eleven months Pakistan signed two defence treaties of a kind it had avoided for half a century. On 17 September 2025 it concluded a Strategic Mutual Defence Agreement (SMDA) with Saudi Arabia, under which aggression against one is treated as aggression against both. On 7 August 2026, in Makkah, the two states and Türkiye signed a joint defence agreement whose second article declares an external armed attack on any party to be an attack on all. It entered into force on 14 November 2026. These pacts raise Pakistan's standing and promise economic and military benefits. They also tie it to one of the world's most volatile regions at a time when it faces India in the east and a war with Afghanistan in the west. This answer argues that the pacts are an opportunity only if Pakistan keeps them defensive, transparent and compatible with its role as a bridge to Iran.

### Background
Pakistan and Saudi Arabia have cooperated militarily since the 1960s. Pakistani troops were stationed in the Kingdom in the 1980s, and Riyadh supported Pakistan financially after the 1998 nuclear tests and again in 2018–23. But in April 2015 Pakistan's parliament voted to stay out of the Yemen war, and neutrality between Riyadh and Tehran became settled policy. Pakistan left SEATO and CENTO in the 1970s after learning that alliances do not guarantee support.

### Why the pacts came about
**1. Doubts about American protection.** Israel's strike on Hamas leaders in Doha on 9 September 2025, in a state hosting a major US base, shocked the Gulf. The SMDA was signed eight days later.

**2. The 2026 Iran war.** Iran struck Saudi territory. Riyadh sought partners with real armies.

**3. Pakistan's raised profile.** Its performance in the May 2025 conflict with India made it a credible security provider.

**4. Saudi–Turkish reconciliation** made a three-way arrangement possible.

**5. Pakistan's needs:** investment, oil on credit, deposits and diplomatic weight against India.

In realist terms this is external balancing: states under threat pool their capabilities.

### What the agreements provide
The SMDA is a bilateral mutual-defence commitment that, by most accounts, does not require automatic military intervention. The Makkah agreement goes further in form. It refers to Article 51 of the UN Charter, sets up a secretariat in Saudi Arabia with a Pakistani as first secretary-general, creates a Strategic Political and Defence Committee that meets twice a year, allows new members by consensus and permits withdrawal only after ten years. It says nothing public about nuclear weapons. The pact was reportedly invoked on 5 October 2026 after Houthi attacks on Saudi Arabia; Türkiye limited itself to defensive and technical support, and Pakistan had said a month earlier that it was not considering an immediate military response. Pakistani aircraft had already been sent to the Kingdom in April 2026.

> **Fig. 1 (map to draw):** A sketch from the eastern Mediterranean to the Indus. Mark Türkiye, Saudi Arabia and Pakistan with a triangle joining Ankara, Riyadh and Islamabad. Shade Iran in the middle, and mark Yemen, the Strait of Hormuz, Israel and India. Label the triangle "Makkah Defence Agreement, 7 Aug 2026".

### The opportunity
**1. Strategic weight.** Pakistan becomes a pillar of security for the custodian of Islam's holiest sites and the leading Arab economy.

**2. Deterrence.** India must now consider the reactions of Riyadh and Ankara. New Delhi's statement that it expects Saudi Arabia to respect its "sensitivities" shows the point was noticed.

**3. Economic return.** A security partner has a stronger claim on investment, oil facilities and jobs for the 2.5 million or more Pakistanis in the Kingdom.

**4. Defence industry.** Saudi finance, Turkish technology and Pakistani manpower and experience could produce drones, ships and aircraft together.

**5. A hedge.** As American guarantees look less certain, a network of Muslim middle powers offers insurance.

**6. Leadership.** The pact is open to others; Bangladesh and Syria have shown interest. It could become the core of a wider framework that the OIC never provided.

### The entanglement
**1. Other people's wars.** Saudi Arabia's main threats come from Yemen and Iran. A treaty duty could draw Pakistani forces into exactly the conflict parliament refused in 2015.

**2. The Iran relationship.** Pakistan shares a 909-kilometre border with Iran and mediated between Tehran and Washington in 2026. A mediator who is also the ally of one side is trusted less.

**3. Sectarian peace at home.** A sizeable Shia minority and the memory of the 1980s and 1990s make any Saudi–Iranian clash dangerous inside Pakistan.

**4. Over-stretch.** The army is engaged against the TTP and Baloch insurgents, deployed on the Indian border and fighting along the Durand Line.

**5. Vague obligations.** Analysts describe the pact as a framework, not a NATO. There is no integrated command. Ambiguity may avoid automatic war, but it also invites misreading by allies and adversaries.

**6. The nuclear question.** Any impression that Pakistan's deterrent covers the Gulf would bring pressure from the United States and others and complicate its India-specific doctrine.

**7. Democratic deficit.** Both pacts were concluded by the executive with little parliamentary debate.

### Assessment
The benefits are real but mostly potential: they depend on Pakistan converting status into investment and technology. The risks are immediate, as the October invocation showed. The decisive variable is how Pakistan defines its commitment. A pledge to help defend Saudi territory, including the holy sites, against external attack is defensible and popular. Participation in offensive operations in Yemen or against Iran would be neither.

### Way forward
1. State publicly that the pacts are defensive and territorial. Lay the texts before parliament and create an oversight committee.
2. Keep Tehran informed; propose a regional security dialogue that includes Iran, building on Pakistan's 2026 mediation.
3. Limit deployments to air defence, training and protection roles, with clear rules of engagement.
4. Reaffirm that Pakistan's nuclear posture is unchanged.
5. Use the committee structure to agree joint production projects and a timetable for Saudi investment.
6. Coordinate with Türkiye so that the alliance's political voice supports mediation in the Gulf.
7. Do not let Gulf commitments reduce attention to the home front.

### Conclusion
The pacts mark Pakistan's return to alliance politics after five decades. They can be an opportunity: a source of weight, resources and partners. They become an entanglement the moment they pull Pakistan into the region's rivalries as a combatant. The line between the two runs through the word "defensive". Pakistan's leaders should hold that line in the text, in deployments and in public.

## Answer 7

**Q. Discuss the achievements and failures of the Conferences of the Parties (COPs), with special reference to COP30 and COP31. What do they mean for a climate-vulnerable country such as Pakistan?**

### Introduction
For three decades the world's governments have met each year under the UN Framework Convention on Climate Change to negotiate their response to global warming. The process has produced a universal treaty, national pledges from almost every state and a fund for loss and damage. It has not stopped emissions from rising. COP30 in Belém, Brazil (November 2025) agreed to triple adaptation finance but could not agree a plan to leave fossil fuels. COP31 in Antalya, Türkiye, ended on 22 November 2026 without an overall decision, the first such failure since Copenhagen in 2009. For Pakistan, which emits under 1 per cent of greenhouse gases and lost over a thousand lives to floods in 2025, the gap between promise and delivery is a matter of survival. This answer assesses the record, explains the two latest conferences and draws out the implications.

### Background: how the regime evolved
The Convention was signed at Rio in 1992 and rests on "common but differentiated responsibilities". The Kyoto Protocol (1997) bound only developed countries; the United States never ratified it. Copenhagen (2009) failed to produce a treaty but set the 2°C goal and the promise of $100 billion a year. The Paris Agreement (2015) changed the model: every country submits a Nationally Determined Contribution, reviewed every five years, to hold warming well below 2°C and pursue 1.5°C.

### Achievements of the COP process
**1. A universal legal framework.** Almost every state is party to Paris and has a climate target.

**2. A lower warming path.** Before Paris the world was heading for about 4°C. Current pledges point to between 2.5 and 3°C: still dangerous, but better.

**3. A signal to markets.** Solar and wind are now the cheapest new power in most countries. COP28 (Dubai, 2023) called for "transitioning away from fossil fuels" and for tripling renewables by 2030.

**4. Recognition of loss and damage.** COP27 (2022) agreed a fund after Pakistan, then chair of the G77, pressed the case following its floods.

**5. A finance goal.** COP29 (Baku, 2024) set a target of $300 billion a year by 2035 from developed countries, within an aspiration of $1.3 trillion.

**6. Rules and transparency:** carbon markets, an adaptation goal and regular stocktakes.

### Failures
**1. Emissions are still at record levels;** the 1.5°C limit will be breached at least temporarily.

**2. Finance falls short.** The $100 billion was delivered two years late and largely as loans. The loss and damage fund holds a tiny fraction of the need.

**3. No enforcement.** Pledges are voluntary.

**4. Consensus rule.** A handful of producers can block.

**5. Unreliable great powers.** The United States has left Paris twice.

**6. Process over substance.** Ever larger meetings, modest outcomes.

### COP30, Belém (2025)
Held in the Amazon ten years after Paris, COP30 was billed as the "implementation COP". It agreed a call to triple adaptation finance by 2035, a "Baku to Belém Roadmap" towards $1.3 trillion, a Global Implementation Accelerator, indicators for adaptation and a just-transition mechanism. A formal roadmap away from fossil fuels, supported by more than eighty countries, was blocked. Brazil promised to prepare voluntary roadmaps on fossil fuels and deforestation outside the negotiated text. The United States sent no federal delegation.

### COP31, Antalya (2026)
Türkiye hosted and presided, while Australia led the negotiations under a compromise between the two bidders. The talks became the longest on record. An early draft called for an immediate transition away from fossil fuels in energy systems. After objections from several oil-producing states the language was removed, and the European Union and others refused to accept a text that did not mention fossil fuels at all. Disputes over finance and carbon capture compounded the deadlock. Narrower decisions survived, on the just-transition mechanism and on reviewing climate finance. The dispute now passes to COP32 in Addis Ababa. (These details come from first-day press reports and should be checked against the final UN texts.)

> **Fig. 1 (diagram to draw):** A timeline arrow: Rio 1992 (UNFCCC) → Kyoto 1997 → Copenhagen 2009 → Paris 2015 → Sharm el-Sheikh 2022 (loss and damage) → Dubai 2023 (transition away) → Baku 2024 ($300bn) → Belém 2025 (triple adaptation) → Antalya 2026 (no deal) → Addis Ababa 2027. Above the arrow sketch a rising line labelled "global emissions".

### Why COP31 failed: an analysis
The collapse reflects the wider world of 2026. A war in the Gulf pushed energy security above climate on most agendas and enriched producers. The largest historical emitter was absent. Trust between North and South was low after years of unmet finance promises. And the consensus rule meant that the more ambitious the majority became, the more certain a veto was. In the language of international relations, the liberal promise of cooperation through institutions ran into the realist fact of conflicting national interests.

### Implications for Pakistan
**1. More risk.** Germanwatch ranked Pakistan first in the world for climate losses in 2022 and fifteenth for the three decades to 2024. The 2025 floods killed over a thousand people and cost about $2.9 billion by the government's estimate.

**2. Less money than promised.** Pakistan needs about $348 billion by 2030, according to the World Bank. Reconstruction pledges after 2022 were mostly loans.

**3. Self-reliance is unavoidable.** Adaptation cannot wait for global consensus.

**4. A diplomatic role.** Pakistan sits on the board of the loss and damage fund and has credibility as a victim.

**5. An opportunity in energy.** The collapse of solar prices has already produced a boom at home; the 2026 oil shock strengthens the case.

### Way forward
**Global**
1. Coalitions of the willing on fossil-fuel transition when consensus fails.
2. Reform of procedure: majority voting as a last resort.
3. Grants and debt relief, not loans, for adaptation and loss and damage; new levies on shipping, aviation and windfall profits.

**Pakistan**
4. Build bankable projects to draw on the adaptation and loss and damage funds and the IMF's resilience facility.
5. Enforce floodplain zoning, restore wetlands and invest in early warning down to the village.
6. Empower local governments for disaster response.
7. Climate-smart agriculture and water storage.
8. Lead a bloc of vulnerable states with clear, shared demands before COP32.

### Conclusion
The COPs have given the world a framework, a direction and a vocabulary of justice. They have not delivered the speed or the money that science and fairness require, and Antalya showed how fragile the process has become. For Pakistan the lesson is double. It must keep pressing the polluters to pay, because the claim is just. And it must protect its own people as if no help were coming, because for now very little is.

## Answer 8

**Q. Is the world moving from a unipolar to a multipolar order? Discuss with reference to US–China rivalry and assess the implications for Pakistan.**

### Introduction
On 19 November 2026 the presidents of the United States, China and Russia met together on the sidelines of the APEC summit in Shenzhen. Three days later the UN climate conference collapsed without agreement, and for most of the year the Security Council had been unable to act on a major war in the Gulf. These events capture the present order: several great powers bargaining directly, common institutions weakening, and middle powers such as Pakistan, Qatar and Türkiye stepping in as mediators and alliance-builders. This answer argues that the unipolar era has ended and that a multipolar order is taking shape, but one with fewer shared rules. For Pakistan this brings room for manoeuvre and greater danger at once.

### Concepts
Polarity refers to the number of great powers in the system. The Cold War was bipolar. After the Soviet collapse in 1991 the United States enjoyed what Charles Krauthammer called a "unipolar moment". Multipolarity means several centres of power. It should not be confused with multilateralism, which means cooperation through agreed rules. A world can be multipolar and lawless.

### Evidence that unipolarity has ended
**1. Economic weight has shifted.** China is the largest economy at purchasing power parity and the main trading partner of over 120 countries. The BRICS group, now eleven members, exceeds the G7 on that measure.

**2. Military competition.** China's nuclear arsenal has passed 600 warheads and is growing; its navy is the world's largest by number of ships. Russia went to war in Europe in defiance of Western sanctions.

**3. The United States acts as one great power among several.** Under "America First" it has imposed tariffs on allies and rivals, withdrawn from the World Health Organization and the Paris Agreement, struck Iran without UN or congressional authority, seized Venezuela's president in January 2026 and pressed Denmark over Greenland. It negotiates spheres of interest with Moscow and Beijing.

**4. Alternative institutions.** The BRICS bank, the Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank, the SCO and the Belt and Road Initiative offer finance and forums outside Western control.

**5. The Global South hedges.** Most states refused to take sides over Ukraine and trade with all poles.

**6. Middle powers matter.** Pakistan mediated between Washington and Tehran; Qatar and Oman host talks; Saudi Arabia, Türkiye and Pakistan formed their own defence pact.

### The US–China rivalry at the centre
The contest is economic, technological, military and ideological. In 2025 a tariff war drove duties above 100 per cent before a truce at Busan in October. China showed its leverage by restricting rare-earth exports. In February 2026 the US Supreme Court struck down the emergency tariffs. At Shenzhen in November the truce was extended to January 2027, while a new American rule on advanced chips took effect the same day. Taiwan remains the most dangerous flashpoint. Graham Allison's "Thucydides Trap" warns that such transitions often end in war.

Yet this is not the Cold War repeated. The two economies remain deeply connected, there are no sealed blocs, and both sit in the same institutions. The present phase is better described as managed rivalry: frequent summits, extended truces and a hardening technology divide.

> **Fig. 1 (diagram to draw):** Three small diagrams side by side. (a) "Bipolar 1947–91": two large circles, US and USSR, with satellites. (b) "Unipolar 1991–2008": one large circle, US, with small ones around it. (c) "Multipolar 2026": circles of different sizes for US, China, Russia, EU and India, with a ring of middle powers (Türkiye, Saudi Arabia, Brazil, Indonesia, Pakistan) linked to several poles by dotted lines.

### Limits of the multipolarity thesis
The United States still has the largest military budget, the main reserve currency and the leading technology firms. Russia's economy is smaller than Italy's. Europe is divided, and India is a rising but not yet global power. Some scholars therefore describe the system as "bipolar with a multipolar fringe" or, in Amitav Acharya's phrase, a "multiplex" world of overlapping regional orders. The safest statement is that power is diffusing and no state can now set the rules alone.

### Is multipolarity good or bad?
**Benefits:** more partners and bargaining power for smaller states; alternative finance; less risk of one power imposing its will; space for regional solutions.

**Costs:** weaker rules protect the weak less; more wars (2022, 2023, 2025, 2026); arms races and the collapse of arms control; economic fragmentation; failure on collective problems such as climate, pandemics and artificial intelligence.

History offers a caution. The multipolar Europe of 1914 slid into war through rigid alliances and miscalculation.

### Implications for Pakistan
**Opportunities**
1. **Simultaneous partnerships.** In 2025–26 Pakistan was courted by Washington (a trade deal at 19 per cent, a White House welcome) and Beijing (CPEC's second phase), and signed defence pacts with Riyadh and Ankara.
2. **A mediator's role,** as in 1971 between the United States and China and in 2026 between the United States and Iran.
3. **Platforms:** the UN Security Council seat in 2025–26 and the SCO chair in 2026–27.

**Risks**
1. **Forced choices** on technology, ports, minerals and votes if the rivalry sharpens.
2. **India's rise** as a pole courted by all, including through the QUAD, which met again in November 2026.
3. **Erosion of law** that Pakistan relies on: the Indus Waters Treaty, UN resolutions on Kashmir.
4. **Entanglement** through new alliances.
5. **Economic weakness.** A state dependent on creditors has limited freedom, whatever the structure of the system.

### Way forward for Pakistan
1. Multi-alignment: issue-based cooperation with all poles, membership of no military bloc against any.
2. Economic strength as the foundation of foreign policy: exports, technology and fiscal health.
3. Consistent support for international law and multilateral institutions, which shield weaker states.
4. Coalitions with other middle powers on debt, climate and reform of global bodies.
5. Regional peace as a strategic asset: de-escalation with India and Afghanistan frees resources and attention.
6. Clear red lines on sovereignty: no bases, no participation in others' wars.

### Conclusion
The unipolar moment has passed. What is replacing it is a world of several powers, shifting coalitions and thinner rules. Such a world rewards states that are internally strong and diplomatically agile and punishes those that are neither. Pakistan showed agility in 2025–26. Its task before 2028 and beyond is to build the strength that makes agility safe.

## Answer 9

**Q. Critically evaluate the revival of Pakistan–US relations during President Trump's second term. Is the new relationship durable?**

### Introduction
At the start of 2025 Pakistan was a low priority in Washington. President Biden had not spoken to a Pakistani prime minister in four years, and the United States had just sanctioned entities linked to Pakistan's missile programme. Within months the picture changed. President Trump credited Pakistan with a counter-terrorism arrest, announced a ceasefire between India and Pakistan in May, hosted Field Marshal Asim Munir for lunch in June, concluded a trade deal with a 19 per cent tariff in July and received the Prime Minister and the army chief at the White House in September. In 2026 Pakistan became the main channel between Washington and Tehran. This answer argues that the revival is real and has brought Pakistan clear gains, but that it rests on personalities and transactions more than on institutions and shared interests, and its durability is therefore uncertain, particularly after the Democratic victory in the November 2026 midterm elections.

### Background: a history of cycles
The relationship has moved in cycles of intense cooperation and neglect. Alliance in the 1950s ended with the arms embargo of 1965. Partnership against the Soviet Union in the 1980s ended with the Pressler sanctions of 1990. Cooperation after 2001 ended in mutual accusation after the Abbottabad raid and the Salala attack of 2011. Each time the United States needed Pakistan for a specific security purpose, and each time the relationship cooled when the purpose was served.

### Drivers of the 2025–26 revival
**1. The May 2025 crisis.** Pakistan publicly thanked the American president for the ceasefire and nominated him for the Nobel Peace Prize. India denied any mediation, which irritated the White House.

**2. Personal diplomacy.** Direct access by the army chief and the Prime Minister suited an administration that prefers leader-to-leader deals.

**3. Counter-terrorism.** Cooperation against Islamic State Khorasan; the American listing of the Baloch Liberation Army as a terrorist organisation.

**4. Economics as an offer.** Critical minerals (a $500 million memorandum with a US firm), cryptocurrency cooperation and talk of oil exploration.

**5. US–India friction.** Tariffs of up to 50 per cent on India over trade and Russian oil left room for Pakistan.

**6. Gaza and Iran.** Pakistan voted for the American-drafted Security Council resolution on Gaza, joined the Board of Peace and then mediated in the Iran war, hosting the Islamabad Talks of April 2026 with the US Vice President present.

### What Pakistan gained
1. **Diplomatic standing** unseen in over a decade, and a failure of India's attempt to isolate it.
2. **A tariff advantage** over competitors in its largest export market.
3. **American crisis management** in South Asia, in effect internationalising the dispute with India.
4. **Goodwill at the IMF,** where the United States has the largest vote.
5. **Recognition of its terrorism concerns** in Balochistan.

Label the pattern "need-based engagement".

### Why durability is doubtful
**1. Thin substance.** As of October 2026 the trade agreement had not been signed, and the minerals and crypto memoranda remained largely on paper. Analysts note the gap between announcements and bankable projects.

**2. Legal uncertainty on tariffs.** The US Supreme Court struck down the emergency tariffs in February 2026. The rates that replaced them are contested in court, so Pakistan's edge may not last.

**3. Dependence on one presidency.** The relationship is tied to particular individuals in both capitals.

**4. A changed Congress.** Democrats won both chambers on 3 November 2026. They are likely to raise democracy, human rights and the imprisonment of the former prime minister, and to scrutinise the personal style of the administration's dealings.

**5. Structural divergences remain:**
- *China:* Pakistan's closest strategic partner is America's main rival. Washington watches Gwadar, Chinese arms and technology.

- *Iran:* Pakistan condemned the American–Israeli strikes and will not join military action.
- *Missiles:* American concern about long-range systems persists.
- *Israel and Palestine:* Pakistani public opinion limits how far any government can go with American plans.

**6. Domestic backlash.** Protesters were killed at the US consulate in Karachi after the strikes on Iran. Closeness to an unpopular war carries political cost.

### A balanced judgment
The revival should not be dismissed. Pakistan converted a military crisis and a change of administration into access and advantage at very low cost. Being a useful intermediary is a real asset. But the foundations are shallow. There is no strategic dialogue, modest trade of a few billion dollars, almost no aid and no shared view of the region. The pattern of the past seventy years suggests that when Washington's immediate needs (a ceasefire, a channel to Tehran) pass, attention will too, unless something more lasting is built.

### Way forward
1. **Institutionalise.** Sign the trade agreement; set up a strategic dialogue with working groups on energy, climate, technology, education and counter-terrorism.
2. **Broaden the base.** Engage both parties in Congress, the business community, universities and the Pakistani-American diaspora.
3. **Make economics real.** Turn minerals memoranda into transparent projects that benefit local communities; diversify exports beyond textiles into IT services.
4. **Keep balance.** Assure Beijing through transparency; avoid any step that turns CPEC or Gwadar into a US–China issue.
5. **Hold red lines.** No bases, no participation in wars against neighbours.
6. **Use the channel for peace.** Risk reduction with India; de-escalation in the Gulf.
7. **Address rights concerns on their merits** at home, which removes a lever others can use.

### Conclusion
Pakistan–US relations in 2025–26 are warmer than at any time since the early 2000s. The warmth was earned by skilful diplomacy and helped by circumstance. It is not yet a partnership in the sense of shared interests embedded in institutions. Unless Islamabad uses the present opening to build trade, investment and broad political ties, the current peak is likely to be followed, as before, by another trough.

## Answer 10

**Q. Assess the prospects for a just settlement of the Palestine question in light of the Trump plan and UN Security Council Resolution 2803. What role can Pakistan play?**

### Introduction
On 17 November 2025 the UN Security Council adopted Resolution 2803 by thirteen votes to none, with Russia and China abstaining. It endorsed President Trump's twenty-point plan for Gaza, welcomed a "Board of Peace" as a transitional administration and authorised an International Stabilization Force (ISF). A ceasefire had begun the previous month after two years of war that killed tens of thousands of Palestinians and destroyed most of Gaza. One year later the Board's own first annual report, issued on 17 November 2026, warned that the ceasefire could collapse: the stabilisation force had not deployed, Hamas had not disarmed and Israel had not allowed the technocratic administration to begin work. This answer argues that the plan stopped the worst of the killing but does not, as designed, lead to a just settlement, because it postpones the central issue of Palestinian statehood. Pakistan can play a useful but limited role if it ties its participation to that goal.

### Background
The conflict's roots lie in the 1917 Balfour Declaration, the 1947 partition plan, the creation of Israel and the displacement of Palestinians in 1948, and Israel's occupation of the West Bank, Gaza and East Jerusalem in 1967. The Oslo Accords of 1993 promised a two-state outcome that never arrived. After the Hamas-led attack of 7 October 2023, Israel's campaign in Gaza produced mass death, displacement and famine. The International Court of Justice ordered provisional measures in a genocide case and found the occupation unlawful; the International Criminal Court issued a warrant for Israel's prime minister. In September 2025 Britain, France, Canada, Australia and others recognised the State of Palestine.

### The plan and the resolution
The plan provided for a ceasefire, exchange of hostages and prisoners, phased Israeli withdrawal, disarmament of Hamas, an international force, reconstruction and a transitional authority chaired by the American president. It speaks of a "pathway" to Palestinian self-determination once reforms are complete. Resolution 2803 gave this structure legal cover until the end of 2027.

### What has been achieved
1. Large-scale fighting stopped in October 2025 and hostages and prisoners were exchanged.
2. Aid increased from famine levels.
3. The Board was formed in January 2026; a fifteen-point framework on disarmament was signed with Hamas in July 2026.
4. Arab and Muslim states, including Pakistan, are inside the process.

### Why the prospects are poor
**1. The core issue is deferred.** There is no timetable for statehood, no commitment on borders or Jerusalem and no halt to settlement expansion in the West Bank.

**2. Legitimacy.** The Board is not a UN body. Palestinians have little voice in it. Legal scholars call it without precedent.

**3. Sequencing deadlock.** Israel wants disarmament before withdrawal; Hamas wants withdrawal and guarantees before disarmament. Hamas still controls about half of Gaza.

**4. The force has no troops on the ground.** Israel has not signed the agreement needed for deployment, and contributing states do not want to fight Hamas.

**5. Israeli politics.** The election of 27 October 2026 gave no bloc a majority (the Prime Minister's bloc won 53 seats, the opposition bloc 48, Arab lists 19). A caretaker period makes hard decisions unlikely.

**6. Regional war.** The 2026 conflict with Iran and renewed fighting in Lebanon diverted attention and hardened positions.

**7. American politics.** The administration's energy is divided, and a new Congress will scrutinise its commitments.

**8. Impunity.** Court rulings remain unenforced.

A senior adviser to the Board has said it may hand the file back to the United Nations if obstruction continues. That admission shows how fragile the structure is.

### What a just settlement requires
International law and decades of resolutions point to the same elements: an independent, viable and contiguous Palestinian state on the 1967 lines with East Jerusalem as its capital; security for both peoples; a just solution for refugees; and an end to occupation and settlement. Any transitional arrangement should be judged by whether it moves towards these.

### Pakistan's position
Pakistan does not recognise Israel and has consistently supported a two-state solution. As a member of the Security Council in 2025–26 it voted for Resolution 2803, judging that ending the war was the priority, and it joined the Board of Peace. Its Deputy Prime Minister has said that Pakistan is ready to contribute to a peace force but will not take part in disarming Hamas. Pakistan was not among the five states that first committed troops. A survey in February 2026 found that a large majority of Pakistanis support sending troops in a peacekeeping role.

### What role can Pakistan play?
**1. Diplomatic.** Use its remaining weeks on the Council and its seat on the Board to insist on full Israeli withdrawal, unhindered aid and a time-bound path to statehood. Work with Saudi Arabia, Türkiye, Egypt, Qatar and Indonesia as a bloc.

**2. Peacekeeping, with conditions.** Pakistan is one of the most experienced UN troop contributors. It could join the stabilisation force if the mandate is clearly UN-anchored, limited to protecting civilians, borders and aid, accepted by Palestinians, and excludes combat against Palestinian groups.

**3. Humanitarian and reconstruction.** Field hospitals, medical teams, engineers, scholarships for Palestinian students and training of civil servants and police.

**4. Legal.** Support proceedings at the international courts and General Assembly action when the Council is blocked.

**5. Intra-Muslim coordination.** Through the OIC and the new Makkah alliance partners, push for Palestinian unity and reform of the Palestinian Authority.

**Limits.** Pakistan has no relations with Israel, modest economic weight and a public that will not accept anything resembling enforcement on Israel's behalf. Its influence depends on acting with others.

### Way forward
1. Deploy the stabilisation force under a UN-linked mandate with a withdrawal timetable.
2. Hand civil administration to Palestinians quickly, leading to elections and a unified leadership.
3. Fund reconstruction with guarantees against renewed destruction.
4. Freeze settlements and annexation in the West Bank.
5. Convert recognition by over 150 states into full UN membership.
6. Link regional normalisation to statehood, as the Arab Peace Initiative of 2002 proposed.

### Conclusion
Resolution 2803 and the plan behind it bought a pause in a catastrophic war. A pause is not peace. Unless the transitional arrangements are tied to an end of occupation and a Palestinian state, they risk becoming a new form of indefinite management. Pakistan cannot determine the outcome, but by placing its diplomatic voice and, on strict conditions, its peacekeepers behind a settlement grounded in law, it can help keep that outcome in view.

## Answer 11

**Q. "The nuclear non-proliferation regime is collapsing." Critically examine this statement and discuss its implications for South Asia.**

### Introduction
In the first half of 2026 two pillars of nuclear order gave way. On 5 February the New START treaty between the United States and Russia expired without a successor, leaving the two largest arsenals without agreed limits for the first time since 1972. On 22 May the Review Conference of the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty (NPT) ended without a consensus document, the third consecutive failure. In the same period two nuclear-armed states attacked the nuclear facilities of a non-nuclear state, and two nuclear neighbours in South Asia were still recovering from a missile exchange. This answer argues that "collapse" overstates the case, since the core bargain still restrains most states, but that the regime is in its deepest crisis, with direct consequences for India and Pakistan.

### The regime and its bargain
The NPT (1968) rests on three pillars: non-nuclear states will not acquire weapons; the five recognised nuclear states will pursue disarmament under Article VI; all may use nuclear energy peacefully under International Atomic Energy Agency safeguards. Around it stand the Comprehensive Test Ban Treaty (not in force), export-control groups such as the Nuclear Suppliers Group, regional nuclear-weapon-free zones and, until recently, a series of US–Russian arms control treaties.

### Evidence of crisis
**1. The end of arms control.** The ABM Treaty (2002), the INF Treaty (2019), Open Skies (2020–21) and now New START have all gone. Russia offered to keep observing the limits for a year; Washington did not formally reciprocate and says it wants a broader agreement that includes China.

**2. Arsenals are growing again.** SIPRI's 2026 Yearbook estimates China at about 620 warheads, the fastest increase in the world, with India at about 190 and Pakistan at about 170. Nearly all nuclear states are modernising.

**3. The disarmament promise is unmet.** Non-nuclear states see no progress under Article VI. Many have turned to the Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons (2021), which no nuclear power accepts.

**4. Three failed review conferences.** In 2026 the immediate cause was the American–Iranian dispute; language on the norm against testing was also lost.

**5. Talk of testing.** Statements from Washington in late 2025 about resuming tests, and Russia's withdrawal of its test-ban ratification in 2023, weaken a thirty-year norm.

**6. Force against safeguarded facilities.** The strikes on Iran in 2025 and 2026 hit sites under IAEA monitoring.

**7. The lesson states may draw.** Ukraine gave up weapons and was invaded. Libya gave up its programme and its government fell. Iran stayed below the threshold and was bombed. North Korea built weapons and has not been attacked.

**8. Allies reconsider.** Debate has grown in South Korea, Japan, Poland, Saudi Arabia and Türkiye about independent deterrents or new umbrellas.

**9. Double standards.** Israel's undeclared arsenal is ignored. India received a waiver from the Nuclear Suppliers Group in 2008 without joining the NPT. AUKUS transfers weapons-grade naval fuel to a non-nuclear state.

### Evidence against "collapse"
1. **The numbers.** Only nine states have nuclear weapons, fewer than the twenty or more predicted in the 1960s. No state has acquired them since North Korea in 2006.
2. **Near-universal membership.** 191 parties remain in the NPT; no one has left since 2003.
3. **Safeguards function** in most of the world, and the IAEA continues to work.
4. **The test moratorium holds** in practice among the major powers.
5. **Restraint in crises.** No nuclear weapon has been used since 1945, including during the 2025 and 2026 wars.

The regime is therefore eroding from the top, through the conduct of the strongest states, more than it is failing from below.

> **Fig. 1 (diagram to draw):** A temple with three pillars labelled "Non-proliferation", "Disarmament (Art. VI)" and "Peaceful use", under a roof labelled "NPT 1968". Draw cracks in each pillar with notes: "Iran strikes; ally hedging" on the first, "New START expired 2026; arsenals growing" on the second, "attacks on safeguarded sites" on the third. Below the base write "export controls, CTBT, IAEA".

### Implications for South Asia
**1. A chain reaction.** China builds against the United States; India builds against China; Pakistan responds to India. The collapse of limits at the top feeds numbers at the bottom.

**2. Discrimination hardens.** The India–US nuclear deal and expanding American technology transfers to India, set against sanctions on Pakistani missile entities, confirm Pakistan's view that the regime is selective.

**3. Testing pressure.** If major powers resume testing, India may follow to validate thermonuclear designs, and Pakistan would then face its own decision.

**4. Fissile material.** With no cut-off treaty and with India's unsafeguarded reactors, production continues on both sides.

**5. New technologies** such as multiple-warhead missiles, missile defence, hypersonic weapons and sea-based systems are entering the region without any agreed limits.

**6. Crisis instability.** The May 2025 conflict brought conventional strikes close to command sites. Without arms control or dialogue, the next crisis will be more dangerous.

**7. Extended deterrence questions.** Pakistan's 2025 defence agreement with Saudi Arabia prompted speculation about a nuclear umbrella, which Pakistan has not confirmed. Any such impression would bring pressure upon it.

**8. Loss of a model.** For decades US–Russian arms control offered a template for confidence-building. Its disappearance removes both an example and a source of outside encouragement.

### Pakistan's position
Pakistan is outside the NPT. It says it will accept non-proliferation obligations on an equal footing with India, supports a criteria-based approach to the Nuclear Suppliers Group, observes a unilateral test moratorium and has proposed a strategic restraint regime to India since 1998. It opposes a fissile material treaty that freezes existing asymmetries.

### Way forward
**Global**
1. An interim US–Russian understanding on limits and transparency; risk-reduction talks that include China.
2. A joint pledge by the five recognised powers not to test; renewed effort to bring the test ban into force.
3. Legally binding assurances that nuclear weapons will not be used against non-nuclear states.
4. A negotiated settlement with Iran under IAEA verification.
5. Non-discriminatory criteria for states outside the NPT.
6. Human control over all nuclear launch decisions in an age of artificial intelligence.

**South Asia**
7. A bilateral test-ban commitment and extension of missile test notification to cruise and hypersonic systems.
8. Nuclear risk-reduction centres and a political hotline.
9. An understanding not to attack command, control and early-warning assets.
10. Resumption of the expert-level dialogue on nuclear and conventional confidence-building measures, which has been dormant for more than a decade.

### Conclusion
The non-proliferation regime has not collapsed, but its guardians are dismantling its supports. When the strongest abandon restraint, attack safeguarded sites and reward those who armed, the weak draw their own conclusions. South Asia, with two growing arsenals, unresolved disputes and no arms control, is where the consequences could be gravest. Rebuilding even modest restraints, globally and between India and Pakistan, is now a matter of basic prudence.

## Answer 12

**Q. Critically evaluate the 26th and 27th Amendments to the Constitution of Pakistan with reference to judicial independence and the separation of powers.**

### Introduction
In thirteen months Pakistan's parliament changed the architecture of the state more than at any time since the 18th Amendment of 2010. The 26th Amendment (October 2024) altered how the Chief Justice is chosen and created constitutional benches. The 27th Amendment, signed on 13 November 2025, went further: it established a Federal Constitutional Court above the Supreme Court in constitutional matters and rewrote Article 243 to make the Army chief the Chief of Defence Forces. Supporters describe these changes as overdue reform by a sovereign parliament. Critics, including judges who resigned, call them an assault on judicial independence. This answer argues that some aims of the amendments are defensible, but that the manner of their passage and several of their provisions weaken the checks and balances on which constitutional government depends.

### Background
Pakistan's superior judiciary has swung between submission and assertion. It validated military takeovers under the doctrine of necessity, then, after the lawyers' movement of 2007–09, used its suo motu power under Article 184(3) widely, removing two prime ministers and intervening in economic decisions with costly results. Politicians of all parties complained of judicial overreach. The 2006 Charter of Democracy had proposed a separate constitutional court. The immediate setting, however, was the dispute over the 2024 election and pending cases on reserved seats and military trials.

### The 26th Amendment
1. The Chief Justice is no longer automatically the senior-most judge. A Special Parliamentary Committee selects from the three most senior, for a fixed three-year term.
2. Constitutional benches were created in the Supreme Court and high courts, with judges nominated by the Judicial Commission.
3. The Judicial Commission was reconstituted to include parliamentarians.
4. The suo motu power was narrowed.
5. A right to a clean and healthy environment (Article 9A) was added.

### The 27th Amendment
1. **A Federal Constitutional Court** now hears constitutional interpretation, fundamental-rights cases and disputes between governments. The Supreme Court is confined largely to civil and criminal appeals.
2. **Transfer of high court judges** on the Judicial Commission's recommendation.
3. **Article 243:** the Army chief becomes Chief of Defence Forces; the office of Chairman Joint Chiefs of Staff Committee was abolished from 27 November 2025; five-star officers hold rank and privileges for life.
4. **Immunity:** lifetime protection from criminal proceedings for the President and five-star officers, as widely reported.

The bill was introduced on 8 November, passed the National Assembly on 12 November and the Senate on 13 November by 64 votes to none after an opposition walkout, and was signed the same day.

### The case for the amendments
**1. Parliamentary sovereignty.** Article 239 gives parliament the power to amend the Constitution by two-thirds majorities, which were obtained.

**2. Specialisation.** Germany, South Africa, Italy and Türkiye have constitutional courts. Separating constitutional work may let the Supreme Court clear its heavy backlog of appeals.

**3. Correcting overreach.** Unstructured suo motu action had damaged governance and investor confidence.

**4. Democratic input in appointments.** Many democracies give elected bodies a role in choosing judges.

**5. Unified command.** A chief of defence is common in modern militaries and reflects lessons of the May 2025 conflict.

### The case against
**1. Process.** Both amendments passed within days, with late drafts and little debate. The 18th Amendment was negotiated for nine months in an all-party committee. Constitutions built without consensus lack legitimacy.

**2. Executive influence over the bench.** The government's majority now shapes who becomes Chief Justice, who sits on constitutional benches and the new court, and where a high court judge serves. Security of tenure, the core of independence, is weakened by the transfer power.

**3. Timing and motive.** The changes came while cases affecting the government's own position were pending, which creates the appearance of choosing one's judges.

**4. Displacement of the Supreme Court.** The court created by the 1973 Constitution as guardian of fundamental rights has lost that role to a body whose first members were appointed under the new system.

**5. Immunity for life** conflicts with equality before law (Article 25) and with the principle that power must be accountable.

**6. Civil–military balance.** Constitutional entrenchment of one office-holder's enhanced powers reduces civilian flexibility.

**7. Reactions.** Justices Mansoor Ali Shah and Athar Minallah resigned from the Supreme Court on the day of assent. The International Commission of Jurists called the amendment a "full-frontal assault on the rule of law". Opposition parties and bar associations protested. Business bodies warned of uncertainty.

> **Fig. 1 (diagram to draw):** Two block diagrams side by side. *Before October 2024:* Supreme Court at the top (constitutional + appellate), five High Courts below, district courts at the base; an arrow labelled "CJP = senior-most judge". *After November 2025:* two boxes at the top, "Federal Constitutional Court (constitution, rights, federation disputes)" and "Supreme Court (civil and criminal appeals)"; High Courts with "constitutional benches"; on the side a box "Judicial Commission incl. parliamentarians" and "Special Parliamentary Committee picks CJP from top three".

### Separation of powers: an assessment
The doctrine does not require that judges choose themselves. It requires that no branch control another. Three tests can be applied. *Appointment:* are judges chosen by a balanced, transparent process? The new commission tilts towards the political majority. *Tenure:* are judges secure against punishment for their rulings? Transfer powers and selection of chiefs by committee create incentives to please. *Jurisdiction:* can courts review executive and legislative acts? Formally yes, through the new court, but its independence will be judged by its conduct. Comparative experience matters here. Germany's and South Africa's constitutional courts were created by broad consensus at founding moments, with supermajority appointments designed to prevent capture. Pakistan's was created by one side during a political conflict.

### Way forward
1. Publish clear rules dividing jurisdiction between the two apex courts to avoid conflict and delay.
2. Adopt transparent, merit-based criteria for appointments, with recorded reasons.
3. Protect tenure: no transfer without consent or a reasoned order subject to review.
4. Narrow the immunity clauses to official acts during office.
5. Send any further amendment, including the proposed 28th on the NFC and local government, through an all-party committee and the Council of Common Interests.
6. Address the real grievance of citizens, which is delay in ordinary courts, through more judges and case management.

### Conclusion
The 26th and 27th Amendments answered genuine problems of judicial overreach and fragmented command with remedies that concentrate influence in the executive and its allies. Their legality under Article 239 is not in doubt; their legitimacy is. Whether they strengthen the state will depend on whether the new court proves willing to rule against those who created it. A constitution endures when all sides see it as a fair set of rules. Rebuilding that consensus is the unfinished work.

## Answer 13

**Q. Would the creation of new provinces strengthen or weaken the federation of Pakistan? Give reasoned arguments.**

### Introduction
Pakistan has four provinces for a population the Economic Survey puts at about 252 million. Punjab alone, with roughly 128 million people, would be the twelfth-largest country in the world. Demands for a Saraiki or South Punjab province, a Hazara province and other units have been raised for decades, and both major parties have at times promised action. The question has returned in the debate on a proposed 28th Amendment, which reportedly includes an easier route to creating provinces, while the Sindh Assembly resolved against any division of that province in September 2026. This answer argues that new provinces can strengthen the federation if they are created on administrative grounds, by consensus and alongside strong local government. Imposed or ethnically driven division would weaken it.

### Background
At independence Pakistan had five provinces and several states. The One Unit scheme (1955) merged West Pakistan into a single province to balance East Pakistan; it deepened resentment and was dissolved in 1970. The 1973 Constitution recognised four provinces. The 18th Amendment (2010) gave them wide autonomy. Under Article 239(4), a bill altering the limits of a province requires a two-thirds majority in both Houses of Parliament and in the assembly of the province concerned. A parliamentary commission on new provinces in Punjab reported in 2013 without result. In 2018 the tribal areas were merged into Khyber Pakhtunkhwa.

### Arguments that new provinces would strengthen the federation
**1. Better administration.** A resident of Rajanpur is over 500 kilometres from Lahore. Smaller units bring the secretariat, the high court and the budget closer to citizens.

**2. A more balanced federation.** One province with more than half the population and the largest bloc in the National Assembly distorts federal politics. More units of comparable size would spread influence, including in the Senate.

**3. Regional equity.** Southern Punjab, Hazara and other regions lag on human development and receive less spending per head than provincial capitals.

**4. Comparative practice.** India has 28 states after repeated reorganisation since 1956; Nigeria moved from 3 regions to 36 states; Türkiye has 81 provinces. In each case smaller units helped manage diversity.

**5. Democratic demand.** Elected assemblies and parties have endorsed a South Punjab province in principle.

**6. Competition and innovation.** More governments mean more experiments in service delivery.

### Arguments that new provinces would weaken the federation
**1. Cost.** Each province needs an assembly, a cabinet, a secretariat, a high court and a public service commission. In a state where interest payments exceed net federal revenue, this is a heavy burden.

**2. Ethnic fragmentation.** Provinces drawn on linguistic lines could harden identities and create new minorities within each unit, inviting further demands.

**3. Conflict over division.** Sindh's parties across the spectrum reject any partition of the province. A move to carve out Karachi would be seen as an attack on Sindhi identity and could trigger unrest.

**4. Reopening settled bargains.** NFC shares, water under the 1991 Accord, Senate seats and job quotas would all need renegotiation at a time when centre–province trust is low.

**5. No guarantee of better governance.** Provincial capitals have centralised power since 2010. A new capital at Multan or Bahawalpur could repeat the pattern.

**6. Political manipulation.** Units might be designed to break rival parties' bases.

**7. Security.** Division of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa or Balochistan during active insurgencies would be destabilising.

> **Fig. 1 (map to draw):** An outline of Pakistan with the four provinces, Islamabad, Azad Kashmir and Gilgit-Baltistan. Shade southern Punjab (Multan, Bahawalpur, Dera Ghazi Khan divisions) and label it "proposed South Punjab". Shade the Hazara division of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and label it "proposed Hazara". Mark Karachi. Add a small table: Punjab 127.7m, Sindh 55.7m, KP 40.9m, Balochistan 14.9m (Census 2023).

### The alternative and complement: local government
Much of the case for new provinces is a case against distance and neglect. Elected district and city governments with their own funds address both at far lower cost. Yet Punjab has had no elected local councils since 2019, and polls are now scheduled only for February 2027. Article 140A remains largely unimplemented. Without a third tier, new provinces would simply create new centres of concentration.

### A balanced position
The evidence supports four propositions.
1. **Size matters.** A federation in which one unit outweighs all others combined is inherently strained. Some rebalancing is healthy.
2. **Consent matters more.** Article 239(4) wisely requires the affected province's agreement. Where consent exists (southern Punjab), reorganisation is feasible. Where it does not (Sindh), it should not be attempted.
3. **Criteria matter.** Administrative viability, population, area, revenue and geography should guide boundaries, as India's States Reorganisation Commission did in 1955. Identity cannot be ignored but should not be the only basis.
4. **Sequencing matters.** Local government and a fair NFC award should come first or together.

### Way forward
1. Establish a national commission of parliamentarians, provincial representatives and experts to set criteria and examine specific proposals.
2. Begin with South Punjab, where demand is long-standing and all major parties have agreed in principle; secure the Punjab Assembly's two-thirds approval.
3. In the interim, give regional secretariats real powers and budgets.
4. Amend Article 140A to guarantee local government tenure, elections and finance.
5. Conclude the 11th NFC with a formula that does not penalise reorganisation.
6. Protect minorities within any new unit and guarantee water rights.
7. Leave Sindh, Balochistan and Khyber Pakhtunkhwa as they are unless their own assemblies decide otherwise.

### Conclusion
New provinces are neither a cure for misgovernance nor a threat in themselves. Done by agreement, on rational criteria and with empowered local governments, they would bring the state closer to citizens and even out a lopsided federation. Done in haste, for partisan advantage or against a province's will, they would add cost and conflict to a country that has enough of both. The federation is strengthened less by the number of its units than by the fairness with which power and resources are shared among them.

## Answer 14

**Q. Can artificial intelligence be governed globally? Evaluate current international efforts and discuss the opportunities and risks for a developing country such as Pakistan.**

### Introduction
Artificial intelligence (AI) has moved in three years from research laboratories into classrooms, offices, newsrooms and battlefields. The IMF's October 2026 outlook credits investment in AI with sustaining world growth despite a war in the Gulf and a tariff conflict, and at the same time warns of a debt-financed bubble. In July 2026 the United Nations convened its first Global Dialogue on AI Governance and received the first report of a new scientific panel. Yet on 19 November 2026, the day the American and Chinese presidents discussed AI cooperation, a new US rule restricting China's access to advanced chips took effect. This answer argues that a single global regime for AI is not achievable in the present climate, but that a layered system of shared science, narrow binding rules for the gravest risks and national regulation is both possible and emerging. For Pakistan, AI offers a route to exports and better services, provided that foundations which its 2025 policy assumes are actually built.

### Why AI is hard to govern
1. **It is general-purpose.** The same model writes code, diagnoses disease and designs weapons.
2. **It is concentrated.** A few firms in the United States and China control frontier models; one company's designs and one island's factories dominate advanced chips.
3. **It moves faster than law.**
4. **It is entangled with great-power rivalry,** so each side fears that restraint means defeat.
5. **Its harms are diverse:** bias, surveillance, job loss, disinformation, autonomous weapons and, in the view of many scientists, loss of human control.

### Current international efforts
**Principles.** The OECD (2019) and UNESCO (2021) agreed non-binding ethical principles.

**Summits.** Bletchley Park (2023) produced a declaration on frontier risks; Seoul (2024) and Paris (2025) followed, the latter without American and British signatures. New Delhi hosted an "AI Impact Summit" in February 2026, the first in the Global South.

**Regional law.** The European Union's AI Act (2024) is the first comprehensive statute, with obligations scaled to risk and bans on practices such as social scoring. The Council of Europe adopted the first binding treaty on AI and human rights.

**National approaches.** The United States has turned from the safety-oriented order of 2023 to deregulation and export promotion, while tightening chip controls on China. China regulates algorithms and generative services under state supervision and promotes its own global initiative.

**United Nations.** The Global Digital Compact (2024) led to General Assembly resolution 79/325 of August 2025. It created an Independent International Scientific Panel on AI, forty experts co-chaired by Yoshua Bengio and Maria Ressa, which issued a preliminary report on 1 July 2026, and a Global Dialogue on AI Governance, first held in Geneva on 6–7 July 2026 and due to meet again in New York in May 2027.

**Other forums.** The G7's voluntary code for developers; a BRICS leaders' statement; talks on autonomous weapons that have produced no treaty.

### Evaluation
**Progress.** There is now a common vocabulary, a UN-based scientific body modelled loosely on the climate panel, and one major jurisdiction with enforceable rules that others are copying.

**Gaps.**
1. *Fragmentation.* Three models compete: rights-based (EU), market-led (US) and state-led (China).
2. *No binding rules on military use,* where the danger is greatest. AI-assisted targeting has already been reported in recent wars.
3. *Enforcement.* The UN panel advises; it cannot inspect.
4. *The South is a rule-taker.* Most developing states lack compute, data and experts.
5. *Corporate power* exceeds the capacity of most regulators.

> **Fig. 1 (diagram to draw):** A pyramid of four layers, base to tip: "National laws and standards (data protection, liability)"; "Regional regimes (EU AI Act, Council of Europe treaty)"; "Global soft law and science (UN panel, Global Dialogue, OECD/UNESCO principles, summits)"; and at the tip "Binding global rules needed: autonomous weapons, AI in nuclear command, biosecurity". Beside it write "US: market-led / EU: rights-based / China: state-led".

### Can AI be governed globally?
A single treaty and agency on the model of the International Atomic Energy Agency is unlikely: AI is software that copies freely, not material that can be counted, and the leading powers are rivals. But arms control and climate show that partial regimes can work. The realistic design has three layers: shared scientific assessment through the UN panel; a small number of binding prohibitions on catastrophic uses, beginning with a commitment that humans control nuclear launch decisions, on which the American and Chinese leaders agreed in principle in 2024; and interoperable national laws for everything else. So the answer is yes, in part and in layers.

### Opportunities for Pakistan
1. **Exports and jobs.** IT exports were about $3.4 billion in the first nine months of FY26, up nearly 24 per cent. AI services, data work and software can extend this.
2. **Public services.** AI in tax administration, land records, flood forecasting, crop advice, tele-medicine and Urdu-language education.
3. **A young population** that adopts technology quickly.
4. **Policy base.** The National AI Policy 2025 aims to train one million people by 2030; the Digital Nation Pakistan Act created a digital authority; 5G spectrum was auctioned in March 2026.

### Risks for Pakistan
1. **Jobs.** Routine outsourcing work is the first to be automated.
2. **Dependence** on foreign models, chips and cloud services, with export controls beyond its influence.
3. **Disinformation.** Synthetic video circulated during the May 2025 conflict; elections are vulnerable.
4. **Surveillance and rights** in the absence of a data-protection law.
5. **Military asymmetry.** India's larger technology base and foreign partnerships.
6. **A widening divide** between connected cities and the rest, and between men and women.
7. **Weak foundations:** unreliable internet, frequent shutdowns, limited electricity for data centres at competitive prices, few researchers.

### Way forward
**For the world**
1. Strengthen the UN panel and make the Global Dialogue a standing forum with developing-country participation funded.
2. Negotiate a binding instrument on lethal autonomous weapons and on AI in nuclear command.
3. Create shared compute and open models for the Global South.
4. Require transparency, testing and incident reporting from frontier developers.

**For Pakistan**
5. Pass a data-protection law that meets international standards.
6. Guarantee reliable connectivity and end blanket shutdowns.
7. Invest in skills from school to university, with a focus on women.
8. Build a national compute facility for universities and start-ups, using surplus power.
9. Adopt a risk-based national framework and an ethics body; label synthetic media.
10. Take an active part in UN processes and coordinate positions with other developing states.

### Conclusion
Artificial intelligence will not wait for the world to agree. Global governance is emerging in pieces: science at the UN, law in Europe, rivalry between Washington and Beijing. For a country like Pakistan the technology is neither salvation nor threat by nature. It will reward those who have connectivity, skills, data rules and trust, and it will bypass those who do not. Pakistan's AI policy has named the goal. The work of the next few years is to build the foundations.

## Answer 15

**Q. Pakistan faces interlinked crises of climate, population, governance, security and public finance. Critically evaluate these vulnerabilities and propose a strategy for a stable, secure and disaster-resilient state.**

### Introduction
Pakistan's problems are usually discussed one at a time. In reality they feed each other. A population growing at 2.55 per cent a year presses on water, schools and jobs. Floods in 2022 and again in 2025 destroyed assets faster than they could be rebuilt. Interest payments now consume more than the federal government's net revenue, leaving little for adaptation or services. Weak institutions cannot plan or deliver. Grievance and thin policing in the border provinces have made Pakistan the country most affected by terrorism in the 2026 global index. Insecurity then deters the investment that could ease the fiscal strain. This answer treats the five vulnerabilities as a single system, shows how they interact and proposes a strategy built on the points where one reform relieves several pressures at once.

### The five vulnerabilities
**1. Climate.** Pakistan emits under 1 per cent of greenhouse gases and was ranked first in the world for climate losses in 2022 and fifteenth for the three decades to 2024. The 2022 floods affected about 33 million people and caused over $30 billion in damage and loss. The 2025 monsoon killed more than a thousand and cost about $2.9 billion by official estimate. Glaciers are melting, heatwaves exceed 50°C, and India's suspension of flood-data sharing under the Indus Waters Treaty adds risk.

**2. Population.** The 2023 census counted 241.5 million people; the latest estimate is about 252 million. Two-thirds are under thirty. Between 20 and 26 million children are out of school, female literacy is about 54 per cent, and Pakistan ranks 143rd of 145 countries on the gender gap index.

**3. Governance.** Local governments are absent in most of the country. Two constitutional amendments in thirteen months restructured the courts amid controversy. An elected provincial government was replaced by governor's rule in October 2026. Policy changes with each political turn.

**4. Security.** There were 1,139 terrorism deaths in 2025 and no improvement in 2026. The state is fighting the TTP and Baloch insurgents at home and has been in open conflict with the Taliban government in Afghanistan since February 2026, eighteen months after a military clash with India.

**5. Public finance.** Public debt is about 68 per cent of GDP. Interest takes over 42 per cent of the federal budget. Tax revenue is around a tenth of GDP. Growth was 3.7 per cent in FY26 and poverty rose to 28.9 per cent. Education and health each receive less than 1 per cent of GDP.

### How the crises reinforce each other
- **Population and climate:** more people live on floodplains and in fragile cities; per capita water has fallen below the scarcity threshold.
- **Climate and finance:** each disaster cuts growth and revenue and adds borrowing; the 2025 floods were one reason the growth target was missed.
- **Finance and governance:** an indebted centre and under-taxing provinces fight over shares instead of delivering services; austerity erodes state capacity.
- **Governance and security:** unfinished reforms in the merged districts and political grievance in Balochistan create space for militants.
- **Security and finance:** defence spending rose 17.7 per cent in the FY27 budget; insecurity deters investors and tourists.
- **Security and population:** unemployed young men in neglected regions are the recruiting pool.

> **Fig. 1 (diagram to draw):** A circle of five boxes joined by arrows: "Rapid population growth" → "Pressure on water, land, services" → "Climate shocks destroy assets" → "Fiscal stress: debt, low revenue" → "Weak governance and service delivery" → "Grievance and insecurity" → "Low investment and growth" → back to "Fiscal stress". In the centre write "elite capture and political instability". Beside it draw the same circle reversed and label it "virtuous cycle: local government → services → trust → revenue → resilience".

### Critical evaluation: what has been tried
The state has not been idle. The IMF programme has rebuilt reserves and produced primary surpluses. A national adaptation plan exists. Military operations continue. The Benazir Income Support Programme reaches millions of women. Out-of-school rates have fallen from 38 to 28 per cent in two years. But responses are sector-by-sector, reactive and centrally driven. Stabilisation has come through taxing the already-taxed and cutting development. Disaster management starts after the water arrives. Security policy relies on force. Population has no political owner. The common missing element is a capable, accountable state at the level where citizens live.

### A strategy for resilience
**Pillar 1: Fiscal space through fairness**
1. Broaden the tax base to agriculture, property and retail; remove exemptions.
2. A national fiscal pact: provinces raise their own revenue and fund devolved services fully; the centre sheds duplicate functions.
3. Debt management, including debt-for-climate swaps, to free resources.

**Pillar 2: Governance from the bottom**
4. Constitutionally protected, elected local governments with assured funds, responsible for water, sanitation, primary schools, clinics and first response to disasters.
5. Civil service reform: tenure security, specialisation and digital systems.
6. A cross-party minimum agenda so that core policies survive changes of government.

**Pillar 3: People**
7. Population stabilisation through girls' secondary education, family-planning services at every health facility and a minimum marriage age of 18.
8. Education and health spending raised in steps towards international benchmarks; skills for a young workforce.
9. Women's economic inclusion.

**Pillar 4: Climate and disaster resilience**
10. Enforce floodplain zoning; restore wetlands and recharge aquifers ("living Indus").
11. Early warning to the last mile, independent of upstream data.
12. Resilient reconstruction standards for housing, roads and schools.
13. Water reform: conservation, pricing, storage at all scales.
14. Risk finance: insurance, contingency funds, access to loss and damage and adaptation funds.
15. A shift to domestic renewable energy, which also cuts the import bill exposed in the 2026 oil shock.

**Pillar 5: Security through law and politics**
16. A renewed National Action Plan owned by all parties and provinces, led by police and prosecutors.
17. Political settlements: development and local government in the merged districts; dialogue and resource-sharing in Balochistan.
18. De-escalation abroad: verified arrangements with Kabul; crisis management with India. Fewer external fronts mean more resources at home.

### Sequencing and feasibility
Not everything can be done at once. Three steps have the widest effects: local government (it improves services, disaster response and trust together), girls' education (it lowers fertility, raises incomes and improves health) and tax reform (it funds everything else). Each faces resistance from entrenched interests, which is why political consensus is the precondition, not an afterthought.

### Conclusion
Pakistan's vulnerabilities form a cycle in which each crisis deepens the next. The same structure means that well-chosen reforms can turn the cycle the other way. A state that taxes fairly, governs locally, invests in its women and children, builds with floods in mind and settles its conflicts by politics where it can will be more stable, more secure and more resilient. The knowledge to do this exists in Pakistan's own policy documents. What has been lacking is continuity and consensus. Supplying them is the central task of the country's leadership in the years ahead.
