# Précis Writing (Q2, 20 marks)

A précis is a short, exact restatement of a passage in your own words. It keeps the author's main idea and supporting points, in the author's order and proportion, and drops everything else. It is about one-third of the length of the original. It carries a title.

The syllabus gives 15 marks to the précis and 5 marks to the title.

Contents:

1. What the examiner wants
2. The method, step by step
3. Rules of form: length, person, tense, own words, title
4. How to cut: what goes and what stays

## 1. What the examiner wants

| The examiner checks | What it means |
|---|---|
| Understanding | You found the real central idea, not a side point |
| Completeness | Every main point of the passage is present |
| Proportion | You gave each point roughly the weight the author gave it |
| Brevity | About one-third of the original, or the limit stated in the question |
| Own words | No sentences lifted from the passage. Key terms may stay |
| Form | One paragraph, third person, consistent tense, no direct speech, no questions |
| Fidelity | No opinion, comment, or outside example of your own |
| Language | Correct grammar, spelling and punctuation |
| Title | Short, accurate, covers the whole passage |

What past papers asked for:

| Year | Wording of the instruction (paraphrased) | Passage length |
|---|---|---|
| 2016, 2018 | Write a précis "in about 120 words" and suggest a title | About 350–400 words |
| 2017, 2019–2024 | Write a précis and suggest a suitable title (no limit given) | About 240–400 words |
| 2025 | Distil the passage into a précis and suggest an apt title | About 620 words (the longest in the period) |
| 2026 | One source reports a stated limit of 100 words | About 290 words |

So: if a limit is given, obey it. If not, write one-third.

---

## 2. The method, step by step (40 minutes)

| Step | Minutes | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| 1. First reading | 3 | Read the whole passage without a pen. Ask: what is this about? What does the author want me to believe? |
| 2. Second reading | 5 | Read with a pencil. Underline the topic sentence of each paragraph. Bracket examples, repetition, quotations and figures of speech (these will go). Number the main points in the margin |
| 3. Main idea | 2 | Write one sentence of your own that states the central idea. If you cannot, read again. This sentence becomes the spine of the précis and the source of the title |
| 4. Point outline | 3 | List the 4–7 supporting points as short notes, in the author's order, in your own words. Close the question paper while you do this. That forces your own wording |
| 5. Count the target | 1 | Count the words in the passage (count one line, multiply by the number of lines). Divide by three. Write the target in the margin |
| 6. Rough draft | 12 | Write the précis from your outline, not from the passage. Join the points with linking words (because, therefore, however, while, since) |
| 7. Count and trim | 5 | Count every word. If you are over, cut adjectives, merge two sentences, replace a phrase with a word. If you are far under, a point is missing |
| 8. Check | 3 | Compare with the passage. Any main point missing? Anything added? Any first person, question, quotation, or lifted sentence? One tense? |
| 9. Fair copy and title | 6 | Write the title, then the précis as one paragraph, then the word count in brackets |

### Layout on the answer sheet

```
Q.2
                    Title: The Value of the Reading Habit

    [One paragraph. No bullet points. No headings.]

                                            (Words: 112)

    Rough work (crossed through with one diagonal line)
```

Do the rough draft on the answer book too, and cross it out with a single line. Examiners accept this. Do not leave the fair copy for the last five minutes.

## 3. Rules of form

### 3.1 Length

- No limit stated: one-third of the passage, give or take 5 words. For a 300-word passage, write 95–105.
- Limit stated ("about 120 words"): stay within 5 words either side.
- Never guess the count. Count it.
- A précis that is much too long shows you could not select. A précis that is much too short has usually dropped a point.

What counts as a word: every word, including *a*, *the*, *of*. A hyphenated word counts as one. A number written in figures counts as one. The title is not counted.

### 3.2 Person

Always the third person.

| The passage says | The précis says |
|---|---|
| I believe that schools kill curiosity. | Schools destroy curiosity. *or* The writer believed that schools destroyed curiosity. |
| We in the developing world must ask ourselves... | Developing nations must consider... |
| You will find that habit rules your day. | Habit governs daily life. |

Use "the author" or "the writer" only when the passage is a personal account or a personal opinion that cannot be stated as a general fact. Use it once, not in every sentence.

### 3.3 Tense

| Kind of passage | Tense in the précis |
|---|---|
| General truths, argument, reflection (most CSS passages) | Present tense: "Education shapes character." |
| A story, a historical account, a personal experience | Past tense: "The writer visited... and found..." |
| A passage in the first person giving opinions | Either state the opinion as a general statement in the present, or report it: "The writer argued that..." followed by the past tense |

Two things matter more than which tense you choose: be consistent, and never use direct speech. The FPSC examiners' report on CSS 2016, as quoted in a search snippet (the report itself could not be opened), complained that candidates did not write in the third person or the past tense. The traditional school rule (Wren and Martin) is reported speech in the past tense. For a passage of general argument, the present tense is the normal modern practice and is safe. For anything narrated, use the past.

### 3.4 Own words

- Do not copy any full sentence or long phrase.
- You may keep technical or key terms that have no simple substitute (democracy, civil society, climate, machine).
- Replace phrases with single words. Examples:

| Phrase in passage | One word |
|---|---|
| people who live in cities | city-dwellers |
| at the present moment in time | now |
| a person who cannot read or write | an illiterate |
| in spite of the fact that | although |
| things that cannot be avoided | necessities / the inevitable |
| over and over again | repeatedly |
| those who came before us | predecessors |
| the place where a person was born | birthplace |

### 3.5 The title

- A phrase, not a sentence. Usually 2–7 words.
- It names the central theme of the whole passage, not the first paragraph only.
- Capitalise the main words. No full stop.
- Neutral and accurate. Not clever, not vague.
- Write it at the top, centred.

| Weak title | Why | Better |
|---|---|---|
| Reading | Too broad | The Value of the Reading Habit |
| Children should read books at home with their parents so that they can become good citizens | A sentence, too long | Reading Habit and Public Life |
| A Dying Art!!! | Vague, dramatic | The Decline of Serious Reading |
| Books vs Headlines | Covers one paragraph only | The Value of the Reading Habit |

A quick way to build a title: take your one-sentence main idea and reduce it to its subject plus its key claim. "City trees are a cheap necessity that planners neglect" becomes "The Neglected Value of City Trees".

## 4. How to cut

| Cut | Keep |
|---|---|
| Examples and illustrations (keep at most the general point they prove) | The claim the example supports |
| Repetition and restatement | The idea, once |
| Quotations and proverbs | The sense, in plain words |
| Figures of speech, similes, metaphors | The literal meaning |
| Lists of items | The class name ("various crafts", "several causes") |
| Statistics | The trend ("most", "a sharp rise"), unless the number is the point |
| Rhetorical questions | The statement they imply |
| Anecdotes and digressions | Usually nothing; sometimes the one-line lesson |
| Introductory warm-up and "as everyone knows" phrases | Nothing |
| Adjectives and adverbs used for colour | Only those that change the meaning |
| | Cause and effect links, contrasts, conditions, conclusions |

Three tests for any sentence in your draft:

1. If I remove it, does the argument still stand? If yes, remove it.
2. Is it the author's point or mine? If mine, remove it.
3. Is it said in fewer words than the original? If not, rewrite it.

---

## 5. Common mistakes

| Mistake | Fix |
|---|---|
| Writing a summary of each sentence in turn | Work from a point outline, not line by line |
| Copying sentences and changing two words | Close the passage while drafting |
| Opening with "This passage is about..." or "The author says that..." | Start with the idea itself |
| Adding a comment ("which is very true in Pakistan today") | Report only |
| Two or three paragraphs | One paragraph, unless the passage is very long and the question allows more |
| Bullet points or headings | Continuous prose |
| Direct speech or quotation marks | Reported form |
| Questions and exclamations | Statements |
| First person ("I", "we", "our") | Third person |
| Mixed tenses | Choose one and keep it |
| Changing the order of ideas without reason | Follow the author's order. Reorder only to join two scattered halves of one point |
| Giving one small example more space than the main claim | Keep the author's proportions |
| No title, or a sentence as a title | A short noun phrase |
| No word count, or a false one | Count, and write it |
| Being 40% or 50% of the length | Cut to one-third |
| Abbreviations, contractions, slang | Formal English: *do not*, not *don't* |

## 6. Marking rubric

FPSC does not publish a marking scheme. This rubric is built from the syllabus split (15 + 5) and the examiners' comments. Use it to mark every practice précis.

| Criterion | Marks | Full marks | Half marks | Zero |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Central idea | 4 | Stated clearly and correctly | Present but blurred or partly wrong | Missed |
| Coverage of main points | 4 | All main points present, in proportion | One point missing or one minor point inflated | Several missing |
| Own words and compression | 3 | No lifted sentences; within 5 words of target | Some lifted phrases, or 10–20% off target | Mostly copied, or far over length |
| Form | 2 | One paragraph, third person, one tense, no speech or questions, nothing added | One slip | Several slips |
| Language | 2 | No errors of grammar, spelling, punctuation | 1–2 minor errors | Frequent errors |
| **Précis subtotal** | **15** | | | |
| Title: accuracy | 3 | Names the central theme of the whole passage | Covers part of it, or too broad | Wrong or missing |
| Title: form | 2 | Short phrase, capitalised | Too long or a sentence | Missing |
| **Total** | **20** | | | |

How to read your score: 12 or more is a strong précis. Real CSS marking is strict, and 8–10 out of 20 is a normal good mark. If you score yourself 14 on this rubric, expect about 9–11 in the exam.

### Example 1. Argument in three paragraphs

The habit of reading is one of the few possessions that cannot be taken from a person once it has been acquired. Money may be lost, health may fail, and friends may move away, but the man who has learned to find company in books is never wholly alone. Yet the habit is not formed by accident. It grows, like any other habit, from small and regular acts. A child who sees books in the house, who is read to at night, and who is allowed to choose what he reads, comes to think of reading as a pleasure and not as a task. A child who meets books only in the classroom, where every page is followed by a test, learns to connect them with anxiety, and he drops them as soon as the last examination is over.

This is why so many educated people do not read. They can read, in the narrow sense that they can turn printed marks into words, but they have never read for themselves. They look at headlines, messages and notices all day long, and they believe that this is reading. It is not. Real reading asks for time and stillness. It asks the reader to follow a long argument, to hold a character in mind for three hundred pages, and to wait for a meaning that does not arrive in the first line. These are exactly the powers that a hurried age is losing.

The loss matters beyond the private pleasure of the individual. A people that cannot read at length cannot think at length. It will prefer slogans to reasons, because slogans are short. It will judge public questions by the feeling of the moment, because it has no patience for evidence. Those who care about the quality of public life should therefore care about the humble habit of reading, and should begin where the habit begins: with children, at home, and with books freely chosen.

**Words in passage:** 325. **Target:** about one-third.

**Main idea:** The reading habit, formed in childhood, is a lasting good for the individual and a condition of sound public thinking.

**Point outline:**

1. Reading habit is a permanent possession and comfort.
2. It is formed early: books at home and free choice create pleasure; books tied only to tests create dislike.
3. So many educated adults only scan short texts; they do not really read.
4. Real reading needs time, attention, patience, which the age is losing.
5. Social cost: people who cannot read at length cannot reason at length; they follow slogans and moods.
6. So public life depends on forming the habit in children.

**What was cut and why:**

- "Money may be lost, health may fail, friends may move away": three illustrations of one idea (permanence). Kept as "lasting".
- The three details about the child (books in the house, read to at night, allowed to choose): reduced to "at home" and "freely".
- "Turn printed marks into words", "headlines, messages and notices": examples of shallow reading. Kept as "scan short texts".
- "Three hundred pages", "the first line": colour.
- "It is not." A rhetorical beat. Dropped.

**Title:** The Value of the Reading Habit

Reading, once it becomes a habit, is a lasting possession that keeps a person company for life. The habit grows from early and regular experience: children who meet books at home and choose them freely learn to enjoy reading, while those who meet books only as examination material soon abandon them. Many educated adults therefore merely scan short texts and never read seriously. Serious reading needs time, attention and patience, which a hurried age is losing. This loss harms society as well as the individual, since people who cannot read at length cannot reason at length and are easily led by slogans and passing emotion. Sound public life thus depends on encouraging children to read.

**Words in précis:** 115

### Example 2. Old prose (Francis Bacon, "Of Studies", 1625; spelling modernised)

Older prose is dense already. The task is to find the plain meaning under the balanced phrases, not to shorten each phrase in turn.

Studies serve for delight, for ornament, and for ability. Their chief use for delight is in privateness and retiring; for ornament, is in discourse; and for ability, is in the judgment and disposition of business. For expert men can execute, and perhaps judge of particulars, one by one; but the general counsels, and the plots and marshalling of affairs, come best from those that are learned. To spend too much time in studies is sloth; to use them too much for ornament is affectation; to make judgment wholly by their rules is the humour of a scholar. They perfect nature, and are perfected by experience: for natural abilities are like natural plants, that need pruning by study; and studies themselves do give forth directions too much at large, except they be bounded in by experience. Crafty men condemn studies, simple men admire them, and wise men use them; for they teach not their own use; but that is a wisdom without them, and above them, won by observation. Read not to contradict and confute; nor to believe and take for granted; nor to find talk and discourse; but to weigh and consider. Some books are to be tasted, others to be swallowed, and some few to be chewed and digested; that is, some books are to be read only in parts; others to be read, but not curiously; and some few to be read wholly, and with diligence and attention. Some books also may be read by deputy, and extracts made of them by others; but that would be only in the less important arguments, and the meaner sort of books; else distilled books are like common distilled waters, flashy things. Reading maketh a full man; conference a ready man; and writing an exact man. And therefore, if a man write little, he had need have a great memory; if he confer little, he had need have a present wit; and if he read little, he had need have much cunning, to seem to know that he doth not.

**Words in passage:** 339

**Main idea:** Study gives pleasure, polish and practical judgment, but only when it is used in moderation, tested by experience, and matched to the worth of the book.

**Point outline:**

1. Three uses of study: private pleasure, better conversation, sounder judgment in affairs.
2. Learned people plan large matters better than those with experience only.
3. Excess is a fault in each use: laziness, showing off, bookish judgment.
4. Study improves natural ability; experience must correct study.
5. The wise use learning; its right use is learned by observation, not from books.
6. Read to weigh and consider, not to argue, accept blindly, or show off.
7. Books differ: sample some, read some quickly, study a few closely. Summaries only for minor books.
8. Reading gives knowledge, discussion gives quickness, writing gives accuracy.

**What was cut and why:**

- The plant and pruning image, the "distilled waters" image, "tasted, swallowed, chewed": metaphors. Only the literal sense is kept.
- "Crafty men... simple men...": two of the three types are contrast only. Kept "the wise use learning".
- The last sentence (what a man needs if he writes, confers or reads little) restates the sentence before it from the negative side. Dropped.
- Archaic words replaced: "conference" = discussion; "curiously" = carefully; "present wit" = quick mind.

**Title:** The Right Use of Study

Study serves three purposes: private enjoyment, graceful conversation, and sound judgment in practical affairs, for the learned plan large matters better than those with experience alone. Each use becomes a fault in excess, producing idleness, vanity, or bookish judgment. Study improves natural ability, but must itself be corrected by experience, and the wise learn its proper use through observation. One should read to weigh ideas, not to dispute, to accept blindly, or to display. Books deserve different treatment: some need only sampling, others a quick reading, and a few close study, while summaries suit only minor works. Reading supplies knowledge, discussion gives readiness, and writing gives precision.

**Words in précis:** 107

### Example 3. Cause and effect, with a list

When a city grows hot, its people usually blame the weather. They should look instead at what the city has done to itself. A street lined with old trees can be several degrees cooler at noon than a bare street a few hundred metres away. The reason is simple. Leaves shade the ground, so that the road and the walls do not store the heat of the day and give it back at night. Trees also draw water from the soil and release it into the air, and this cools the air in the same way that sweat cools the skin. No machine does this work so cheaply or so quietly.

The benefits do not end with temperature. Trees catch dust and smoke on their leaves. Their roots hold the soil and let rainwater sink into the ground instead of rushing into drains that cannot carry it. Birds return to streets where there are branches to nest in. People walk more, and stay longer in the open, where there is shade to walk in, and shopkeepers on shaded streets do better business. Doctors have even found that patients who can see trees from a hospital window recover a little faster than those who look out on a wall.

In spite of all this, trees are the first thing to be removed when a road is widened or a plaza is built, and the last thing to be put back. The reason is that their value does not appear in any budget. The cost of cutting a tree is written down; the cost of living without it is paid slowly, by everyone, in electricity bills, in floods and in illness, and nobody adds it up. A city that wishes to remain fit to live in must begin to count its trees as it counts its roads and pipes: as public works that cost money to build, that take decades to replace, and that no sensible government would destroy without a very good reason.

**Words in passage:** 332

**Main idea:** Trees are cheap and vital urban infrastructure, yet cities destroy them because their value is never counted.

**Point outline:**

1. Urban heat is largely man-made; trees cool streets by shade and by releasing moisture, more cheaply than any machine.
2. Other benefits: cleaner air, less flooding, wildlife, more outdoor life and trade, better health.
3. Yet trees are cut first and replaced last, because their value is not in any budget; the cost of losing them is spread and hidden.
4. Cities should treat trees as public works, costly to replace, not to be destroyed lightly.

**What was cut and why:**

- "Several degrees cooler", "a few hundred metres": detail of the example.
- The sweat comparison: a simile.
- The list of benefits (dust, soil, rain, birds, walking, shopkeepers, hospital patients): seven items reduced to five class words.
- "Electricity bills, floods and illness": examples of the hidden cost. Kept as "hidden costs borne gradually by everyone".

**Title:** Trees as Neglected Urban Infrastructure

The heat of cities is largely self-made, and trees are the cheapest remedy. They cool streets by shading surfaces that would otherwise store heat and by releasing moisture into the air. They also clean the air, reduce flooding, shelter wildlife, encourage outdoor life and trade, and even aid recovery from illness. Nevertheless, trees are the first casualty of construction and the last thing restored, because their worth appears in no budget, while the price of their absence is paid gradually and invisibly by everyone. A city that wishes to stay habitable should therefore value trees as public works, slow to replace and not to be destroyed without strong reason.

**Words in précis:** 109

### Example 4. A first-person account (convert to third person, past tense)

I spent the first week of my posting in a small district town doing what every new officer does: I sat behind a large desk and waited for files to come to me. They came in heaps, each tied with red tape, each marked urgent. I read them carefully, wrote careful notes on them, and sent them on. By Friday I was pleased with myself. I had cleared more than a hundred files, and my desk was empty.

On Saturday an old clerk, who had served eleven officers before me, asked whether I would like to see the town. We walked for three hours. He showed me the hospital where the only doctor had not been paid for four months, the school whose roof had fallen in during the last monsoon, and the canal that had been "under repair", according to my files, for six years. Not one of these things had reached my desk. The files I had cleared so proudly were about transfers, allowances and the purchase of furniture.

I learned that day a lesson which I have tried not to forget. A file is not the district; it is only what somebody has chosen to write down about the district, and people do not readily write down their own failures. An officer who governs from his desk governs a town that exists on paper. The real town, with its unpaid doctor and its broken roof, lies outside his window, and it will not come to him. He must go to it. Since then I have made it a rule, wherever I have served, to spend at least one day in every week away from my office, on foot if possible, and to ask the people I meet what they need before I ask my staff what has been done. I have often been told that this is not the proper work of a senior officer. I have never found a better way of knowing what my proper work is.

**Words in passage:** 332

**Main idea:** An officer learned that files do not show real conditions, and that a good administrator must go out and see for himself.

**Point outline:**

1. As a new officer he worked only on files and was proud of clearing them.
2. A veteran clerk took him round the town; he saw serious neglected problems that no file mentioned; the files dealt with routine office matters.
3. Lesson: files record only what people choose to report, not failures; desk-bound rule is rule on paper.
4. He adopted the practice of weekly field visits and asking people directly, despite criticism, and found it the best guide to his duty.

**What was cut and why:**

- "Large desk", "red tape", "marked urgent", "more than a hundred files", "eleven officers": narrative colour and numbers.
- Hospital, school, canal: three examples. Kept as "serious neglected problems".
- "On foot if possible": detail.
- The first person throughout: changed to "the writer" and "he", past tense.

**Title:** Governing Beyond the Files

As a newly posted district officer, the writer spent his first week disposing of files and felt satisfied with his efficiency. A veteran clerk then took him through the town, where he found grave public problems that no file had mentioned, the files being confined to routine office matters. He realised that files contain merely what officials choose to record, and seldom their failures, so that an administrator confined to his desk governs only on paper. He therefore made it his practice to spend one day each week in the field, questioning ordinary people before his staff. Although criticised as improper for a senior officer, this habit proved his best guide to his real duty.

**Words in précis:** 115

### Example 5. Argument by contrast and example

We have come to admire the busy man without asking what he is busy about. A full diary is taken as proof of an important life, and a person who admits that he spent an afternoon doing nothing is thought to be confessing a fault. This is a curious superstition. Activity is not a virtue in itself. A man who digs a hole and fills it again has been active all day and has achieved nothing; a general who marches his army briskly in the wrong direction has done worse than nothing. What matters is not how much we do but whether what we do is worth doing, and that is a question which can only be answered by someone who has stopped doing things long enough to think.

Most of the ideas that have changed the world came to people who were, at the moment, apparently idle: walking in a garden, sitting under a tree, lying awake at night. This is not an accident. The mind needs empty time in order to join together things that it has gathered in busy time. A student who reads for ten hours and never pauses to ask what he has read will remember less than one who reads for six and thinks for one. A manager who runs from meeting to meeting will take many decisions and few good ones.

None of this is a defence of laziness. The idle man who never works has nothing in his mind to join together, and his leisure is as empty as the busy man's labour. The point is that work and rest are partners. Rest gives direction to work, and work gives material to rest. A society that honours only the first, and treats every quiet hour as wasted, will be full of energetic people going nowhere in particular, and will be surprised, after all its effort, to find how little it has to show.

**Words in passage:** 321

**Main idea:** Busyness is not a virtue in itself; reflection in leisure gives work its direction, and the two must go together.

**Point outline:**

1. Society wrongly admires busyness and treats idleness as a fault.
2. Activity has value only if its aim is worthwhile, and judging that needs a pause for thought.
3. Great ideas arise in idle moments because the mind needs free time to connect what it has gathered; ceaseless work or study yields less.
4. This is not praise of laziness: leisure without work is empty too.
5. Work and rest are partners; a society that values only activity achieves little.

**What was cut and why:**

- The hole-digger and the general: examples of pointless activity.
- Garden, tree, lying awake: examples of idle moments.
- The student (ten hours, six hours) and the manager: examples. Kept as one general clause.
- "A curious superstition", "going nowhere in particular": rhetoric.

**Title:** The Partnership of Work and Leisure

Modern society admires busyness for its own sake and regards idleness as a fault. Yet activity has value only when its purpose is worthwhile, and that can be judged only by those who pause to reflect. Great ideas have usually come in idle moments, because the mind needs unoccupied time to connect what it has collected while busy; unbroken study or work therefore yields poorer results. This is no defence of laziness, since leisure without labour is equally empty. Work and rest are partners: rest directs work, and work supplies matter for reflection. A society that honours activity alone will spend great energy and achieve little.

**Words in précis:** 106

### Example 6. Abstract argument about politics

It is commonly said that a nation is strong when its people are united, and from this it is hastily concluded that a nation is strongest when its people all think alike. The conclusion does not follow. Unity of purpose is one thing; uniformity of opinion is another, and the second is more often a sign of weakness than of strength. Where everyone agrees, either the question is trivial or someone is afraid to speak.

Consider how any group arrives at a sound decision. A proposal is made. Somebody objects to it. The proposer answers, or fails to answer, and the proposal is improved, or dropped. It is the objection that does the useful work. Remove the objector, and the first idea that occurs to the most powerful person in the room becomes the policy of the whole, with all its faults untested. Governments that silence their critics do not thereby become wiser. They merely lose the only people who would have told them, in time, that they were wrong, and they learn of their mistakes later, from events, at a much higher price.

It is true that disagreement is uncomfortable, and that it can be carried to a point where nothing is decided at all. A parliament that only quarrels is no better than one that only applauds. But the cure for too much quarrelling is not silence. It is a set of habits: the habit of attacking arguments rather than persons, of accepting a vote that has gone against one, and of remembering that an opponent may love his country as much as one does oneself. Where these habits exist, disagreement is the method by which a free people thinks. Where they do not, no amount of enforced agreement will supply their place, for a silence that has been imposed proves nothing except the power of whoever imposed it.

**Words in passage:** 311

**Main idea:** National strength needs unity of purpose, not uniform opinion; open disagreement, conducted with good habits, is how a free people reaches sound decisions.

**Point outline:**

1. Unity is confused with uniformity; total agreement signals triviality or fear.
2. Sound decisions come from objections that test and improve proposals.
3. Governments that silence critics lose early warning and learn from costly events.
4. Disagreement can be excessive, but the remedy is not silence; it is civil habits: debate ideas not persons, accept votes, respect opponents' loyalty.
5. With these, disagreement is collective thinking; imposed agreement proves only power.

**What was cut and why:**

- The step-by-step account of a proposal and an objection: an illustration of point 2.
- "The most powerful person in the room": detail.
- "A parliament that only quarrels... only applauds": an example of the concession.
- The repetition of the final idea in two forms: stated once.

**Title:** Disagreement as the Strength of a Free Nation

National strength requires unity of purpose, not uniformity of opinion, which usually indicates either a trivial question or fear. Sound decisions emerge when proposals are tested and improved by objection. Governments that suppress criticism therefore grow no wiser; they lose timely warning and discover their errors later, at greater cost. ### Example 7. Explanation of a concept

The popular picture of the scientist is of a person who knows. He stands in a white coat before a board covered with symbols and announces the truth. The picture is almost exactly the opposite of the fact. The scientist is, by profession, a person who doubts. What distinguishes him from other people is not the number of things he is sure of but the care with which he separates what he has good reason to believe from what he merely hopes or has been told.

This habit of doubt is not natural to us. Left to ourselves we believe what is pleasant, what is repeated often, and what is said by people we admire. Science is a set of devices for defeating these tendencies. An experiment must be described so exactly that a stranger can repeat it. A result is not accepted because a famous man reports it, but only when others, who would be glad to prove him wrong, have tried and failed. A theory is valued not for explaining everything but for running the risk of being refuted: it must say plainly what would count as evidence against it. Even then it is held only for the present, until something better is found.

People sometimes find this disappointing. They want certainty, and they are offered probability. They want final answers, and they are told that every answer is provisional. But the modesty of science is the source of its power. Systems of belief that claimed to be complete and final have stood still for centuries, because nothing in them could be corrected without bringing down the whole. Science has moved forward precisely because it expects to be wrong in detail and has built into itself the means of finding out where. To teach science as a list of established facts, to be learned and repeated, is therefore to miss the point of it. What should be taught first is the method: how to ask a clear question, how to test an answer, and how to change one's mind.

**Words in passage:** 341

**Main idea:** Science is a disciplined method of doubt, and its willingness to be corrected is the source of its progress; it should be taught as a method, not as fixed facts.

**Point outline:**

1. The scientist is not one who knows but one who doubts and separates grounded belief from mere hope or hearsay.
2. Humans naturally believe what is pleasant, repeated or authoritative; science has safeguards: repeatable experiments, independent checking, theories that can be refuted, provisional acceptance.
3. This lack of certainty disappoints, but it is science's strength: closed systems stagnate, science advances by finding its own errors.
4. So science should be taught as a method of questioning, testing and revising.

**What was cut and why:**

- "They want certainty... they want final answers": the same idea twice.
- "For centuries", "bringing down the whole": elaboration.

**Title:** Doubt as the Method of Science

Contrary to popular belief, the scientist is distinguished not by certainty but by disciplined doubt, carefully separating well-grounded belief from hope and hearsay. Many find this absence of certainty disappointing, yet it is the source of scientific power. Belief systems claiming finality stagnate because they cannot be corrected, whereas science progresses by expecting error and possessing the means to detect it. Science should therefore be taught not as a body of fixed facts but as a method of asking, testing and revising.

### Example 8. A passage built on a list of causes

A child may now spend more hours a day listening to a telephone than listening to his grandmother, and the telephone does not speak the language of the valley.

What is lost then is more than a set of words. ### Example 9. Rhetorical style: questions and repetition

What has happened to our attention? Thirty years ago a person waiting for a bus simply waited. He looked at the street, he thought his own thoughts, he was sometimes bored. Today the same person takes out a telephone before he has reached the bus stop, and in the four minutes before the bus arrives he will look at a dozen different things and remember none of them. Is he better informed? Is he happier? Is he even entertained? He would find it hard to say.

The devices we carry were not designed to inform us. They were designed to hold us, because the companies that make them are paid according to the number of minutes we spend looking. Every colour, every sound, every little red number on the screen has been tested on millions of people to discover which version is hardest to ignore. We are not weak when we fail to put the telephone down. We are outnumbered. On the other side of the glass are thousands of clever people whose whole work is to make sure that we do not.

And what do we pay for this free entertainment? We pay with the one thing that cannot be bought back. Attention is not merely a tool for getting things done. It is the stuff of which a life is made. What we attend to, hour after hour, is what we will have lived. A man who gives his evenings to his children has had one kind of life; a man who gives them to a stream of strangers' quarrels has had another, and he did not exactly choose it. It was chosen for him, a few seconds at a time.

The answer is not to throw the telephone into the river. It is a useful servant. The answer is to decide, before we pick it up, what we are picking it up for, and to put it down when that is done. This sounds easy. It is one of the hardest disciplines of the age, and one of the most necessary.

**Words in passage:** 344

**Main idea:** Digital devices are designed to capture attention for profit; since attention is the substance of life, people must use such devices deliberately.

1. People now fill every idle moment with their phones, gaining little.
2. Devices are engineered to hold users, because companies profit from time spent; failure to resist is not mere personal weakness.
3. The price is attention, which is irreplaceable and determines the quality of one's life; it is being spent without real choice.

- The bus-stop scene: an anecdote. One clause keeps its point.
- Colours, sounds, red numbers, "thousands of clever people": detail of the design process.
- The two men and their evenings: an example of point 3.
- "Throw the telephone into the river": a figure.

**Title:** The Cost of Captured Attention

People now fill every idle moment with their telephones, yet gain little knowledge, pleasure or contentment from doing so. These devices are deliberately engineered to hold users, since their makers profit from the time spent on them; failure to resist is therefore less a personal weakness than an unequal contest. The price paid is attention, which cannot be recovered and which determines the very content of a person's life. Much of it is now surrendered in small portions, without genuine choice. The remedy is not to abandon such useful instruments but to use them only for a decided purpose and then set them aside, a discipline that is both extremely difficult and essential.

### Example 10. Historical narrative

Before the middle of the fifteenth century every book in Europe was written by hand. A single copy of a long work might take a scribe a year, and it cost as much as a small farm. Books were therefore owned by monasteries, universities and princes, and the ordinary man, even if he could read, might pass his whole life without holding one. Knowledge travelled slowly and changed as it travelled, for every copyist added his own mistakes.

The printing press altered this within a single lifetime. By setting movable metal letters in a frame, inking them and pressing them on paper, a workshop could produce in a week more pages than a scribe produced in years, and every copy was the same. The first printers thought of themselves as tradesmen supplying a known demand for Bibles and grammars. They did not foresee what followed. Within fifty years there were presses in more than two hundred towns and millions of books in circulation. Prices fell so far that a merchant or a craftsman could own a shelf of them.

The results went far beyond convenience. Scholars in different countries could now refer to the same page of the same edition and so correct and build upon each other's work; modern science is hard to imagine without this. Reformers could put their arguments directly before the public in cheap pamphlets, and rulers and churches found that an idea, once printed, could not be recalled. Languages that had existed in a hundred local forms began to settle into standard spelling and grammar, because printers needed to sell the same book across a wide region. Even the idea of childhood changed, as reading became a skill that had to be learned at school before one could enter the adult world.

No one planned these changes. They followed from a mechanical improvement in the copying of texts. It is worth remembering this when we are told that a new means of communication is only a faster way of doing what was done before.

**Words in passage:** 339

**Main idea:** The printing press, a mechanical improvement, made books cheap and uniform and thereby transformed scholarship, religion, politics, language and education in ways nobody planned.

**Point outline:**

1. Before printing, books were hand-copied, slow, costly, error-prone, confined to institutions and the rich.
2. The press produced fast, identical, cheap copies; within fifty years it spread widely and books reached ordinary people.
3. Unforeseen effects: cumulative scholarship and science; public spread of reforming ideas beyond control of authority; standardised languages; schooling as the gateway to adult life.
4. Lesson: a new medium is never merely a faster version of the old.

**What was cut and why:**

- "A year", "a small farm", "two hundred towns", "millions": figures. Kept as "slow", "costly", "rapidly".
- How the press works (metal letters, ink, frame): process detail not needed for the argument.
- "Bibles and grammars", "merchant or craftsman": examples.
- The narrative is in the past tense, so the précis is in the past tense. The final lesson is a general truth and takes the present.

**Title:** The Unforeseen Revolution of Printing

Until the mid-fifteenth century, European books were copied by hand, a slow, costly and inaccurate process that confined them to institutions and the wealthy. The printing press changed this within a generation by producing identical copies quickly and cheaply, so that presses multiplied and books reached ordinary people. The consequences, which the first printers never foresaw, were profound. Scholars could build on a shared text, making modern science possible; reformers addressed the public directly, and authorities could no longer suppress ideas; languages acquired standard forms; and schooling became the gateway to adult life. These unplanned changes show that a new medium of communication is never merely a faster version of the old.

### Example 11. A passage with statistics

A country that received about five thousand cubic metres of water for each citizen at the time of its independence now has less than one thousand. Nothing has happened to the rivers. They carry roughly what they carried seventy years ago. What has changed is the number of people who must share them, which has grown more than sixfold, and the way in which the water is used.

More than nine-tenths of the water drawn from the rivers goes to agriculture, and a large part of that never reaches a plant. It leaks from unlined canals, evaporates from flooded fields, and is poured onto crops such as rice and sugar cane in quantities that the same crops do not receive in countries with far less water to spare. A farmer at the head of a canal, who pays a small flat charge whatever he uses, has no reason to save a drop; a farmer at the tail often receives nothing at all and must pump from the ground. Groundwater, in turn, is being taken out much faster than rain can replace it, and in many districts the water table falls by a metre or more each year.

Cities add their own waste. Pipes laid generations ago lose a third of what is put into them, and almost none of the water that is used is cleaned and used again. Meanwhile the country can store only about thirty days of its river flow, so that the floods of one season run to the sea and the drought of the next finds the reservoirs empty.

The usual response is to call for great new dams, and storage is certainly needed. But storage alone is like a larger tank on a house whose taps are all left running. The cheaper and quicker gains lie in using less: lining canals, pricing water by volume, shifting to crops and methods that need less of it, repairing city pipes and recycling waste water. These measures are less visible than a dam and win fewer votes. They are also the only ones that can be completed in time.

**Words in passage:** 351

**Main idea:** The country's water crisis comes from population growth and wasteful use rather than from less water, and efficiency measures, not only new dams, are the practical remedy.

**Point outline:**

1. Water available per person has fallen sharply; river flows are unchanged; population has multiplied and use is wasteful.
2. Agriculture takes most water and wastes much: leaking canals, flooding, thirsty crops, flat pricing, unequal distribution; groundwater is over-pumped.
3. Cities lose water through old pipes and do not recycle; storage capacity is small.
4. New dams are needed but insufficient; quicker, cheaper gains come from conservation: lining canals, volumetric pricing, less thirsty crops, pipe repair, recycling. These are politically unattractive but timely.

**What was cut and why:**

- "Five thousand", "one thousand", "sixfold", "nine-tenths", "a third", "thirty days", "a metre": figures become "sharply", "most", "much", "little". None of the numbers is itself the point.
- Head-of-canal and tail-end farmers: an example of bad pricing and unequal supply.
- The tank and taps comparison: an analogy.
- "Win fewer votes": kept as "politically unattractive", because it explains why the measures are neglected.

**Title:** Water Scarcity: Waste, Not Shortage

Water available per person in the country has fallen drastically since independence, not because the rivers have shrunk but because the population has multiplied and water is used wastefully. Agriculture consumes most of the supply and loses much of it through leaking canals, flood irrigation, thirsty crops and flat charges that reward overuse, while tail-end farmers exhaust the groundwater. Cities lose much through decayed pipes and recycle almost nothing, and storage capacity is very small. New dams are necessary but insufficient. Faster and cheaper relief lies in conservation: lining canals, charging by volume, changing crops and methods, repairing pipes and reusing waste water. Though politically unattractive, only such measures can be achieved in time.

### Example 12. Dense reflective prose with a concession

There are two opposite errors about the bringing up of children, and most of us commit each of them in turn. The first is to suppose that a child is clay, to be pressed into whatever shape the parent or the teacher has decided upon. Those who hold this view fill the day with rules, punish every departure from them, and are proud of the quiet and orderly result. They do not notice that the quiet is the quiet of fear, and that a child who has never been allowed to choose anything will, as a man, either be unable to choose at all or will choose wildly as soon as the hand is removed.

The second error is the natural reaction against the first. It is to suppose that a child is a seed, which contains its whole future within itself and needs only to be left alone. Those who hold this view make no rules, correct nothing, and call the result freedom. But a child is not born knowing that fire burns, that other people have feelings, or that some pleasures are followed by pain. Left wholly to himself he does not grow into his best self; he grows into the creature of his strongest impulse, or of the strongest child in the playground.

The truth lies between, though not at a fixed point between. A child needs both a fence and a field: firm limits on the few things that matter greatly, such as honesty, kindness and safety, and wide liberty in the many things that matter little, such as how he plays, what he reads and which of several good roads he takes. The limits should be explained as soon as he is able to understand reasons, and they should be widened as he shows that he can be trusted, until at last they are withdrawn and he governs himself. The whole art lies in the timing. Discipline that comes too late has to be harsh; freedom that comes too late is not known how to be used. The wise parent is the one who is always, a little reluctantly, letting go.

2. Second error: leaving the child entirely free; he lacks necessary knowledge and becomes ruled by impulse or by stronger children.

### Practice 1

It is often claimed that games build character, and the claim is usually made by people who were good at games. It deserves to be examined rather than simply repeated. What a boy or girl learns on a playing field depends very largely on how the game is played and on what the adults around it choose to praise.

At its best, sport teaches things that are hard to teach in a classroom. A player learns to keep trying when the score is against him, to obey a rule even when the referee is not looking, to accept a decision that he believes to be wrong, and to shake hands with the opponent who has beaten him. He learns that his own success depends on others, and that the most brilliant individual will lose to a side that works together. These are not small lessons. A person who has truly learned them will be a better colleague, a better citizen and a better loser in the larger contests of life.

But the same game can teach exactly the opposite. Where winning is the only thing that is rewarded, children quickly discover that a hidden foul, a little time-wasting, and a loud appeal for a decision they know to be false are all useful. Where one or two gifted players receive all the attention, the rest learn that they do not matter, and many of them give up exercise for life. Where parents shout abuse from the boundary, children learn that an opponent is an enemy.

The game itself is neutral. It is a small world with clear rules, in which habits of every kind can be practised and made strong. Those who organise sport for the young should therefore worry less about the trophy and more about the habits. They should praise effort and fairness as loudly as victory, see that every child plays, and treat a dishonest win as a defeat. Otherwise they are building character indeed, but not the kind they intended.

### Practice 2

An examination is supposed to be a measuring instrument, like a thermometer. It is meant to tell us how much a student knows without changing what he knows. In practice it behaves quite differently. ### Practice 5

The words patriotism and nationalism are often used as if they meant the same thing, but it is useful to keep them apart, for they describe two different states of mind. A healthy public life needs the first feeling and is endangered by the second. The task of education is not to weaken the love of country, which is natural and good, but to keep it from hardening into contempt for everyone outside it.

### Practice 6

A newspaper has two different things to offer its readers, and its honesty depends on keeping them apart. The first is news: an account of what has happened. The second is opinion: a view of what ought to be done about it. Both are legitimate, and a paper without opinions would be a dull thing. The danger begins when the second is dressed up as the first.

This can be done without printing a single false statement. An editor chooses which of a thousand daily events to report and which to ignore. He decides what goes on the front page under a large headline and what is buried at the bottom of page nine. He selects the adjective: the same crowd may be described as a gathering, a protest or a mob. He picks which of two speakers to quote at length and which to dismiss in a line. A reader who sees only the result believes that he is being told what happened. He is in fact being told what to think, and he is the more easily led because he does not know it.

The pressures on an editor to behave in this way are strong. Owners have business interests and political friends. Advertisers can withdraw their money. Governments can grant or refuse access and licences. And readers themselves reward the paper that confirms what they already believe; a paper that told its public unwelcome truths every morning would soon have a smaller public.

Against all this there is no complete defence, but there are two partial ones. The first lies with the profession: a paper worth reading states its opinions openly on a page reserved for them, corrects its mistakes prominently, and gives a fair hearing to the side it opposes. The second lies with the reader. He should read more than one paper, notice what each leaves out, and distrust most the reports that please him most. A free press does not mean a press free from bias. It means that there are enough voices for the careful reader to find the truth between them.

### Practice 8

Conversation is the one art that everybody practises and hardly anybody studies. We are taught to read and to write, and sometimes to make speeches, but it is assumed that we shall pick up the art of talking with one another as we pick up walking. Most of us pick it up badly.

### Practice 9

### Practice 10

History, as it is taught in most schools, is a procession of kings, battles and dates, arranged so as to show that one's own nation has always been brave, wise and wronged. This discipline is exactly what the citizen of a modern state requires. To teach history as inquiry is not to teach contempt for one's country. A nation's real achievements survive examination, and a pride that depends on concealing the record is not worth having. ### Practice 12

Tolerance is commonly praised and seldom understood. It is confused, on one side, with indifference, and on the other with approval. The indifferent man lets his neighbour worship as he pleases because he does not care how anybody worships; that is not tolerance, for it costs him nothing. The man who approves of his neighbour's opinions does not tolerate them either; he shares them. Tolerance begins only where there is real disagreement. To tolerate is to believe that another person is wrong, perhaps seriously wrong, on a matter that one thinks important, and nevertheless to leave him free to hold his belief, to state it and to live by it.

Why should anyone do so difficult a thing? Not because all opinions are equally true. That doctrine, though often offered as the ground of tolerance, would actually destroy it, since if no opinion were better than another there would be nothing to disagree about. The better reasons are three. The first is that we may be mistaken ourselves. Every age has been certain of things that the next age found absurd, and it is unlikely that ours is the single exception. The second is that belief cannot be compelled. Force can produce silence, or a show of agreement, but a conviction that is merely imposed is not a conviction. The third is that the alternative is war. Where each group tries to enforce its own truth, the result is not the victory of truth but the victory of the strongest, after much blood.

Tolerance has, however, a limit, and it is important to place it correctly. It does not extend to those who would use their freedom to destroy the freedom of others. A society may tolerate the preaching of almost any doctrine; it cannot tolerate the burning of the opponent's meeting-hall. The line falls between word and violence, between persuading a man and silencing him. ### Practice 13

For most of history a person's private life was protected less by law than by the sheer difficulty of finding things out. ### Practice 15

The phrase "the rule of law" is repeated so often that it has almost ceased to mean anything. Many who use it mean only that there should be order, and that people should obey the government. That is a serious mistake. A country in which the ruler's commands are promptly obeyed has rule by law, which is a very different thing. Tyrants have always been fond of laws; they find them a convenient way of giving orders.

The rule of law means, first, that the law binds those who make and enforce it as firmly as it binds everyone else. The minister, the general and the policeman must be able to point to a legal authority for what they do, and must answer in the ordinary courts if they exceed it. It means, secondly, that the law is general and known in advance. A citizen must be able to discover, before he acts, what is permitted and what is not; he must not be punished under a rule invented for his case after the event. And it means, thirdly, that disputes are decided by judges who are independent of the parties, including the most powerful party of all, the state itself.

Where these conditions hold, something valuable follows that has nothing to do with the content of any particular law. People can plan. The trader can sign a contract in the belief that it will be enforced, the farmer can improve his land in the belief that it will not be seized, and the writer can publish in the knowledge of where the limits lie. Liberty and prosperity both grow from this ability to rely on the future.

The rule of law is therefore not a gift that rulers grant. It is a restraint that they accept, usually unwillingly, and it survives only where citizens insist upon it: where judges are respected for deciding against the government, where lawyers defend the unpopular, and where the public regards an official who places himself above the law, however good his intentions, as a danger and not as a hero. A nation that admires the strong man who cuts through legal difficulties will not long have any law to protect it from him.

**Points:** (1) the writer arrived lost, injured, a stranger, expecting little; (2) he was fed, housed, treated, and refused payment; the whole village shared the duty for four days; (3) the custom is a collective obligation to strangers, failure in which shames the village; (4) reflection: the city has services but no neighbourliness; the villagers had little but unfailing mutual help; he doubts the exchange was a gain.

Note the conversion: first person to "the writer", past tense throughout.

The writer, lost and injured after three days of walking, arrived as a complete stranger in a remote village, expecting little. Instead, an old man gave him food and his family's only bed, a neighbour treated his injury, and payment was refused. During the four days of his recovery the households fed him in turn, and he later learned that the villagers regarded hospitality to a traveller on foot as a collective duty whose neglect would disgrace them all. Reflecting on this in his city, he observed that urban people rely on hotels, insurance and emergency services, yet scarcely know their neighbours. The villagers, though poor, possessed an unfailing system of mutual help, and he doubted whether replacing neighbours with paid services had been a real gain.

### Answer 15

**Title:** The True Meaning of the Rule of Law

**Main idea:** The rule of law is not mere obedience to government but the subjection of rulers to general, known laws applied by independent judges; it gives security for planning and survives only if citizens insist on it.

**Points:** (1) the rule of law is confused with order and obedience, which is rule by law and suits tyrants; (2) three elements: law binds rulers and officials too; law is general and known beforehand; independent judges decide, even against the state; (3) benefit: people can rely on the future, so liberty and prosperity grow; (4) it is a restraint rulers accept unwillingly, kept alive by public insistence; admiration for a strong man above the law destroys it.

The rule of law is often mistaken for mere order and obedience to government, which is only rule by law. Properly understood, it has three elements: rulers and officials are bound by the law and answerable to ordinary courts; laws are general and known beforehand, so that nobody is punished under rules made afterwards; and disputes, including those involving the state, are settled by independent judges. Where these conditions exist, people can rely on the future, and liberty and prosperity grow from that security. The rule of law is not granted by rulers but is a restraint they accept reluctantly, and it endures only where citizens insist on it and distrust officials who set themselves above the law. A nation that admires the lawless strong man soon loses the law's protection against him.
