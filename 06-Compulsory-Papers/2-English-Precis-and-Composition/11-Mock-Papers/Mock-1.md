## PART II

**Q.2 Write a précis of the following passage and suggest a suitable title. (20)**

Those who govern us have developed a language of their own. A minister does not say that prices have risen; he says that the economy is undergoing an upward adjustment. An office does not refuse a request; it regrets that the matter cannot be acceded to at the present stage. A report does not admit that a project has failed; it observes that the desired outcomes have not been fully realised within the envisaged time frame. The words are English, but the citizen who reads them is no wiser than before, and that, very often, is the intention.

Obscure language serves the official in several ways. It hides bad news, since a failure described in long words does not sound like a failure. It hides responsibility, since the passive voice allows things to be done without anybody having done them: mistakes were made, funds were diverted, decisions were taken. It hides ignorance, since a writer who does not know what he thinks can always produce a paragraph that sounds as if he does. And it keeps the public at a distance, for a man who cannot understand a notice will hesitate to challenge it.

The cost of all this is paid by ordinary people. The farmer cannot follow the conditions of his loan. The patient cannot read the leaflet in the box. The taxpayer signs a form that he does not understand and is later fined for an error he could not have avoided. A government that writes in this way has not really informed its citizens at all; it has only gone through the motions of informing them.

Plain writing is therefore not a matter of style. It is a matter of honesty and of respect. To write so that one is understood at the first reading takes more thought, not less, because the writer must first be clear in his own mind. Officials should be trained to do it, and judged by whether the people they serve can understand what they have written.

**Q.3 Read the following passage carefully and answer the questions given at the end. (20)**

For most of human history nobody knew what time it was, and nobody needed to. The farmer rose with the light and stopped when it failed. He measured his day by tasks, not hours: the time it took to plough a field, to milk the cows, to walk to the next village. Work expanded and contracted with the seasons, and in the slack months there was a good deal of sitting about. When there was urgent work, as at harvest, he laboured from dawn to dark without complaint; when there was none, he did not pretend to be busy.

The mechanical clock, and after it the factory, altered this completely. A machine that costs money must be kept running, and it can be kept running only if everyone who tends it arrives at the same moment. The early mill-owners found their workers, who were farmers' sons, maddeningly irregular. They came late, went home when they had earned enough for the week, and took Monday as a holiday. It required bells, fines, locked gates and several generations of schooling to teach them that time was something that belonged to the employer and could be stolen from him. The school itself, with its timetable and its punishments for lateness, was largely designed for this purpose.

We are the heirs of that training, and we have learned the lesson so thoroughly that we no longer notice it. We speak of spending time, saving it and wasting it, as though it were money. We feel guilty when we are idle. We wear on our wrists the instrument by which our great-grandfathers were disciplined, and we consult it a hundred times a day of our own free will.

It would be absurd to wish the clock away; without it there would be no railways, no hospitals and no modern life at all. But it is worth remembering that punctuality is a recent habit and not a law of nature, and that a man who measures his life only in hours may find, at the end, that he has counted everything and enjoyed very little.

2. Why did factory owners need punctual workers, and what difficulties did they face with the first generation? (4)
3. What part did the school play in changing attitudes to time? (4)
4. What does the author mean by saying that we speak of time "as though it were money"? (4)
5. What is the author's final view of the clock and of punctuality? (4)

## Q.2

Passage: 335 words. Target: about one-third.

**Points:** (1) officials use an obscure language that leaves citizens uninformed, often on purpose; (2) it serves them by hiding failure, hiding responsibility (impersonal passive), hiding ignorance, and discouraging challenge; (3) ordinary people pay: they cannot understand loans, medical leaflets, forms, and are penalised; they are informed only in form; (4) plain writing is a matter of honesty and respect, needs clearer thought, and officials should be trained in it and judged by it.

**Title:** Plain Language as a Public Duty

Officials have developed an obscure language that leaves citizens uninformed, often deliberately. Such language serves them in several ways: it disguises failure, conceals responsibility through impersonal constructions, covers the writer's own ignorance, and discourages the public from questioning authority. Ordinary people bear the cost, for they cannot understand the terms of loans, medical instructions or official forms, and are penalised for unavoidable mistakes; they are thus informed only in name. Plain writing is consequently a question of honesty and respect, not of style. It demands clearer thinking from the writer, and officials should be trained in it and judged by whether the public understands them.

## Q.7

The secret of any nation's progress lies in hard work, honesty and discipline. The example of Japan is before us: it was devastated in the Second World War, yet within a few decades it emerged as one of the great economic powers of the world. We, on the other hand, though rich in natural resources, are crushed under a burden of debt. The reason is that we shirk hard work and look for the easy way in everything. Until we search our own hearts, conditions will not change.
