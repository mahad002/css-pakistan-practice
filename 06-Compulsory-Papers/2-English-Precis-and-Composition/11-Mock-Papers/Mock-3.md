## PART II

**Q.2 Write a précis of the following passage and suggest a suitable title. (20)**

The growth of knowledge has made specialists of us all. A physician of two centuries ago could carry in his head most of what was then known about the human body. Today no doctor can master more than a small corner of medicine, and the same is true of law, engineering and every science. This division of labour has brought enormous gains. The surgeon who performs one operation a thousand times does it better than a general practitioner could, and the engineer who thinks of nothing but bridges builds bridges that do not fall.

But specialisation has a price, which is seldom reckoned. The specialist knows more and more about less and less. Outside his own field he is as ignorant as any layman, and often more dangerous, because success in one subject has given him a confidence that he carries into others. A brilliant physicist may hold childish opinions about politics, and a great financier may believe nonsense about medicine. Worse, the great problems of a society do not arrive neatly divided into university departments. A decision to build a dam is at once a question of engineering, economics, law, agriculture and the lives of the people who will be moved. Each expert sees his own part clearly, and nobody sees the whole.

The remedy is not to have fewer specialists; we cannot do without them. It is to give every specialist, before he specialises, a broad general education: enough history to know how societies have gone wrong before, enough science to respect evidence, enough literature to imagine lives unlike his own. And it is to keep, in every large decision, a place for the person whose training is in none of the specialisms but in weighing them against one another. Such a person is sometimes despised as an amateur. He is in fact performing the most difficult task of all, and a state that has no one able to perform it will be governed by experts, each right in his own field, towards results that none of them intended.

**Q.3 Read the following passage carefully and answer the questions given at the end. (20)**

A rumour is a piece of news that travels without a passport. Nobody knows where it began, nobody can say who is responsible for it, and yet within a day a whole city may be repeating it. Students of the subject have found that rumours do not spread at random. They flourish under two conditions: when the matter is important to people, and when reliable information about it is lacking. Where either condition is absent, the rumour dies. Nobody spreads rumours about the price of bread in a country where the price is printed in every newspaper and never changes.

It follows that a rumour is less a lie than a question. When people are anxious and are told nothing, they do not simply wait. They put together such scraps as they have, a remark overheard, a lorry seen at night, a minister's cancelled visit, and they make a story of them, because a frightening story is easier to bear than no story at all. As the tale passes from mouth to mouth it changes in regular ways. Details that do not fit are dropped. What remains is sharpened and made more definite: "some" becomes "hundreds", and "it is said" becomes "my cousin saw". And the whole is bent to agree with what the tellers already feared or hoped.

Authorities usually respond in the worst possible manner. They deny the rumour without supplying the facts, or they forbid people to repeat it. A bare denial persuades nobody, since it is exactly what would be said if the rumour were true, and a prohibition is taken as proof. The only effective reply to a rumour is the thing whose absence produced it: prompt, full and verifiable information, including information that is unwelcome. A government that is known to tell the truth when the news is bad will be believed when it says that the news is good.

The telephone has not changed the nature of rumour, only its speed. What once took a day to cross a city now crosses a country in an hour. The cure remains the same, and it has become more urgent.

1. What does the author mean by calling a rumour "a piece of news that travels without a passport"? (4)
2. Under what two conditions do rumours flourish? Explain the example of the price of bread. (4)
3. Why does the author say that a rumour is "less a lie than a question"? (4)
4. In what regular ways does a rumour change as it is passed on? (4)
5. Why do official denials and bans fail, and what remedy does the author recommend? (4)

## Q.2

Passage: 341 words. Target: about one-third.

**Points:** (1) growth of knowledge forces specialisation, which brings great gains in skill; (2) the cost: specialists are ignorant and overconfident outside their field; public problems span many disciplines, so nobody sees the whole; (3) remedy: not fewer specialists but a broad general education before specialising, and a place in big decisions for the generalist who weighs expert views; (4) without such a person, a state is led by experts to unintended results.

**Title:** The Limits of Specialisation

The expansion of knowledge has compelled everyone to specialise, with great benefits, since concentrated practice produces superior skill. Specialisation, however, carries a neglected cost. Specialists are ignorant outside their own fields, and their success makes them overconfident there; moreover, major public questions involve many disciplines at once, so that each expert understands one aspect while none grasps the whole. The solution is not to reduce specialisation, which is indispensable, but to give all specialists a broad general education first and to reserve, in large decisions, a role for the generalist trained to weigh expert views against one another. Without such a person, a state will be led by experts to consequences nobody intended.

(Words: 113)

## Q.3

1. The author means that a rumour spreads freely without any proof of its origin or authority. Like a traveller with no papers, it cannot be traced to a source and nobody answers for it, yet it moves everywhere and is quickly repeated by a whole city.
2. Rumours flourish when a matter is important to people and when trustworthy information about it is missing. Bread is important, but where its price is published daily and stays the same, people already have the facts, so the second condition is absent and no rumour arises.
3. He says so because a rumour is the public's attempt to answer something it has not been told. Anxious people who receive no information join together small observations into a story, since even an alarming explanation is easier to bear than uncertainty.
4. A rumour changes in three ways. Details that do not suit the story are left out; what remains becomes more exact and more striking, with vague numbers and sources made definite; and the whole account is shaped to match the fears or hopes that the tellers already hold.
5. A bare denial fails because the authorities would deny the story even if it were true, and a ban is treated as confirmation. The author recommends quick, complete and checkable information, even when it is unpleasant, because a government known for honesty about bad news will be believed.

(i) **Allude** (to refer indirectly): In his speech he alluded to the mistakes of the previous government. **Elude** (to escape): The robbers eluded the police by changing cars.
(ii) **Antic** (a playful or foolish act): The monkey's antics amused the children. **Antique** (an old and valuable object): She bought an antique lamp in the old city.
(iii) **Ingenious** (clever, inventive): The engineer devised an ingenious method of saving water. **Ingenuous** (innocent, frank): The child's ingenuous question embarrassed the guests.
(iv) **Luxuriant** (growing thickly): The hills are covered with luxuriant forest after the rains. **Luxurious** (very comfortable and costly): The delegates stayed in a luxurious hotel.
(v) **Draught** (a current of air): Close the window; there is a cold draught. **Drought** (a long period without rain): Three years of drought turned the fields to dust.
(vi) **Persecute** (to treat cruelly for one's beliefs): No one should be persecuted for his faith. **Prosecute** (to bring before a court): The company was prosecuted for polluting the river.
(vii) **Credible** (believable): The police found his explanation credible. **Credulous** (too ready to believe): Credulous villagers gave their savings to the fake saint.
(viii) **Wreak** (to cause): The floods wreaked havoc in the valley. **Reek** (to smell strongly and unpleasantly): The room reeked of stale tobacco.

## Q.7

Corruption is eating into our society like termites. From the peon to the senior officer, bribery flourishes at every level. People have to grease palms even for lawful work; otherwise their applications are tossed from office to office for months. The pity of it is that now neither the taker of a bribe feels any shame nor the giver. This festering sore will not be healed merely by making laws. What is needed is that accountability should be without discrimination and that the big fish, too, should be caught.
