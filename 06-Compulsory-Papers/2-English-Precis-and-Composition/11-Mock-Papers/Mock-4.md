## PART II

**Q.2 Write a précis of the following passage and suggest a suitable title. (20)**

When we see a hungry man and give him bread, we have done a good act, and nobody should speak slightingly of it. The giver is the better for giving, and the man has his bread. Charity of this kind is praised by every religion, and a society in which it had died out would be a hard and frightening place. Yet there is a question which the charitable seldom ask, and it is this: why is the man hungry?

Sometimes the answer is misfortune: a flood, an illness, the death of a breadwinner. For such cases charity is the proper remedy, because nothing else is quick enough. But very often the answer is that the man works a full day and is paid too little to feed his family, or that he would work if there were work to be had, or that the land he farmed was taken from him by someone stronger. In these cases his hunger is not an accident. It is the result of arrangements that human beings have made and could alter. To give him bread and leave the arrangements untouched is to treat the symptom and bless the disease.

Charity can even stand in the way of the cure. It allows the comfortable to feel that they have discharged their duty, and it teaches the poor to be grateful for a part of what they ought to have received as a right. A factory owner who underpays a thousand workers and then builds them a clinic will be honoured as a benefactor, although he has returned to them only a fraction of what he withheld.

None of this is an argument for closing one's hand. While wages are unjust and work is scarce, people will go hungry, and they cannot eat a promise of reform. The argument is that charity, though necessary, is not enough. A decent society will be judged less by the generosity of its alms than by how few of its members have need of them.

**Q.3 Read the following passage carefully and answer the questions given at the end. (20)**

When penicillin came into general use in the 1940s, it seemed that the long war between mankind and bacteria had been won. Diseases that had killed millions, such as pneumonia, tuberculosis and infected wounds, could suddenly be cured in a few days with a course of tablets. Surgery that would once have been too dangerous to attempt became routine, because the infections that followed it could be controlled. A generation of doctors grew up believing that infectious disease belonged to the past.

They had reckoned without the bacteria. These organisms multiply so quickly that a single one can produce millions of descendants in a day, and among those millions there will always be a few that happen, by chance, to be less easily killed by a particular drug. When the drug is used, the ordinary bacteria die and the tougher ones survive and multiply in their place. Every dose, in other words, is a small experiment in breeding resistance. Used carefully, the drug still does far more good than harm. Used carelessly, it trains the enemy.

And it has been used with astonishing carelessness. Antibiotics are swallowed for colds and influenza, which are caused by viruses and on which they have no effect whatever. Patients stop taking them as soon as they feel better, which kills the weak bacteria and spares the strong. In many countries they are sold over the counter without a prescription. Vast quantities are fed to healthy cattle and poultry merely to make them grow faster. The result is that strains of bacteria now exist that no available drug can touch, and hospitals are again seeing patients die of infections that were curable thirty years ago.

New antibiotics could be developed, but few are. A drug that is taken for a week and then kept in reserve earns little compared with one that is taken daily for life, and companies have turned to more profitable work. Unless governments pay for the research themselves, and unless doctors, farmers and patients learn restraint, medicine may find itself back where it stood before 1940, with one difference: we shall know exactly what we have thrown away.

1. Why did doctors in the middle of the twentieth century believe that infectious disease belonged to the past? (4)
2. Explain in your own words how the use of an antibiotic produces resistant bacteria. (4)
3. What careless practices in the use of antibiotics does the author describe? (4)
4. Why are few new antibiotics being developed? (4)
5. What does the author mean by the closing words, "with one difference: we shall know exactly what we have thrown away"? (4)

## Q.2

Passage: 336 words. Target: about one-third.

**Points:** (1) charity is good and necessary; (2) but the charitable seldom ask the cause of need; (3) for misfortune, charity is the right remedy; often the cause is low wages, lack of work or dispossession, which are man-made and changeable; relief alone treats the symptom; (4) charity can hinder reform by easing the conscience of the comfortable and making the poor grateful for part of their due; (5) charity must continue but is not enough; a society is judged by how few need alms.

**Title:** Charity and Justice

Giving food to the hungry is a good act, rightly praised, and a society without charity would be harsh. The charitable, however, seldom ask why people are in need. Where the cause is misfortune, charity is the right and quickest remedy. Often, though, the cause is low wages, unemployment or dispossession, which are human arrangements capable of change; relief that leaves them untouched treats only the symptom. Charity may even hinder reform by satisfying the consciences of the comfortable and making the poor grateful for part of what is theirs by right. Charity must nevertheless continue while injustice lasts, but it is insufficient: a decent society is measured by how few of its members need alms.

## Q.3

1. They believed so because penicillin and similar drugs quickly cured diseases that had once killed millions. Serious infections could be ended in days, and operations became safe because infection after surgery could be controlled. It appeared that bacteria had been finally defeated.
2. Bacteria reproduce extremely fast, and among the huge numbers produced a few are by chance harder for a given drug to kill. When the drug is taken, it destroys the ordinary bacteria, but these stronger ones survive and multiply. Repeated use therefore gradually replaces weak bacteria with resistant ones.
3. The author describes four practices: taking antibiotics for viral illnesses such as colds, on which they do not work; stopping the course early, which spares the strongest bacteria; selling the drugs freely without a doctor's prescription; and feeding them in great quantities to healthy farm animals to speed their growth.
4. Few are developed because they are not profitable. An antibiotic is taken only for a short time and is then held back for emergencies, so it earns far less than a medicine taken every day for life. Drug companies have therefore moved to more rewarding products.
5. He means that if resistance continues, medicine will again be as helpless against infection as it was before antibiotics, but this time people will be aware of the cure they once possessed. The loss will be more bitter because it was caused by their own carelessness and could have been prevented.
