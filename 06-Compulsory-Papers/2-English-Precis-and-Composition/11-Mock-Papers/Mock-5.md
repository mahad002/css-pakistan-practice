## PART II

**Q.2 Write a précis of the following passage in about 140 words and suggest a suitable title. (20)**

A society that had no traditions would have to invent everything afresh in each generation, and it would not survive the attempt. Most of what we know, we did not find out for ourselves. We did not discover which plants are poisonous, how to build an arch, or why it is unwise to trust a man who flatters us; these things were learned slowly, at great cost, by people long dead, and handed down in the form of customs, proverbs, crafts and laws. Tradition is, in this sense, the stored experience of the race. A man who despises it on principle is like an heir who burns his inheritance in order to have the pleasure of earning his own living.

It does not follow that whatever is old is right. Traditions are made by men, and men make mistakes. Some customs were sensible when they began and have outlived the conditions that justified them, as a rule about the storing of water may survive in a town that has long had pipes. Others were never sensible, but served the interest of those who had the power to impose them, and have been defended ever since with arguments invented afterwards. Slavery was a tradition. So was the burning of widows. A reverence for the past that cannot tell the difference between its wisdom and its crimes is not respect; it is laziness.

The difficulty is that from the inside the two kinds of tradition look much alike. Both feel natural to those who have grown up with them, and both are defended with equal warmth. The test must therefore be applied from outside the feeling. We may ask of any custom what purpose it serves, whether that purpose is still worth serving, and who bears the cost. A custom that protects the weak, restrains the strong, or carries knowledge that could not easily be recovered deserves to be kept, even when we cannot fully explain it. A custom whose chief effect is to keep some people beneath others deserves to be questioned, however ancient it may be.

Reform carried out in this spirit is slow, and the impatient find it tiresome. They would prefer to sweep everything away and build on a clear site. History does not encourage them. The revolutions that destroyed every inherited institution in the name of reason have generally ended by discovering, too late, how much those institutions had been quietly doing. The wiser course is that of the gardener, who neither worships the old tree nor fells it, but cuts out the dead wood so that the living may grow.

**Q.3 Read the following passage carefully and answer the questions given at the end. (20)**

We treat boredom as an enemy and have built an entire industry to defeat it. No queue is so short, no journey so brief, that it cannot be filled with a screen. Yet it is worth asking what boredom is before we congratulate ourselves on its abolition.

Boredom is not the absence of things to do. A prisoner in a cell and a child at the end of a long summer afternoon are both bored, but so, often, is a man with a hundred channels to choose from. It is better described as the discomfort of a mind that finds nothing in its surroundings worth its attention and has not yet turned to its own resources. That last clause is the important one. Boredom is a threshold. On the far side of it lie the activities that nobody undertakes while something easier is on offer: daydreaming, noticing, remembering, making plans, asking idle questions that turn out not to be idle. The child who complains that there is nothing to do, and who is left alone, will within the hour have invented a game, begun a drawing, or taken a clock to pieces. The child who is handed a screen at the first complaint never crosses the threshold at all.

There is a further cost. A person who has never learned to tolerate an empty hour becomes dependent on outside stimulation, and the dose must be continually increased. What amused him last year bores him now. He mistakes this restlessness for a lively mind, but it is the opposite: it is a mind that cannot generate its own interest and must be fed. Such a person is also, it may be noticed, an ideal customer and an ideal subject. He will buy whatever promises distraction, and he will not sit still long enough to ask whether he is being well governed.

None of this is a recommendation of dullness for its own sake. There is a sterile boredom, that of the assembly line or the pointless meeting, which leads nowhere and merely deadens. The distinction is between emptiness imposed on us by others and emptiness that we are free to fill. The first should be reduced wherever possible. The second should be protected, especially in childhood, as one protects a field left fallow: nothing appears to be happening in it, and next year's harvest depends on it.

4. Why does the author describe the person who cannot bear an empty hour as "an ideal customer and an ideal subject"? (4)
5. Distinguish between the two kinds of emptiness described in the last paragraph, and explain the comparison with a fallow field. (4)

## Q.2

Passage: 434 words. Stated limit: about 140 words (close to one-third).

**Points:** (1) tradition is stored experience; a society cannot rediscover everything; rejecting it on principle is foolish; (2) but age does not make a custom right: some are outdated, some always served the powerful (slavery); blind reverence is laziness; (3) good and bad traditions feel alike from within, so test each by purpose, present value and who bears the cost; keep those that protect the weak or preserve knowledge, question those that maintain inequality; (4) such reform is slow; sweeping destruction has proved disastrous; careful pruning is the wise course.

**Title:** The Critical Use of Tradition

Tradition is the accumulated experience of past generations, transmitted through customs, crafts and laws, and no society could survive if it had to rediscover everything anew; to reject it on principle is folly. Age alone, however, does not make a custom right. Some traditions have outlived the conditions that justified them, and others merely served the powerful, as slavery did; uncritical reverence for the past is therefore mere laziness. Since good and bad traditions feel equally natural to those raised in them, each must be judged objectively by its purpose, its present usefulness and the people who bear its cost. Customs that protect the weak or preserve valuable knowledge should be retained, while those that chiefly maintain inequality should be questioned. Such reform is slow, but wholesale destruction of inherited institutions has usually proved disastrous. Careful pruning is wiser than either worship or demolition.

(Words: 144)

Note the last sentence: the gardener image is a metaphor, so the précis states its literal sense (selective removal) in a word, "pruning", which is itself a common figurative use. "Selective reform" would be the fully literal alternative.

## Q.3

1. The author defines boredom as the unease of a mind that finds nothing around it worth attention and has not yet begun to use its own inner resources. The common view is that boredom means having nothing to do; he rejects this, pointing out that people with endless entertainment are often bored too.
2. He means that boredom is a stage one passes through, not a final state. Beyond it lie valuable activities of the mind, such as imagining, observing, remembering and planning, which people begin only when no easier amusement is available. Removing boredom at once prevents a person from ever reaching them.
3. A lively mind produces its own interests. The restless person cannot do this: he depends on outside stimulation, needs more and more of it, and quickly tires of each amusement. His constant movement therefore shows a mind that must be supplied from outside, which is the reverse of liveliness.
4. He is an ideal customer because he will pay for anything that offers distraction. He is an ideal subject because he never stays quiet long enough to think critically about how he is ruled. His dependence thus benefits those who sell to him and those who govern him.
5. One kind is emptiness forced on people by others, as in monotonous work or useless meetings; it only dulls the mind and should be reduced. The other is free time that a person may fill for himself, which should be protected. Like a fallow field, it looks unproductive but is preparing future growth.

## Q.6

(i) **Gibe** (a taunt): The speaker ignored the gibes of the crowd and continued. **Jibe** (to agree, to be consistent): His evidence does not jibe with the statement he gave to the police.
(ii) **Epigram** (a short witty saying): "I can resist everything except temptation" is a famous epigram. **Epigraph** (a quotation at the head of a book or chapter): The author chose a line from Rumi as the epigraph of his novel.
(iii) **Funeral** (a burial ceremony): The whole village attended the old teacher's funeral. **Funereal** (gloomy, as at a funeral): The losing team walked back to the pavilion at a funereal pace.
(iv) **Fain** (gladly; old use): I would fain stay longer, but my train leaves at six. **Feign** (to pretend): The prisoner feigned madness to escape punishment.
(v) **Gamble** (to risk money on chance): He gambled away his father's land at cards. **Gambol** (to skip about playfully): The lambs gambolled on the grass beside their mothers.
(vi) **Venal** (open to bribery): A venal official sold the examination paper. **Venial** (minor, pardonable): Forgetting a name is a venial fault.
(vii) **Palate** (the roof of the mouth; sense of taste): The curry was too hot for the foreign guest's palate. **Palette** (an artist's board for mixing colours): The painter mixed blue and white on his palette.
(viii) **Sere** (dry, withered): The sere grass of June turned green with the first rains. **Seer** (a prophet): The old seer warned the king that the river would change its course.
