## PART II

**Q.2 Write a précis of the following passage and suggest a suitable title. (20)**

A public library is one of the few places left in a modern city where a person may sit for a whole day without being expected to buy anything. That alone would make it valuable. But the library does a great deal more than offer a chair and a roof.

It is, first, the poor student's university. The boy whose family cannot afford books, a quiet room or a connection to the internet finds all three in the reading hall, and many who have risen from poverty to distinction have said that they owe more to a library than to any school. It is, secondly, a storehouse of the common memory. Old newspapers, local histories, records of births and land and law are kept there when every private owner has thrown them away, and a town that loses its library loses, in a real sense, its past. Thirdly, it is a meeting place of a peculiar kind. The retired clerk, the unemployed graduate, the child and the scholar sit at the same tables under the same rules, and nobody asks who they are. There are not many institutions of which that can be said.

It is often argued that the library has been made unnecessary by the telephone in everyone's pocket, since any fact can now be found in a moment. The argument confuses information with knowledge. The telephone supplies answers to questions one has already thought of asking. A library, with its open shelves, puts in front of the reader books that he did not know existed, and a trained librarian can tell him which of a hundred sources deserves his trust. Besides, the telephone is not free, and its signal does not reach the homes of those who need it most.

Governments in search of economies find the library an easy victim, because its benefits are slow and cannot be counted. That is a false economy. What a town saves by closing its reading room it will spend many times over on the consequences of ignorance.

**Q.3 Read the following passage carefully and answer the questions given at the end. (20)**

Outside the post office of our town, under a faded umbrella, sat a man whom everyone called Munshi-ji. He had a low table, a bottle of ink and a stack of forms, and for a few rupees he would write whatever a person who could not write needed written: a money order to a son in Karachi, a petition to the canal officer, a letter to a husband working in the Gulf. I was twelve, and I thought him the most unimportant man I knew.

One summer my father, who was a schoolmaster and believed that idleness was the root of every evil, sent me to sit beside the Munshi for a month and "learn to be useful". I went sulkily. For the first few days I merely filled in addresses. Then I began to listen. A woman came whose husband had not written for a year. She did not want to reproach him, she said; she only wanted him to know that the roof had been mended and that his mother asked after him daily. The Munshi wrote for a long time. When he read the letter back to her, it contained no reproach at all, and yet nobody could have heard it without understanding that she had been waiting. She wept, paid him, and went away comforted.

I saw then what his work really was. He did not sell handwriting. People came to him with a tangle of pride, fear and affection that they could not put into words, and he found the words. He knew which official liked to be addressed humbly and which one responded only to a mention of the law. He knew how to ask for money without begging and how to refuse a request without making an enemy. Half the town's quarrels, I learned afterwards, had been prevented by letters he had quietly softened.

When the month ended I did not want to leave. My father asked what I had learned. I said that I had learned to fill in a money order. It was years before I understood that I had also learned what it means to write for someone else.

## Q.2

Passage: 338 words. Target: about one-third.

**Points:** (1) a library is more than a free place to sit; (2) three services: resources for poor students; preservation of records and local memory; a meeting place of all classes on equal terms; (3) the argument that telephones replace it confuses information with knowledge: libraries reveal unexpected books and give expert guidance; telephones are not free or universal; (4) governments close libraries because benefits are slow and uncounted, a false economy.

**Title:** The Lasting Value of Public Libraries

A public library is valuable not merely as a free and quiet place but for three larger services. It gives poor students the books, space and access that their homes lack; it preserves records and local history that would otherwise vanish; and it brings people of every class and age together on equal terms. The claim that mobile telephones have made it unnecessary confuses information with knowledge, for a library introduces readers to unexpected books and offers expert guidance on reliable sources, while telephones are neither free nor universally available. Governments close libraries to save money because their benefits are gradual and unmeasurable, but the resulting ignorance costs far more.

## Q.3

1. At twelve, the narrator thought the Munshi the least important person he knew, a mere writer of forms for the illiterate. His father, a schoolmaster who disliked idleness, sent him to sit with the Munshi for a month in the summer so that he might learn to be useful.
2. The woman wished to tell her husband the family news without blaming him for his silence. The Munshi's letter contained no word of complaint, yet it conveyed clearly that she had been waiting for him. It expressed her feelings exactly while respecting her wish, and it comforted her.
3. He understood that the Munshi did not merely sell neat handwriting. People brought him confused feelings that they could not express, and he found the right words for them. His skill lay in understanding people and in choosing a tone suited to each reader and purpose.
4. It can be inferred that the Munshi was far more important than he appeared. He knew how each official should be approached, helped people obtain what they needed without loss of dignity, and prevented many quarrels by softening angry letters. The town's peace depended partly on him.
5. The narrator means that, beyond the mechanical task of filling in forms, he had learned that writing on behalf of another person requires entering into that person's feelings and expressing them faithfully and tactfully. He grasped the value of this lesson only when he was older.

## Q.6

(i) **To break the ice** (to end the awkwardness at a first meeting): The host broke the ice by asking each guest to describe his home town.
(ii) **A wild goose chase** (a hopeless search): The wrong address sent the delivery man on a wild goose chase round the whole colony.
(iii) **To sit on the fence** (to avoid taking sides): When the union and the management quarrelled, the supervisor sat on the fence and supported neither.
(iv) **To nip in the bud** (to stop at an early stage): The headmaster nipped the bullying in the bud by punishing the first offender.
(v) **Over the moon** (extremely pleased): Her parents were over the moon when she was selected for the Foreign Service.
(vi) **To ring a bell** (to sound familiar): The name of the village rings a bell, but I cannot remember where I heard it.
(vii) **To chicken out** (to withdraw through fear): He had agreed to make the speech but chickened out when he saw the size of the crowd.
(viii) **To keep up appearances** (to hide difficulties behind an outward show): Although the business was failing, the family kept up appearances with a large wedding.

## Q.7

No society can progress without the education of women. An educated mother improves the whole family, whereas a generation brought up in the lap of an illiterate mother falls behind in the race of life. In our villages, girls are still taken out of school on the plea that they are not going to take up any job. This way of thinking is entirely wrong. Our Prophet (peace be upon him) declared the acquisition of knowledge a duty for every Muslim man and woman. A nation that keeps half its population ignorant does grave harm to its own interests.

*Idioms:* سنوار دینا = to improve, to set right; زندگی کی دوڑ میں پیچھے رہ جانا = to fall behind in the race of life; سراسر غلط = entirely wrong; اپنے پاؤں پر کلہاڑی مارنا = to harm one's own interests (English also says "to shoot oneself in the foot", but the plain rendering is safer in formal prose).
