# Reading Comprehension (Q3, 20 marks)

One passage, five questions, 4 marks each. This is the most predictable 20 marks on the paper, and candidates still lose half of them by copying lines from the passage.

Contents:

1. What the question looks like
2. Method (30 minutes)
3. Answer-writing rules
4. Question types and how to answer each
5. Eight practice passages
6. Model answers

---

## 1. What the question looks like

| Year | Passage (described, not reproduced) | Kinds of question asked |
|---|---|---|
| 2016 | A humorous personal essay on failed New Year resolutions | Three "why" questions (5 marks each) and one item asking for words in the passage that match given meanings (5 marks) |
| 2017 | An essay on art and beauty in ordinary life | What is lamented; a contrast (ordinary man and artist); how; meaning of a quoted sentence; agree or disagree with reasons |
| 2018 | An essay on dependence on machines and the use of leisure | "In what sense..."; why; meaning of a term; personal response ("what would you make and how") |
| 2019 | A narrative extract from early science fiction (a crowd at a pit, a creature emerging) | Evidence for the genre; behaviour of a crowd; why a mood changed; narrator's reaction; why he felt disgust |
| 2020 | An expository passage on globalization | Define or explain five terms as used in the passage |
| 2021 | A dense political commentary on a superpower after 2001 | Five "why / what is meant by" questions needing inference |
| 2022 | An academic passage on civil society and social capital | Characterise a concept; why; meaning of a term; role; feared impact |
| 2023 | A passage on voluntary simplicity and consumerism | Relation between two ideas; description; feared impact; influences; challenges and rewards |
| 2024 | Not retrieved | – |
| 2025 | An abstract, philosophical passage on wandering | Five analytical questions, some asking for a judgement |
| 2026 | A narrative extract about a discontented wife of a minor official (in the manner of a classic short story) | Best word to describe conditions, and why; best line as evidence; inference about a marriage; values of two characters; main idea |

Pattern: mostly "why", "what does the author mean by", "explain the term", "what can be inferred", and the main idea. Narrative passages appear about one year in four. Vocabulary-from-passage appeared once.

---

## 2. Method (30 minutes)

| Step | Minutes | Action |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 2 | Read the five questions first. Underline the key word in each (why, how, meaning, infer, main idea) |
| 2 | 5 | Read the passage once at normal speed for the whole sense |
| 3 | 4 | Read again. Mark in the margin where each question is answered (Q1, Q2...). Some answers are spread over two places |
| 4 | 17 | Write the five answers, about 3.5 minutes each |
| 5 | 2 | Check: question numbers, full sentences, nothing copied, spelling |

---

## 3. Answer-writing rules

1. **Own words.** Do not lift sentences. You may reuse single key terms. If the question says "explain", copying earns almost nothing.
2. **Answer the question asked.** "Why" needs a reason. "How" needs a process or manner. "What is meant by" needs a meaning, then its sense in this passage.
3. **Start with a direct answer** in the first sentence. Then support it with one or two points from the passage.
4. **Length:** 3 to 5 sentences, 40–70 words, for a 4-mark question. All five answers should be about equal.
5. **Full sentences.** No bullet points, no notes.
6. **Stay inside the passage.** Do not bring in outside facts unless the question asks for your view.
7. **For "do you agree" questions:** take a side in the first sentence, give two reasons, and connect at least one to the passage.
8. **Tense and person:** keep the tense of the passage. Refer to "the author" or "the writer", not "he says in line 3".
9. **Number answers exactly as the questions are numbered.** Leave a line between answers.
10. **Term questions:** one sentence of general meaning, one sentence of what it means in this context.
11. **Inference questions:** state the inference, then the clue in the passage that supports it ("This is suggested by...").
12. **Main idea / title questions:** one or two sentences covering the whole passage, not the first paragraph only.

A model of the shape:

> **Q.** Why does the author call leisure a curse?
> **A.** The author calls leisure a curse because most people do not know how to use it well. [direct answer] Machines have freed them from labour, but they fill the free hours with empty amusement and so gain nothing from them. [support] Leisure that is wasted in this way weakens people instead of improving them. [closing point]

---

## 4. Question types

| Type | Signal words | How to answer |
|---|---|---|
| Factual | What, who, which, when | Find it, restate it in your own words |
| Reason | Why, what causes, account for | "Because..." in the first sentence; give every reason the passage gives |
| Manner | How, in what way | Describe the process in order |
| Meaning of a term or phrase | What does the author mean by, explain | General meaning, then meaning in context |
| Inference | What can be inferred, what does this suggest | Inference + supporting clue |
| Contrast | Difference between, compare | One sentence for each side, using "while" or "whereas" |
| Main idea | Central theme, main idea, title | Whole-passage statement |
| Tone or attitude | Attitude of the author | Name the attitude (critical, ironic, approving, anxious) and give evidence |
| Evaluation | Do you agree, justify | Position + reasons + link to passage |
| Vocabulary | Find a word that means, give the meaning of | Give the word, or a short meaning that fits the sentence |

## 5. Practice passages

Do each in 30 minutes, by hand. Then compare with the model answers in section 6. Give yourself 4 marks for an answer that is correct, complete and in your own words; 2 for a correct but partly copied or incomplete answer; 0 for a wrong one.

### Passage 1

When a new road is opened, the engineers who built it expect the traffic on the old road to be divided between the two. For a few months this is what happens. Journeys become quicker, and the newspapers praise the minister. Then, slowly, the new road fills. Within three or four years it is as crowded as the old one was, the old one is no emptier than before, and the engineers are asked to design a third.

The explanation of this puzzle has been known for half a century, though it is rarely acted upon. The number of journeys people make is not fixed. It depends on how easy the journey is. When a road is fast and empty, people who used to travel by bus buy cars, people who used to travel at dawn to avoid the crowd travel at eight, and families move to cheaper houses further out because the drive has become bearable. Shops and offices follow them. Each of these decisions is reasonable in itself, and together they generate traffic that did not exist before the road was built. The road has not relieved the demand. It has created it.

It follows that a city cannot build its way out of congestion. Every addition to road space is, in time, an invitation to drive more. The cities that have kept their streets moving have done so by the opposite method: they have made the alternatives to the car quick, cheap and pleasant, and they have charged the motorist something nearer the true cost of the space he occupies. A bus that carries sixty people takes up the room of three cars that carry four. If the bus is given a lane of its own, so that it passes the queue instead of sitting in it, people will leave their cars at home without being told to.

None of this is popular. The motorist sees the empty bus lane beside him and feels robbed. The minister prefers the opening of a flyover, which can be photographed, to the reform of a bus timetable, which cannot. And so the cycle of building and filling continues, at great expense, in the name of a solution that has failed wherever it has been tried.

**Questions**

1. What do engineers expect when a new road opens, and what actually happens over time?
2. Explain, in your own words, how a new road "creates" traffic.
3. What does the author mean by saying that a city "cannot build its way out of congestion"?
4. What methods have successful cities used to keep traffic moving?
5. Why, according to the author, do governments continue to build roads in spite of the evidence?

### Passage 2

The schoolmaster who taught me arithmetic had a single method. He wrote a rule on the board, worked one example, and set us forty sums of exactly the same kind. Whoever finished first with all forty correct was allowed to go out and play. I was quick at this and often went out first. I believed, and he believed, that I was good at mathematics.

I discovered my mistake at sixteen, when a different teacher gave the class a problem and no rule. A farmer, he said, wishes to fence a rectangular field along a river, using the river as one side, and has a fixed length of fence. What shape should the field be to enclose the most land? We waited for him to tell us which rule to apply. He sat down and opened a newspaper. After ten minutes of silence a boy at the back, who had never been first at anything, began to draw fields of different shapes and to work out their areas. The rest of us watched him as one watches a man doing something slightly improper. By the end of the hour he had found the answer, and, what was more, he could explain why it was the answer. I could do neither.

What I had learned in eight years was not mathematics but obedience. I could carry out an instruction faster than most. I had no idea how to begin when there was no instruction, and I had come to regard the feeling of not knowing what to do as a sign that the question was unfair. The boy at the back had somehow escaped this training. Perhaps he had been too slow to profit by it.

I have since met my old difficulty in many grown men. They are excellent at tasks that have been defined for them, and helpless before a situation that has not been met before. They ask for the procedure. When there is none, they conclude that nothing can be done. I do not blame them. They were taught, as I was, that the purpose of thinking is to arrive quickly at an answer that somebody else already knows.

**Questions**

1. How did the first schoolmaster teach arithmetic, and how did he judge ability?
2. What was different about the problem set by the second teacher, and why did the class wait?
3. Why does the writer say that what he had learned was "not mathematics but obedience"?
4. What can be inferred about the boy at the back of the class and the reason for his success?
5. What general criticism of education does the passage make? State it as the main idea.

### Passage 3

It is one of the oddities of public opinion that we fear most the dangers that are least likely to harm us. A man who drives every day without a thought will lie awake before a flight, although the road is many times more dangerous than the air. Parents who would never allow a child to walk to school alone, for fear of a stranger, give the same child a diet that will shorten his life by years. Whole nations spend heavily against threats that kill a few dozen people a year and grudge the cost of clean water, the lack of which kills thousands.

Psychologists explain the pattern by pointing to the shortcuts our minds take in judging risk. We judge a danger to be common if we can easily call an example to mind, and examples come easily when they are dramatic, recent or frequently shown to us. An air crash is reported for a week with photographs; the daily deaths on the road are not reported at all. We also fear what we cannot control more than what we can. The driver holds the wheel and feels safe; the passenger in an aircraft can do nothing and feels helpless. And we fear what is new and strange more than what is old and familiar, however deadly the familiar may be.

These habits of mind were probably useful to our ancestors, for whom a rustle in the grass deserved an instant reaction. They serve us badly in a world where the chief dangers are slow, invisible and statistical. They also make us easy to manipulate. Anyone who wishes to sell a product, win an election or enlarge a budget has only to attach it to a vivid fear.

The remedy is not to feel nothing. Fear is a useful servant. It is to form the habit of asking two plain questions before we act upon it: how likely is this, compared with other things that might harm me, and how much would it cost to reduce it? A public that had learned to ask these questions would be safer, richer and a good deal harder to frighten.

**Questions**

1. What "oddity of public opinion" does the author describe? Give one example from the passage in your own words.
2. Explain two mental shortcuts that distort our judgement of risk.
3. Why were these habits of mind useful once, and why are they harmful now?
4. How does misjudgement of risk make people "easy to manipulate"?
5. What remedy does the author propose, and what benefits does he expect from it?

### Passage 4

The bazaar of an old city looks to the stranger like confusion itself. The lanes are too narrow for a cart. Goods spill from the shops onto the ground. A hundred voices call out at once, and no price is written anywhere. The visitor from a country of supermarkets concludes that he is looking at a primitive form of trade which efficiency will sooner or later sweep away.

He is mistaken, and his mistake is instructive. The bazaar is not disorder. It is an order of a different kind, built to solve a problem that the supermarket never faces: how to trade safely where the law is slow and the quality of goods is uncertain. Notice first that the sellers of each thing sit together, the coppersmiths in one lane, the cloth merchants in another. This seems foolish, for each is placing himself beside his rivals. But it means that a buyer can compare a dozen offers in ten minutes, and that a seller who cheats will be known to the whole lane by evening. Notice next that buyers return, year after year, to the same shop. The long bargaining over tea, which the stranger thinks a waste of time, is how each side learns whether the other can be trusted. Once trust exists, credit is given without a signature, on the strength of a name.

In this world a merchant's most valuable possession is not his stock but his reputation. A contract is enforced not by a court, which might take ten years, but by the knowledge that a man who breaks his word will find that no one in the lane will deal with him or with his sons. The system is slow to admit outsiders, and it has little room for those without family or connections. That is its real weakness. But within its limits it works, and it has worked for a thousand years.

Those who wish to modernise such markets by pulling them down and building halls of glass should first understand what they are replacing. They may find that they have removed the trust and kept only the goods.

**Questions**

1. Why does a stranger think the bazaar primitive?
2. What problem, according to the author, is the bazaar designed to solve?
3. Explain why sellers of the same goods sit together and why long bargaining is not a waste of time.
4. How are agreements enforced in the bazaar, and what is the system's weakness?
5. What warning does the author give to those who want to modernise such markets?

### Passage 5

Nobody who has watched a child of two can doubt that human beings are born wanting to know. The child pulls things apart to see what is inside, drops them to see what will happen, and asks "why" until the adults are exhausted. He does all this without reward and in the face of a good deal of discouragement. Fifteen years later the same child sits at the back of a classroom, looks at the clock, and asks only one question: will this be in the examination?

Something has happened to his curiosity in the interval, and it is worth asking what. One answer is that curiosity has been replaced by a system of rewards. The young child inquires because he wants to know. The schoolchild is taught to inquire because he wants a mark, a prize or a word of praise, and these are given for correct answers, not for good questions. Experiments have shown repeatedly that when people are paid to do something they previously did for pleasure, they do it less once the payment stops. The reward does not strengthen the interest. It takes its place.

A second answer is that curiosity needs gaps, and the school day has none. To wonder about something one must first notice that one does not understand it, and then be allowed to remain in that state for a while. The timetable does not permit this. The bell rings, the subject changes, and the half-formed question is lost. A third answer is that the child soon learns which questions are welcome. The question that the teacher can answer is welcome. The question that leads away from the lesson, or that the teacher cannot answer, is a nuisance, and its author is made to feel it.

It would be unjust to blame teachers, who work within a system they did not design and are themselves judged by results. But the cost should be recognised. A country that turns curious children into incurious adults has thrown away the one resource on which all its other progress depends, and it has done so at great public expense.

**Questions**

1. What contrast does the author draw between the child of two and the same child fifteen years later?
2. How do rewards damage curiosity? Refer to the evidence mentioned.
3. What does the author mean by saying that "curiosity needs gaps"?
4. Which kinds of question are unwelcome in the classroom, and why?
5. What is the author's attitude towards teachers, and what is his final warning?

### Passage 6

I went back last winter to the valley where I was born, after an absence of thirty years. I had been warned that I should find it changed, and I believed that I was prepared. I was not.

The river was the first shock. I remembered a wide green water in which we swam every summer and from which my uncle took fish as long as my arm. I found a grey stream, a few paces across, running between banks of plastic bags. The glacier that fed it has retreated far up the mountain, an old shepherd told me, and each year the snows come later and go sooner. The orchards had gone too. Apricot trees that my grandfather planted had been cut down to make room for rows of concrete guest houses, most of them empty in that season, with names in English painted on their walls.

Yet it would be dishonest to pretend that everything was worse. The road, which used to be closed by snow for four months of the year, is now open in all but the worst weeks. There is a clinic with a doctor. In my childhood a woman in a difficult labour was carried on a bed for two days to the nearest town, and often died on the way. The girls of the valley, who in my time were married at fifteen, now go to a high school, and three of them, I was told with great pride, are studying medicine in the city. My cousin, who would have spent his life behind a plough, owns two jeeps and takes tourists to the lake.

I asked him whether he was sorry about the orchards. He looked at me as one looks at a foolish child. "You left," he said. "You remember the apricots. I remember being hungry in March." I had no answer. I had come as a mourner, expecting the people of the valley to mourn with me, and I found that what I regretted was a beauty that I had enjoyed from a distance and that they had paid for. I still grieve for the river. But I no longer imagine that my grief gives me the right to tell them how to live.

**Questions**

1. What changes for the worse did the writer find in the valley?
2. What improvements does he honestly admit?
3. Explain the meaning of the cousin's reply: "You remember the apricots. I remember being hungry in March."
4. What does the writer realise about his own regret?
5. Describe the tone of the passage and state its main idea.

### Passage 7

Money is so familiar that we seldom ask what it is. We speak of it as a thing, like gold or grain. It is better understood as an agreement. A banknote is a piece of paper that costs almost nothing to print. It buys a week's food only because the shopkeeper believes that his supplier will accept it, the supplier believes that his workmen will accept it, and so on round a circle that includes everyone in the country. Remove that belief and the note is paper again.

History offers many examples of the belief being removed. When a government spends far more than it collects, and covers the difference by printing notes, there is soon more money chasing the same quantity of goods. Prices rise. For a time people respond in the ordinary way, by spending a little more carefully. But there comes a point at which they realise that money is losing value every week, and then their behaviour changes. They hurry to turn their wages into anything solid, flour, cloth, bricks, on the day they are paid. This rush to spend drives prices up faster still, the government prints more to keep pace, and the currency collapses. In the worst cases, workers have been paid twice a day and prices have doubled in a few days.

The damage of such an episode is not only economic. The people who suffer most are those who trusted the system: the clerk who saved for his old age, the widow living on a pension, the lender who was repaid in worthless paper. Those who gain are debtors and speculators. A whole class learns that prudence is punished and gambling rewarded, and that lesson is not forgotten for generations. Several of the ugliest political movements of the last century grew in soil prepared by ruined currencies.

This is why sound money is not a technical matter to be left to bankers. It is a public trust. A government that protects the value of the currency protects the weak, who have no other store of wealth, and keeps a promise on which every other promise in economic life depends.

**Questions**

1. Why does the author describe money as "an agreement" and not a thing?
2. Trace the steps by which excessive printing of money leads to the collapse of a currency.
3. Who loses and who gains during such a collapse?
4. What social and political consequences does the author attribute to ruined currencies?
5. Why does the author call sound money "a public trust"?

### Passage 8

The traveller of former times set out to see what he had not seen. The tourist of today sets out to see what he has already seen, a thousand times, in photographs. He arrives at the famous tower, stands where everyone stands, and takes the picture that everyone takes. If the tower should happen to look different from its photograph, because it is raining or covered in scaffolding, he feels cheated. He has come not to discover the place but to confirm it.

The change is more than a matter of cameras. Travel was once difficult, and its difficulty was a large part of its value. A man who had crossed a desert on foot to reach a city looked at that city with eyes that the journey had prepared. He had learned some of the language from necessity. He had eaten what there was to eat and slept where there was room, and by the time he arrived he had already been altered. The modern tourist is carried from his own door to a hotel that has been designed to resemble, as closely as possible, the country he has left. He eats familiar food, hears his own language, and views the foreign city through the window of a bus. He has moved his body several thousand miles and his mind not at all.

It would be foolish to regret cheap travel. It has given to millions a pleasure once reserved for the rich, and it supports whole economies. Nor is the old-style traveller extinct; one meets him still, usually alone, usually on a slow train. But it is fair to ask what the hurried visitor brings home besides his photographs. To be changed by a place one must give it time and allow it to be inconvenient. One must be lost in it at least once, be dependent at least once on the kindness of someone who shares no word of one's speech, and stay long enough for the first excitement to be replaced by a more ordinary and more truthful acquaintance. The tourist buys an experience. The traveller submits to one. The difference lies not in the distance covered but in the willingness to be surprised.

**Questions**

1. What difference does the author see between the purpose of the old traveller and that of the modern tourist?
2. Why was the difficulty of travel "a large part of its value"?
3. What does the author mean by "He has moved his body several thousand miles and his mind not at all"?
4. Does the author condemn modern tourism entirely? Support your answer from the passage.
5. According to the author, what must a person do in order to be changed by a place?

## 6. Model answers

### Passage 1

1. Engineers expect the existing traffic to be shared between the old road and the new one, so that both become less crowded. This happens only for a short period. Within a few years the new road becomes as congested as the old one had been, while the old road is no less busy, and a further road is demanded.
2. A new road creates traffic because the number of journeys depends on how convenient travel is. When driving becomes quick, people switch from buses to cars, travel at peak hours, and move to distant suburbs, and businesses follow them. These choices add journeys that would never have been made without the road.
3. The author means that congestion cannot be cured by adding road space. Since every new road encourages more driving, the extra capacity is soon used up, and the city returns to the same crowded condition at greater cost.
4. Successful cities have reduced the need to drive instead of adding roads. They have made public transport fast, cheap and comfortable, for example by reserving lanes for buses, and they have made motorists pay a charge closer to the real cost of the road space they use.
5. Governments keep building roads because the alternatives are unpopular and unimpressive. Drivers resent losing space to buses, and politicians prefer a visible project such as a flyover, which earns publicity, to quiet improvements in public transport, which do not.

### Passage 2

1. The first schoolmaster taught by stating a rule, showing one example and setting many identical sums. He judged ability by speed and accuracy, rewarding the pupil who finished first with every answer correct.
2. The second teacher set a practical problem without telling the class which rule to use. The pupils waited because they had always been given a method to follow and did not know how to start thinking for themselves.
3. He says so because his training had only taught him to carry out instructions quickly. He had never learned to approach an unfamiliar problem, and he even felt that a question without a given method was unfair. His skill was in following, not in reasoning.
4. It can be inferred that the boy succeeded because he was willing to experiment and reason independently. As he had never excelled at the mechanical exercises, he had not been shaped by them, and so he was free to try different shapes and discover the answer and its explanation.
5. The passage criticises an education that trains pupils to apply given procedures instead of thinking. Its main idea is that such schooling produces adults who perform defined tasks well but are helpless in new situations, because they have learned that thinking means reaching an answer already known to someone else.

### Passage 3

1. The oddity is that people are most afraid of dangers that are unlikely to hurt them and careless of those that are common. For instance, a person may be anxious about flying yet drive daily without concern, although road travel is far more dangerous.
2. First, people judge a danger to be frequent when examples come readily to mind, which happens with dramatic and widely reported events. Secondly, they fear risks they cannot control more than those they feel they manage themselves. (A third shortcut is that unfamiliar dangers frighten more than familiar ones.)
3. These habits once helped early humans, who faced sudden physical threats and needed to react at once. They are harmful now because modern dangers are gradual, unseen and known only through statistics, so quick emotional reactions lead to wrong priorities.
4. Because people respond to vivid fears and not to real probabilities, anyone can influence them by linking a product, a political cause or a demand for funds to a frightening image. Their fear is thus used to serve another person's purpose.
5. The author proposes that people should keep their fear but test it with two questions: how probable the danger is compared with others, and what it would cost to reduce it. He expects that a public with this habit would be safer, wealthier and much less easily frightened.

### Passage 4

1. A stranger thinks the bazaar primitive because it appears chaotic. Its lanes are narrow, goods lie everywhere, sellers shout, and no prices are displayed. Being used to supermarkets, he assumes that such a market is backward and will be replaced by more efficient trade.
2. The bazaar is designed to make trade safe in conditions where the courts act slowly and the quality of goods cannot be guaranteed. It provides its own means of checking honesty and enforcing promises.
3. Sellers of the same goods sit together so that buyers can compare offers quickly and so that a dishonest trader is soon exposed to all his neighbours. Long bargaining allows buyer and seller to judge each other's reliability, and the trust it builds later makes credit possible without written agreements.
4. Agreements are enforced through reputation, not through courts. A merchant who breaks his word is shunned by the whole market, along with his family. The weakness of the system is that it is closed: newcomers and people without family ties or connections find it hard to enter.
5. He warns them to understand the hidden functions of the bazaar before destroying it. If they replace it with modern buildings, they may preserve the trade in goods but lose the network of trust on which that trade depends.

### Passage 5

1. The child of two is intensely curious: he explores and questions everything without any reward. Fifteen years later the same child is bored in class and cares only whether a topic will be examined. The contrast shows that natural curiosity has been lost during schooling.
2. Rewards damage curiosity by replacing the wish to know with the wish for marks or praise, which are given for right answers and not for good questions. The author cites experiments showing that people who are paid for an activity they once enjoyed lose interest in it when payment ends.
3. He means that curiosity can develop only when a person has time to notice that he does not understand something and to think about it. The crowded school timetable allows no such pauses, so questions fade before they are formed.
4. Questions that lead away from the lesson, and questions the teacher cannot answer, are unwelcome. They disturb the planned work and embarrass the teacher, so the pupil who asks them is discouraged and learns to stay silent.
5. The author is sympathetic to teachers, since they work in a system they did not create and are judged by results. His warning is that a country which destroys the curiosity of its children wastes, at great expense, the resource on which all its progress depends.

### Passage 6

1. He found that the river had shrunk to a dirty stream, littered with plastic, because the glacier had retreated and the snows had become shorter. The old apricot orchards had been felled and replaced by concrete guest houses, many standing empty.
2. He admits that the road is now open nearly all year, that the valley has a clinic and a doctor, and that girls attend high school and even study medicine. People such as his cousin now earn a good living from tourism instead of farming in poverty.
3. The cousin means that the writer, having left, remembers only the valley's beauty, while those who stayed remember its hardship. For the residents, the orchards belonged to a time of hunger at the end of winter, so they do not regret the change that brought them food and income.
4. He realises that his regret was that of an outsider. He had enjoyed the beauty of the valley from a distance, whereas the inhabitants had borne the poverty that went with it. He concludes that his sorrow gives him no right to dictate how they should live.
5. The tone is reflective and honest, moving from nostalgia and grief to humility. The main idea is that change in a traditional place brings real loss of natural beauty but also real gains for its people, and that those who have left should not judge the choices of those who remain.

### Passage 7

1. He calls money an agreement because a banknote has almost no value in itself. It can buy goods only because everyone believes that everyone else will accept it. Its value therefore rests on shared confidence, not on the material it is made of.
2. A government that spends beyond its income prints notes to cover the gap, so more money pursues the same goods and prices rise. When people see that money is steadily losing value, they rush to exchange it for goods at once. This pushes prices up faster, more notes are printed, and finally the currency becomes worthless.
3. The losers are those who relied on the currency: savers, pensioners and lenders, whose money loses its worth. The gainers are debtors, who repay in valueless notes, and speculators, who profit from rising prices.
4. He argues that a ruined currency teaches a whole class that careful saving is punished and gambling rewarded, and that this bitterness lasts for generations. He adds that some of the worst political movements of the twentieth century arose in societies whose money had collapsed.
5. He calls it a public trust because the value of money is a promise on which all other economic promises rest. By keeping the currency sound, a government protects the weakest people, who hold their small wealth in money, and so the matter concerns the whole public, not bankers alone.

### Passage 8

1. The old traveller went abroad to discover what was unknown to him. The modern tourist goes to see sights already familiar from photographs, and wants them to match those pictures. His aim is to confirm his expectations, not to find anything new.
2. The difficulty of travel was valuable because it prepared and changed the traveller. Hardship forced him to learn the language, eat local food and live as the people lived, so that he reached his destination already altered and able to see it with understanding.
3. The author means that the tourist travels physically but not mentally. Surrounded by familiar food, language and comfort, and seeing the foreign country only through a bus window, he learns nothing and returns with the same outlook as before.
