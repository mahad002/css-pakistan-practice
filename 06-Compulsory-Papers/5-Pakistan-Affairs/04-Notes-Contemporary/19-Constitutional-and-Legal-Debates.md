## 1. The judiciary in constitutional history (2017)

### Structure

- **Until 2025:** Supreme Court at the apex (original, appellate and advisory jurisdiction); five high courts; the Federal Shariat Court (1980); the Supreme Judicial Council for removal of judges (Art. 209).
- **Since the 27th Amendment (November 2025):** a **Federal Constitutional Court** decides constitutional questions and fundamental-rights matters; the Supreme Court is the final court of appeal in civil and criminal cases.

### Landmark cases

| Case | Year | Held | Significance |
|---|---|---|---|

### Assessment of the judicial role

**Negative:** validated every military takeover while it lasted; the doctrine of necessity; judges took oath under PCOs; selective accountability of politicians; **over-use of suo motu powers** (especially 2009–13 and 2018–19) intruded on executive and economic decisions (the Reko Diq and Steel Mills rulings carried heavy costs); internal divisions and "bench-fixing" allegations; huge backlog (over two million cases in all courts).

**Positive:** *Asma Jilani* and the 2009 judgment; protection of fundamental rights and public-interest litigation; restoration of an elected government (1993) and of the Assembly (2022); the Lawyers' Movement (2007–09) as a popular defence of judicial independence; rulings on missing persons, minorities (the 2014 Jillani judgment), environment and women's rights.

**Verdict:** the courts have swung between deference to power and activism against elected governments. Neither built stable constitutionalism. Paula Newberg's *Judging the State* (on the FPSC list) is the standard reference.

## 2. The reshaping of the judiciary, 2023–2026

| Date | Step |
|---|---|
| April 2023 | **Supreme Court (Practice and Procedure) Act:** bench formation and suo motu notices by a committee of three senior judges; a right of appeal in Art. 184(3) cases |
| March 2024 | Six Islamabad High Court judges wrote to the Supreme Judicial Council alleging interference by intelligence agencies |
| **October 2024** | **26th Amendment:** Parliament's committee picks the Chief Justice from the top three; three-year term; constitutional benches; a reconstituted Judicial Commission. Number of Supreme Court judges raised by law to 34 |
| 2025 | Transfers of judges to the Islamabad High Court and a seniority dispute; constitutional benches decide the major political cases |
| **November 2025** | **27th Amendment:** Federal Constitutional Court; transfer of high court judges without consent; end of the Supreme Court's constitutional and suo motu role. Justices Mansoor Ali Shah and Athar Minallah resigned |
| 2026 | The FCC begins to function; it took over the challenges to governor's rule in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (November 2026) |

### The debate (2026 question)

| Government's case | Critics' case |
|---|---|
| Parliament is supreme and may restructure courts; the Charter of Democracy (2006) proposed a constitutional court | The amendments were passed in days, without debate, by a Parliament whose mandate is disputed |
| Judicial overreach had destabilised governments and the economy | The remedy for overreach is better appointments and rules, not executive control |
| A specialist court speeds up constitutional cases and lets the Supreme Court clear appeals | The first judges of the new court were chosen by the executive; transfers can punish independent judges |
| Many democracies (Germany, South Africa, Turkey, Indonesia) have such courts | In those countries the court was created by consensus and its judges are insulated |
| Fixed terms and parliamentary input make appointments accountable | Judicial independence is a salient feature of the constitution (Objectives Resolution; Art. 175) |
| Unified military command and protected ranks suit modern warfare | Lifelong immunity offends equality before law (Art. 25) and accountability |

**A balanced conclusion for the exam:** the idea of a constitutional court is defensible; the manner, timing and accompanying provisions weakened the separation of powers. Whether constitutionalism is strengthened will depend on how the new court behaves in cases against the government.

## 4. Live constitutional debates (November 2026)

1. **Judicial independence versus parliamentary sovereignty** after the 26th and 27th Amendments.
2. **Emergency powers:** governor's rule in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa under **Arts. 232 and 234** (October 2026). Art. 234 is meant for a breakdown of constitutional machinery; it needs approval by a joint sitting, runs two months at a time, and at most six months. Critics say it was used against a province governed by the opposition; the centre cites security failure.
3. **New provinces and a 28th Amendment:** **Art. 239(4)** requires two-thirds of the affected provincial assembly to agree to any change in a province's limits. The PPP rejects any division of Sindh.
4. **The 18th Amendment and the NFC:** pressure to move subjects or money back to the centre (file 21).
5. **Military courts and civilians;** fair trial under **Art. 10A**.
6. **Elections:** the 90-day rule; the Election Commission's independence; the 2024 results; reserved seats.
7. **Speech and the digital space:** PECA; internet shutdowns; **Arts. 19 and 19A**.
8. **Civil–military balance** under the amended **Art. 243**.
9. **Missing persons** and the Commission of Inquiry on Enforced Disappearances.
10. **Riba:** the Federal Shariat Court's 2022 ruling and the constitutional deadline of 1 January 2028.
11. **Status of Gilgit-Baltistan and the merged districts.**
12. **Local government:** making Art. 140A enforceable.

## 5. Way forward

- Constitutional change by consensus and with debate: committee hearings, published drafts, time.
- Transparent, merit-based judicial appointments with a real voice for judges, the bar and Parliament, and security of tenure.
- Clear jurisdictional lines between the two top courts.
- Case-management reform to cut backlog: more judges, technology, alternative dispute resolution.
- Restraint on both sides: courts out of policy, executives out of benches.
- Use emergency powers only as a last resort and for the shortest time.
- Civilian courts for civilians; a credible appeal for those already tried.
- Strengthen Parliament so that disputes are settled there and not in court.
