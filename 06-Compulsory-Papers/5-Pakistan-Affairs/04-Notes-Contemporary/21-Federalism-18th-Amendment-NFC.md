# 21. Federalism, the 18th Amendment and the NFC

Not a separate syllabus line, but examined under items XVI and XXVII more often than almost anything else: centre–province relations (2018, 2019), the 18th Amendment (2022, 2023 special), provincial autonomy and integration (2024), health and education after devolution (2026). With a 28th Amendment on new provinces under discussion, expect it again.

## 1. Federalism in Pakistan: the long view

| Period | Centre–province balance |
|---|---|
| 1947–55 | 1935 Act: strong centre; Section 92A used to dismiss provincial governments |
| 1955–70 | One Unit and parity; two provinces; in effect unitary under Ayub |
| 1970 | Four provinces restored |
| **1973** | Federal, with a Senate, CCI and NFC; but a long Concurrent List and a dominant centre |
| 1977–88, 1999–2008 | Military rule centralised power; governors and devolution to districts bypassed provinces |
| **2009–10** | **7th NFC Award and 18th Amendment:** the largest shift to the provinces in the country's history |
| 2018 | 25th Amendment: FATA merged with Khyber Pakhtunkhwa |
| 2024–26 | Pressure to recentralise: fiscal strain, the 27th Amendment's early drafts, governor's rule in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, the new-provinces debate |

**Why federalism matters here:** a multi-ethnic country in which one unit holds over half the population. The alternative was tried and ended in 1971.

## 2. The 18th Amendment: what changed for the provinces

- **Concurrent Legislative List abolished:** 47 subjects; most went to the provinces. Seventeen federal ministries were devolved by June 2011.
- **Council of Common Interests** strengthened: chaired by the Prime Minister, quarterly meetings, a secretariat, a longer Federal List Part II (ports, electricity, regulatory authorities, census, higher-education standards, inter-provincial coordination, national planning).
- **NFC:** Art. 160(3A) — provincial share never below the previous award.
- **Natural resources:** Art. 172(3) joint and equal ownership of oil and gas; Art. 161 net hydel profits and royalties; Art. 158 priority in gas use.
- **Borrowing:** provinces may borrow within limits set by the National Economic Council (Art. 167).
- **Art. 140A:** elected local governments.
- **Emergency:** a provincial emergency needs the provincial assembly's resolution or, failing that, approval by both houses of Parliament (Art. 232).
- **Identity:** Khyber Pakhtunkhwa named.
- **Hydel power:** the federation must consult a province before building a hydro station there (Art. 157).

## 3. The 7th NFC Award (December 2009)

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Vertical share | Provinces **57.5%** of the divisible pool (56% in the first year); the centre 42.5%. Before: about 47.5% to provinces |
| Horizontal formula | **Population 82%; poverty and backwardness 10.3%; revenue collection and generation 5%; inverse population density 2.7%.** First time anything but population was used |
| Shares | Punjab 51.74%; Sindh 24.55%; Khyber Pakhtunkhwa 14.62%; Balochistan 9.09% |
| Extras | 1% of the pool to Khyber Pakhtunkhwa for the war on terror; Balochistan's share guaranteed; sales tax on services to provinces; gas development surcharge arrears settled |
| Since | No new award. The 7th has been extended each year. The 11th Commission was constituted in 2025 **[verify any progress]** |

## 4. Assessment of the 18th Amendment

### Gains

1. Restored the parliamentary and federal character agreed in 1973; removed the main tools of dictatorship.
2. Answered long-standing demands of the smaller provinces; lowered the temperature of nationalist politics in Sindh and Khyber Pakhtunkhwa.
3. More money and responsibility where services are delivered.
4. Provinces legislated and experimented: right-to-information and right-to-services laws, women's protection laws, education and health reforms, agricultural income tax, and their own revenue authorities for sales tax on services.
5. Consensus across all parties — a rare event.

### Problems (why it is criticised — 2022)

1. **Fiscal squeeze on the centre:** after transfers, the federation's net revenue barely covers interest and defence; it borrows for everything else. Yet it still funds BISP, higher education, subsidies and provincial-type projects.
2. **Weak provincial tax effort:** provinces raise about 1% of GDP themselves; agriculture and urban property are lightly taxed.
3. **Capacity gaps:** provinces inherited subjects without staff, systems or regulators.
4. **Uneven results and disparities** between and within provinces.
5. **Devolution stopped at the provincial capital:** local governments are weak, delayed or dissolved; Provincial Finance Commissions are dormant.
6. **Loss of national standards and coordination** in curriculum, drug regulation, higher education, food safety, climate and data.
7. **International commitments** (SDGs, climate, trade standards) are federal obligations for subjects the centre no longer controls.
8. **Duplication:** new federal ministries re-created in devolved areas (health regulation, education and professional training, climate change, food security).
9. **CCI under-used:** irregular meetings; decisions unimplemented.
10. **Security and disaster response** revealed coordination failures — though the COVID-19 command centre (NCOC) showed that cooperative federalism can work.

### The rollback debate (2023 special)

**Arguments for returning some subjects or money to the centre:** uniform standards in education and health; population control; fiscal sustainability and the IMF's "National Fiscal Pact"; drug and food regulation; disaster and climate management.

**Arguments against:** it would break the 1973 and 2010 consensus and revive provincial grievances; the centre ran these subjects poorly before 2010; problems are of implementation, not design; national standards can be set through the CCI and by agreement (as with DRAP, set up under Art. 144 with provincial consent); the fiscal gap is better closed by sharing expenditure and raising taxes than by cutting provincial shares.

**A reasoned position:** keep the devolution; fix its working. Use the CCI and inter-provincial ministerial bodies for standards; let the federation keep regulation of medicines, higher-education standards and data; make population planning a shared national programme; rewrite the NFC to match money with responsibilities; push power down to districts.

## 5. Health and education after devolution (2026)

### What improved

- **Laws:** every province passed a compulsory-education law under Art. 25A; health-care commissions regulate hospitals; public-health and vaccination laws.
- **Budgets:** provincial allocations for both sectors rose several-fold in rupee terms after 2010.
- **Innovations:** merit-based teacher hiring and monitoring by data (Punjab, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa); education foundations and public–private schools (Punjab, Sindh); girls' stipends; **Sehat Card** universal health insurance (Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, then Punjab); contracting-out of health units (Sindh's PPHI); medical teaching institution reforms; regularisation of Lady Health Workers.
- **Local fit:** curricula and languages closer to provincial needs.

### What did not

- **Outcomes:** about 25–26 million children out of school; literacy stuck near 60%; learning poverty about three in four; stunting about 40%; polio not eradicated; high maternal and infant mortality.
- **Spending as a share of GDP has fallen** to about 0.8% each — the opposite of what devolution promised.
- **Disparities widened:** Punjab and urban Sindh far ahead of Balochistan, rural Sindh and the merged districts.
- **Coordination failures:** vertical health programmes (immunisation, TB, malaria, family planning) suffered funding gaps in the hand-over; curriculum disputes (the Single National Curriculum, 2021); friction between the HEC and provincial higher-education commissions; chaos in medical education regulation; no common data standards.
- **Money spent on salaries and buildings,** not on quality.
- **No third tier:** schools and clinics are run from provincial secretariats.

### Why

Low priority by provincial elites; weak planning capacity; political interference in postings; population growth outrunning provision; floods and security crises; fiscal stress; the absence of local government; and the centre's withdrawal from financing without a transition plan.

### Way forward

1. Minimum national standards through the CCI and inter-provincial ministers' conferences: learning outcomes, essential health package, data.
2. **Conditional grants** in the next NFC for out-of-school children, nutrition, immunisation and family planning.
3. Raise combined spending towards 4% of GDP for education and 3% for health.
4. Devolve management to districts and school and facility councils.
5. Primary health care, nutrition and family planning first.
6. Foundational literacy and numeracy; teacher support; second-shift and non-formal schools for out-of-school children.
7. Special equalisation for Balochistan and the merged districts.
8. Federal role in research, regulation, international commitments and emergencies.

## 6. Present pressures on the federation (to 23 Nov 2026)

- **27th Amendment (November 2025):** early drafts would have touched the NFC guarantee and moved education and population planning to the centre; the PPP objected and these were dropped or deferred **[verify]**.
- **IMF's National Fiscal Pact:** provinces to run surpluses, tax agriculture, and take over devolved spending.
- **Water:** the Cholistan canals plan shelved by the CCI (April 2025) after Sindh's protests.
- **Minerals:** federal-backed mines and minerals bills drew protests in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and Balochistan (2025) over provincial ownership.
- **Khyber Pakhtunkhwa:** emergency and governor's rule since 11 October 2026 — the first suspension of an elected provincial government in many years, and a test of federal principles ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/12/what-do-emergency-and-governor-rule-in-pakistans-khyber-pakhtunkhwa-mean)).
- **New provinces / a 28th Amendment:** ministers say new provinces could come soon; no bill has been tabled; the PPP says the debate must end as far as Sindh is concerned; without the PPP the coalition lacks two-thirds ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/17/is-pakistan-about-to-carve-out-new-provinces-inside-the-controversial-plan)).

## 7. Local government: the missing tier

- Systems tried: Basic Democracies (1959), Zia's local bodies (1979), Musharraf's devolution (2001) — each under a military ruler and each rolled back by the next elected government.
- Since 2010 provinces have delayed elections, cut terms and kept powers. Art. 140A has no enforcement clause.
- **Reform:** a constitutional chapter fixing tenure, functions, direct election and a guaranteed share of provincial funds — as India did with its 73rd and 74th Amendments.
