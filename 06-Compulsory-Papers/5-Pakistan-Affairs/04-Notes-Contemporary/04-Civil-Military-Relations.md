# 4. Civil–Military Relations in Pakistan

Syllabus item VI. Asked in 2024 (pragmatic civil–military relations) and 2026 (youth, digital activism and civil society). A sensitive topic. Write analytically, cite scholars, criticise structures and policies rather than institutions or persons, and always end with a constructive way forward.

## 1. Concepts

- **Civil–military relations:** how authority is shared between elected civilians and the armed forces, especially over defence, foreign policy and internal security.
- **Civilian supremacy:** elected governments make policy; the military advises and implements. In the constitution: Art. 243 (the federal government has control and command of the armed forces), Art. 244 (oath not to engage in politics), Art. 245 (the forces act on the federal government's directions), Art. 6 (high treason).
- Useful terms from the literature:
  - Samuel Huntington's **"objective control"**: a professional army kept out of politics.
  - Morris Janowitz and S.E. Finer: why armies intervene where civilian institutions are weak.
  - Hamza Alavi's **"overdeveloped state"**: Pakistan inherited a bureaucracy and army stronger than its political classes.
  - Hasan-Askari Rizvi (*Military, State and Society in Pakistan*): the army as the most cohesive institution in a divided polity.
  - Ayesha Jalal (*The State of Martial Rule*): the early security threat and the choice of alliances tilted the balance towards the army and bureaucracy.
  - Ayesha Siddiqa (*Military Inc.*): the military's economic interests (**"Milbus"**).
  - Aqil Shah (*The Army and Democracy*): the army's institutional beliefs about its guardian role.
  - Stephen Cohen (*The Idea of Pakistan*, *The Pakistan Army*).
  - **"Hybrid regime"**: civilian governments in office with the military holding decisive influence.

## 2. Background

| Period | Pattern |
|---|---|
| 1947–58 | Weak parties; no election; Kashmir war and insecurity; the army chief joined the cabinet as defence minister (1954); US military aid |
| 1958–71 | Direct military rule (Ayub, Yahya); ended in defeat in 1971 |
| 1971–77 | Civilian supremacy asserted by Bhutto (Art. 6; changes to the command structure); but he used the army in Balochistan and against the 1977 protests |
| 1977–88 | Zia's martial law; the 8th Amendment gave the President power over governments |
| 1988–99 | The "troika" of President, Prime Minister and army chief; four governments removed |
| 1999–2008 | Musharraf; National Security Council by law |
| 2008–18 | Civilian governments completed terms; friction over the Kerry–Lugar aid conditions (2009), Memogate (2011), the trial of Musharraf, India policy, the "Dawn leaks" (2016) |
| 2018–22 | "Same page" under the PTI government, ending in a dispute over the appointment of the ISI chief (October 2021) and the vote of no confidence (April 2022) |
| 2022–present | PTI's confrontation with the military leadership; **9 May 2023**; trials of civilians in military courts; the SIFC; the 26th and 27th Amendments |

Direct military rule: 1958–71, 1977–88, 1999–2008 — about 33 years.

## 3. Why the imbalance arose

**Structural and historical**

1. An army inherited intact and a political party that had no roots in the new country.
2. The **security threat from India** from the first weeks: Kashmir, 1965, 1971, and a defence budget that took most of early revenue.
3. Cold War **alliances**: US aid went to the army and raised its capacity and standing.
4. **Afghanistan** (1979–89, 2001–21) placed the security services at the centre of foreign policy.

**Civilian weakness**

5. Delay in constitution-making; no general election for 23 years.
6. Parties built on families and personalities; little internal democracy; poor governance and corruption charges.
7. Politicians invited or welcomed intervention against rivals (1977, 1990s, later).
8. Weak local government and parliamentary committees; little civilian expertise in defence.

**Institutional**

9. **Judicial validation** of takeovers: *Dosso* (1958), *Nusrat Bhutto* (1977), *Zafar Ali Shah* (2000).
10. The military's **cohesion, discipline and public standing**, especially after disasters and wars.
11. Its **economic footprint**: Fauji Foundation, Army Welfare Trust, Shaheen and Bahria Foundations, National Logistics Cell, Frontier Works Organisation, housing schemes.
12. Its role in **internal security** (counter-terrorism operations, Rangers in Karachi, Balochistan), which grows whenever civilian policing fails.

## 4. Current position (to Nov 2026)

- **SIFC (June 2023):** the Special Investment Facilitation Council gives the army chief a formal seat in economic decision-making.
- **Military courts:** after 9 May 2023, over a hundred civilians were tried by military courts. The Supreme Court struck this down in October 2023; a constitutional bench restored the trials on 7 May 2025 by a 5–2 majority ([Dawn](https://www.dawn.com/news/1909300)).
- **Service tenures:** in November 2024 the terms of service chiefs were extended from three to five years by ordinary law.
- **May 2025:** after the conflict with India, General Asim Munir was promoted to **Field Marshal** (20 May 2025), the first since Ayub Khan. Public support for the forces rose sharply.
- **27th Amendment (13 November 2025):** created the **Chief of Defence Forces**, held by the army chief; abolished the Chairman Joint Chiefs of Staff Committee; gave five-star officers rank and immunity for life. Field Marshal Munir was notified as CDF for five years on 5 December 2025 ([Dawn](https://www.dawn.com/news/1959310)).
- **Foreign policy:** the army chief has been central to relations with Washington, Riyadh, Beijing and Tehran, including the 2026 mediation.
- **Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (October 2026):** emergency and governor's rule were imposed on the PTI-run province, with the federal government citing its failure against militancy ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/11/pakistan-imposes-emergency-rule-in-khyber-pakhtunkhwa)).
- **Assessments differ.** The government presents the arrangement as a working partnership that has delivered stability, investment and diplomatic gains. Critics (opposition parties, bar associations, Chatham House, international jurists) describe a hybrid system in which elected institutions have ceded ground.

### Youth, digital activism and civil society (the 2026 question)

- About 64% of Pakistanis are under 30. Over 110 million use the internet and most political news now reaches the young through social media.
- **What changed:** political parties (first PTI, then others) built large online followings; in 2022–24 criticism of the military's political role, once confined to small circles, became mass online speech; the February 2024 election showed that young voters could be mobilised through phones despite restrictions on campaigning.
- **Earlier civil society waves:** the Lawyers' Movement (2007–09) restored a chief justice and helped end military rule; the Pashtun Tahaffuz Movement (from 2018) and the Baloch Yakjehti Committee raised rights issues from the periphery; women's marches; journalists' unions.
- **The state's response:** amendments to the Prevention of Electronic Crimes Act (January 2025) criminalising "false information"; a national firewall; the long ban on X (February 2024 – May 2025); internet shutdowns during protests; cases against activists; the banning of the PTM (October 2024).
- **Effects on civil–military relations:** (a) the military's role is now publicly debated, which earlier generations avoided; (b) institutions have invested heavily in their own digital communication and speak of "digital terrorism" and fifth-generation warfare; (c) polarisation has grown, with disinformation on all sides; (d) after May 2025 the same digital space produced a surge of support for the forces.
- **Balanced judgement:** digital activism has widened accountability and participation, but it cannot replace parties, parliament and courts. Without institutions, online anger produces confrontation rather than reform.

## 5. Effects of the imbalance

| Negative | Counter-points made by defenders |
|---|---|
| Interrupted democratic learning; no prime minister has completed a five-year term | Military governments delivered periods of growth and order |
| Weak parliament and parties; politics by engineering | Civilian failures created the vacuum |
| Security-first foreign policy; delayed normalisation with neighbours | Threats are real: India, Afghanistan, terrorism |
| Grievances in smaller provinces (the army is drawn mainly from Punjab and Khyber Pakhtunkhwa) | Recruitment has been broadened |
| Blurred accountability: power without responsibility and responsibility without power | Joint forums (National Security Committee, SIFC, apex committees) improve coordination |
| Resources: defence about Rs 3 trillion in the 2026–27 budget, plus pensions | Defence is under 2.5% of GDP and has saved the country in war |

## 6. Way forward (what "pragmatic" relations would mean — 2024)

1. **Performance first.** Civilian authority grows with good governance, clean administration and economic results.
2. **Institutional forums, not personal deals.** A National Security Committee with a permanent secretariat; regular Cabinet Committee on Defence; active parliamentary committees on defence with proper briefings.
3. **Stronger parties:** internal elections, transparent funding, policy units.
4. **Political consensus:** a renewed Charter of Democracy in which parties agree not to use institutions against each other, and accept election results.
5. **Credible elections** under an Election Commission all sides trust.
6. **Rule of law:** courts that do not validate departures from the constitution; civilian trials for civilians.
7. **Local government** as a nursery of leadership.
8. **Civilian capacity** in policing and counter-terrorism (NACTA, provincial CTDs) so that the army is not the default.
9. **Gradual, negotiated change.** Indonesia and Turkey show that the balance shifts step by step, when civilians deliver and the military is reassured about its core interests.
10. **Regional peace.** A lower threat from India and Afghanistan would reduce the weight of security in national life.
