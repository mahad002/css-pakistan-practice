## 1. From shortage to surplus that no one can afford

| Phase | Problem |
|---|---|
| 1994–2000 | Private power policy brought in **independent power producers (IPPs)** on imported fuel oil with guaranteed dollar-indexed returns |
| 2006–2017 | **Shortage:** demand outran supply; power cuts of 8–18 hours; industry crippled |
| 2015–2022 | **CPEC and LNG plants** added about 12,000–14,000 MW, mostly coal and gas, on "take-or-pay" contracts |
| 2022–present | **Surplus capacity and unaffordable prices:** capacity about twice average demand; consumers pay for idle plants; grid demand is falling as users switch to rooftop solar |

## 3. The problems

### 3.1 Capacity payments

- Under take-or-pay contracts the state pays plants a fixed charge whether or not it takes their power.
- Capacity payments run to about **Rs 2 trillion a year** and make up well over half of the price of a unit.
- When demand falls (recession, high prices, solar), the same fixed bill is spread over fewer units, so the price rises again. This is the "death spiral".

### 3.2 Circular debt

A chain of unpaid bills: consumers and government departments do not pay distribution companies in full → distributors cannot pay the central purchaser → it cannot pay generators → they cannot pay fuel suppliers.

**Causes:** transmission and distribution losses of about 16–18% (far higher in Peshawar, Quetta, Sukkur, Hyderabad companies); bill recovery below 100%; tariffs set below cost and subsidies not paid on time; late payment surcharges; capacity charges.

In 2025 the government borrowed about Rs 1.2 trillion from banks to retire part of the stock, to be repaid through a surcharge on bills. The flow resumed in 2025–26.

### 3.3 Costly, imported fuel mix

- Dependence on imported LNG, coal, furnace oil and crude makes energy the biggest item in the import bill and exposes the country to price shocks: Ukraine in 2022, and **the closure of the Strait of Hormuz during the 2026 Iran war**.
- Long-term LNG contracts with Qatar oblige Pakistan to take cargoes it no longer needs as solar cuts gas-fired generation.

### 3.4 Gas

- Domestic gas (Sui and others) is depleting; no major new discovery for years.
- Cheap piped gas for households and fertiliser, cross-subsidised by industry.
- Winter shortages; gas-sector circular debt.

### 3.5 Transmission and distribution

- A weak grid cannot move cheap southern power (Thar coal, nuclear, wind) north; forced reliance on costlier plants.
- A nationwide blackout in January 2023 showed the grid's fragility.
- State-owned distribution companies are inefficient and politicised.

### 3.6 Governance and planning

- Too many bodies: the Power Division, NEPRA, the central purchaser, the grid company, distribution companies, provincial energy departments.
- Policy made in crisis mode: each shortage answered by expensive quick capacity.
- Demand over-forecast; no integrated plan until the IGCEP.
- Delays in hydropower (file 12).

### 3.7 The solar disruption

- **Benefit:** cheap, clean power for households, farms (tube wells) and factories; less imported fuel.
- **Cost:** better-off users leave the grid by day and return at night; fixed costs shift to poorer users who cannot afford panels.
- **Policy response:** new solar users were moved from net metering to **net billing** with a lower buy-back rate **[verify details]**; a levy on imported panels was debated.

### Was the crisis a failure of strategy and will? (2020)

Largely yes. Planners knew of the coming shortage in the early 2000s; hydropower and local coal were delayed by politics; contracts were signed in haste on terms that shifted every risk to the state; losses and theft were tolerated for electoral reasons; tariffs were held down before elections and raised afterwards. External shocks (oil prices, the rupee's fall) made it worse but did not cause it.

## 4. Effects

| Area | Effect |
|---|---|
| Industry and exports | Among the highest industrial tariffs in the region; factories closed or moved to captive and solar power; textile competitiveness lost |
| Public finances | Subsidies and bail-outs of over Rs 1 trillion a year; the largest single risk to the IMF programme |
| Households | Bills taking a large share of income; protests (Azad Kashmir 2024, traders' strikes) |
| External account | Fuel imports of about $15–17 billion a year |
| Jobs and growth | The shortages of 2008–15 were estimated to cost about 2% of GDP a year |
| Social | Unrest; the poor cross-subsidising those with solar |
| Environment | Coal lock-in; smog — though hydro, nuclear and solar are raising the clean share |
| Federation | Disputes over net hydel profit, gas priority (Art. 158) and loss-making distribution companies |
| Foreign relations | Arrears to Chinese power producers strain CPEC; the Iran–Pakistan pipeline is stalled by US sanctions and carries a penalty risk |

## 5. What has been done (2024–26)

- Contracts with older IPPs ended or revised to "take-and-pay"; returns on government plants cut; talks with Chinese IPPs on re-profiling debt.
- An **independent system and market operator (ISMO)** and steps towards a competitive wholesale market (CTBCM).
- Plans to privatise distribution companies, starting with the better ones.
- Captive gas plants pushed onto the grid; winter and incremental-use packages to raise demand; cheaper tariffs for electric vehicles and data centres.
- The **Indicative Generation Capacity Expansion Plan** trimmed: thousands of megawatts of planned projects dropped.
- Hydropower: Dasu, Mohmand, Diamer-Bhasha; nuclear: C-5.
- Refinery upgrade policy; Russian crude trials; offshore and onshore exploration bids; US interest in oil and minerals.

## 6. Alternative and renewable sources (2017, 2021)

| Source | Potential | Status |
|---|---|---|
| **Hydropower** | About 60,000 MW | About 11,500 MW installed |
| **Solar** | Among the best sunshine in the world, especially Balochistan and Sindh | Quaid-i-Azam Solar Park (Bahawalpur); the rooftop boom |
| **Wind** | About 50,000 MW in the Gharo–Jhimpir corridor | About 1,800 MW installed |
| **Thar coal** | 175 billion tonnes | Several mine-mouth plants (about 3,300 MW); local but carbon-heavy |
| **Nuclear** | — | About 3,500 MW; C-5 under way |
| **Biomass and bagasse; waste-to-energy** | Sugar mills; city waste | Small |
| **Regional imports** | CASA-1000 (Tajik and Kyrgyz hydropower), TAPI, Iran | Held back by Afghanistan and sanctions |

Policy target (Alternative and Renewable Energy Policy 2019): 30% of capacity from wind, solar and similar sources by 2030, on top of hydro.

**Why renewables matter:** local, so they save foreign exchange; cheapest new power; quick to build; climate commitments; energy security.

**Barriers:** surplus contracted thermal capacity leaves no room; a weak grid and no storage; policy reversals; financing.

## 7. Way forward

1. **Renegotiate and retire** expensive contracts fairly and transparently; convert imported-coal plants to Thar coal.
2. **Raise demand on the grid:** electrify transport, industry heat, cooking; competitive marginal-cost tariffs.
3. **Fix distribution:** privatise or contract out management; smart meters; cut theft; let provinces own their companies.
4. **Strengthen the grid:** north–south lines; storage; flexible operations to absorb solar.
5. **A fair tariff design:** fixed charges for grid access; targeted support for the poor through BISP rather than cheap units for all.
6. **Stop adding take-or-pay capacity;** competitive bidding only.
7. **Local resources:** hydro, Thar, exploration, and renewables with storage.
8. **Gas reform:** price rationally; weighted average cost of gas; cut losses.
9. **Regional trade** in power and gas when politics allow.
10. **One empowered regulator and an integrated energy plan** that survives changes of government.
11. **Conservation:** building codes, efficient fans and motors, early market closing.
