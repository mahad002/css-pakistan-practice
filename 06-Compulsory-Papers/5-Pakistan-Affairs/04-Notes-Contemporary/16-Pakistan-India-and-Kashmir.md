### The disputes

1. **Kashmir** — the core.
2. **Water** — the Indus Waters Treaty (file 12).
3. **Siachen** — the world's highest battlefield since 1984; the line beyond map point NJ9842 was never marked. A 1989 understanding to withdraw was not carried out.
4. **Sir Creek** — a 96-km tidal estuary between Sindh and Gujarat; affects the maritime boundary and economic zone.
5. **Terrorism** — each accuses the other: India cites Mumbai, Pathankot, Pulwama, Pahalgam; Pakistan cites Balochistan, the TTP, the Jadhav case and assassinations on its soil.
6. **Trade and transit** — suspended since 2019; most-favoured-nation status never fully exchanged.
7. **Nuclear and missile competition.**

### Why relations stay hostile

- The unfinished business of Partition and two opposed national ideas.
- Kashmir as a matter of identity for both states.
- Asymmetry of size and power; Pakistan's fear of dominance, India's refusal of parity.
- Domestic politics: hard lines win votes; the rise of Hindutva nationalism in India; the role of security establishments.
- The "spoilers": each peace move has been followed by an attack or crisis.
- No sustained dialogue structure since 2008; weak crisis-management links.

## B. The Kashmir issue

### 1. Origins

- **Treaty of Amritsar (16 March 1846):** the British sold Kashmir to the Dogra ruler Gulab Singh for Rs 7.5 million.
- **1947:** the state had a Muslim majority of about 77% and a Hindu ruler, Maharaja **Hari Singh**. By geography, population, rivers and roads it was linked to Pakistan. The Maharaja signed a standstill agreement with Pakistan and delayed.
- **Poonch rising** against the Maharaja; killings of Muslims in Jammu; **tribesmen from the Frontier entered on 22 October 1947**.
- The Maharaja signed the **Instrument of Accession to India (26 October 1947)**; Indian troops flew into Srinagar on **27 October**. Mountbatten accepted the accession with the note that the people's wishes would be ascertained. Pakistan has always disputed the accession's validity and timing.
- The **Gurdaspur** award had given India its road into the state.

### 2. At the United Nations

| Date | Step |
|---|---|
| 1 January 1948 | India took the case to the Security Council |
| 20 January 1948 | Resolution 39: UN Commission for India and Pakistan (UNCIP) |
| **21 April 1948** | **Resolution 47:** a ceasefire, withdrawal of tribesmen and Pakistani nationals, reduction of Indian forces to the minimum, and a **free and impartial plebiscite** |
| 13 August 1948 and 5 January 1949 | UNCIP resolutions accepted by both: ceasefire, truce agreement, plebiscite |
| **1 January 1949** | Ceasefire; **Karachi Agreement (27 July 1949)** fixed the ceasefire line; UN observers (UNMOGIP) |
| 1950–58 | Mediators — Sir Owen Dixon, Frank Graham, Gunnar Jarring — failed to get agreed demilitarisation |
| 1957 | Resolution 122: an elected assembly in Kashmir cannot decide the state's future in place of a plebiscite. Soviet vetoes began to block further action |
| After 1972 | India treats the issue as bilateral under Simla. The Council met informally on Kashmir in August 2019 for the first time in decades, and again in 2025 |

### 3. Positions

| Pakistan | India | Kashmiris |
|---|---|---|
| A disputed territory whose future must be decided by its people in a UN plebiscite. The accession was fraudulent and conditional | An integral part of India by a legal accession. The only issue is "Pakistan-occupied" territory and cross-border terrorism. Simla makes it bilateral | Divided: accession to Pakistan, independence (JKLF), or autonomy within India (the mainstream parties). All object to being left out of talks |

### 4. Phases

- **1947–53:** war and UN mediation; Article 370 gave the state special status in India's constitution; Sheikh Abdullah dismissed and jailed (1953).
- **1954–88:** erosion of autonomy; wars of 1965 and 1971; the Line of Control (1972); the Indira–Abdullah accord (1975).
- **1989–2003:** after the rigged state election of 1987, a mass armed uprising; heavy Indian deployment; tens of thousands killed; the All Parties Hurriyat Conference (1993); Kargil.
- **2004–08:** dialogue; cross-LoC bus (2005) and trade (2008). **Musharraf's four points:** no change of borders, but make them irrelevant through free movement; self-governance on both sides; phased demilitarisation; a joint supervisory mechanism. Reported close to agreement in 2007, then lost in Pakistan's domestic crisis.
- **2008–18:** stone-pelting uprisings (2008, 2010, 2016 after Burhan Wani's death); pellet-gun injuries; UN human rights reports (2018, 2019).
- **Since 5 August 2019:** India ended Articles 370 and 35A, split the state into two **Union Territories** (Jammu and Kashmir; Ladakh), detained leaders, and imposed a long communications shutdown. New domicile and land rules followed, which Pakistan and Kashmiris describe as demographic change. India's Supreme Court upheld the move (11 December 2023). Assembly elections were held in 2024 (National Conference government under Omar Abdullah) with limited powers; statehood has not been restored **[verify 2026 status]**.

### 5. Kashmir and the weakness of the Security Council (2026 question)

1. **Chapter VI resolutions** are recommendations; they need the parties' consent to implement.
2. **The veto:** the USSR shielded India from 1957; today no permanent member wants to confront India, a large market and strategic partner.
3. **Great-power interest decides enforcement:** compare East Timor or Kuwait with Kashmir and Palestine.
4. **Bilateralism:** India uses Simla to keep the Council out; the Council has let the issue lie on its agenda without action.
5. **No mechanism to enforce a plebiscite** or to supervise demilitarisation without both sides' agreement.
6. **Structural reform is blocked** by the same veto. (Note: Pakistan opposes new permanent seats — which would include India — and supports more elected seats, through the "Uniting for Consensus" group.)

**Balance:** the UN did achieve a ceasefire, keeps observers on the line, and its resolutions remain the legal basis of Pakistan's case. But it has failed to deliver self-determination. The failure is one of political will among the powerful as much as of structure.

### 6. Options for a settlement

| Option | Problem |
|---|---|
| Plebiscite under UN resolutions | India refuses |
| LoC as the permanent border | Unacceptable to Pakistan and Kashmiris as it stands |
| Independence | Rejected by both states |
| Partition on communal or regional lines (the Dixon and Chenab formulas) | Never agreed |
| Soft borders and self-rule (the four points) | The most practical so far; needs trust that is absent |
| Status quo | The actual outcome; costly and dangerous |

### 7. Pakistan's policy assessed (2025 question)

- **Constant elements:** diplomatic, political and moral support for self-determination; raising the issue at the UN, OIC and human rights bodies; rejection of the 2019 changes.
- **Tools tried:** war (1947–48, 1965), UN diplomacy, support for the insurgency in the 1990s (which India and others used to recast the issue as terrorism), limited war (Kargil), dialogue and back channel (2004–07), and since 2019 a diplomatic and legal campaign.
- **Results:** the issue is alive internationally, but no instrument has changed the territorial status. Militancy damaged the cause's standing. The 2019 response had limited effect.
- **After May 2025:** international attention returned; President Trump offered to mediate; India refused third-party involvement.

## C. The 2025 conflict and after (to 23 Nov 2026)

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| 22 April 2025 | Gunmen killed 26 people, mostly tourists, at **Pahalgam**. India blamed Pakistan-based groups; Pakistan denied involvement and offered a neutral inquiry |
| 23–24 April | India held the Indus Waters Treaty in abeyance, closed the Attari crossing, expelled diplomats. Pakistan closed its airspace to Indian airlines, suspended trade, and said it would hold the Simla Agreement in abeyance |
| 7 May | India launched **Operation Sindoor**: missile and air strikes on sites in Punjab and Azad Kashmir. Pakistan reported civilian deaths and said it shot down several Indian aircraft, including Rafales; India later acknowledged losses without numbers |
| 8–9 May | Drone and missile exchanges; heavy shelling on the LoC |
| 10 May | India struck Pakistani air bases; Pakistan launched **Operation Bunyan-um-Marsoos** against Indian bases. A **ceasefire** was announced the same day by President Trump after US and other mediation. India says it was agreed directly between the two militaries |
| After | Both claimed victory. General Asim Munir was made Field Marshal. India declared a "new normal": any major attack would bring strikes on Pakistan |

**Position at November 2026:** no dialogue; the treaty remains suspended; trade, airspace and visas remain closed; the two sides exchanged accusations at the SCO; reports speak of several unofficial Track II and back-channel meetings in third countries focused on preventing escalation; sporadic firing on the LoC ([Al Jazeera, May 2026](https://aljazeera.com/news/2026/5/10/two-wins-two-losses-what-india-pakistan-have-learned-a-year-after-war); [News on AIR](https://www.newsonair.gov.in/india-says-indus-waters-treaty-to-remain-in-abeyance-until-pakistan-ends-support-to-terrorism)).

**What the conflict changed**

1. Conventional strikes deep inside each other's territory are now part of the repertoire for both.
2. Drones and stand-off missiles shorten warning time.
3. Water has been added to the list of weapons.
4. Third-party crisis management (the USA, Gulf states) proved decisive again, despite India's doctrine of bilateralism.
5. Kashmir is back on the international agenda, but the bilateral track is at its lowest since 1971.

## D. Way forward

1. **Crisis stability first:** DGMO hotline, restoration of the LoC ceasefire understanding, new agreements on drones and missile notifications, and a political back channel.
2. **Restore the treaty's functioning,** starting with data-sharing.
3. **A joint or neutral mechanism on terrorism,** which Pakistan has proposed.
4. **Step-by-step normalisation:** high commissioners, visas for pilgrims and divided families, limited trade.
5. **Kashmir:** relief for the people first — release of prisoners, end of restrictions, restoration of statehood, cross-LoC travel and trade — then talks that include Kashmiris, building on the four-point ideas.
6. **For Pakistan:** keep the case legal and political; avoid anything that lets it be framed as terrorism; strengthen self-rule and development in Azad Kashmir and Gilgit-Baltistan; build economic strength, the true source of leverage.
7. **Use multilateral forums** (the SCO, where both sit) for quiet contact.
