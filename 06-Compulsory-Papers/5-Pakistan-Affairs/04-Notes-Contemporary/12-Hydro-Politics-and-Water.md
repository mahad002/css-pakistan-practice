# 12. Hydro Politics: Water Issues in Domestic and Regional Context

Syllabus item XVII. Asked in 2019 (delay in building dams) and 2025 (the Indus Waters Treaty). With the treaty held in abeyance by India since April 2025, this is one of the most likely questions for 2028.

## 1. The water picture

| Fact | Figure |
|---|---|
| Source | The Indus system supplies almost all surface water; about 60–70% of flow comes from snow and glacier melt |
| Annual flow | About 140–145 million acre-feet (MAF) on average, highly seasonal: over 80% arrives in about 100 days of summer |
| Use | About 90–93% goes to agriculture |
| Per-person availability | About 5,000 cubic metres in 1951; **under 1,000 today** (the "water scarce" line) |
| Storage | About **30 days** of supply (India about 170 days; the USA's Colorado about 900). Live storage about 13–14 MAF, shrinking with silt |
| Dependence | Over three-quarters of the water originates outside Pakistan's borders |
| Losses | Around half of canal water is lost before it reaches the crop; about 30 MAF flows to the sea in an average year, far more in flood years |
| Groundwater | Over a million tube wells; water tables falling in Punjab and Balochistan; arsenic and salinity |
| Cities | Karachi, Quetta, Lahore, Islamabad all face shortages or falling aquifers |

Useful references: the World Bank's *Pakistan: Getting More from Water* (2019); the **National Water Policy (2018)**; John Briscoe's writings on the Indus; Daanish Mustafa on water governance.

## 2. Regional dimension I: India and the Indus Waters Treaty

### Origin

- Partition cut the canal system: headworks in India, canals in Pakistan.
- **1 April 1948:** East Punjab stopped the water. **Inter-Dominion Agreement, 4 May 1948.**
- 1951: David Lilienthal proposed joint development; the **World Bank** (President Eugene Black) mediated for nine years.
- **Signed at Karachi on 19 September 1960** by President Ayub Khan, Prime Minister Nehru and W.A.B. Iliff of the World Bank.

### Terms

| Provision | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Eastern rivers** — Ravi, Beas, Sutlej | For India's unrestricted use (about 33 MAF) |
| **Western rivers** — Indus, Jhelum, Chenab | For Pakistan (about 135 MAF). India may use them for domestic needs, limited agriculture, and **run-of-river hydropower** within design limits set in the annexures |
| Transition | Ten years, during which Pakistan built replacement works with a fund from the World Bank, donors and India (India paid about £62 million): **Mangla** and **Tarbela** dams, barrages and link canals |
| **Permanent Indus Commission** | One commissioner each; annual meetings; data exchange; inspections |
| **Dispute settlement (Art. IX)** | Three steps: the Commission → a **Neutral Expert** for technical "differences" → a **Court of Arbitration** for "disputes" |
| Duration | No end date and **no clause for unilateral suspension or exit**; it can be changed only by a new agreed treaty (Art. XII) |

### How it shaped relations (2025 question)

**Positive:** survived the wars of 1965, 1971 and 1999; gave both countries certainty to build their irrigation systems; often cited as the world's most successful water treaty.

**Negative, from Pakistan's side:** it lost three rivers outright; as the lower riparian it depends on India's good faith on the other three; India's dams on the Jhelum and Chenab, taken together, give it some ability to alter the timing of flows.

**Negative, from India's side:** it received only about a fifth of the system's water; design limits make its projects in Kashmir slower and costlier; Kashmiri politicians complain that the state bears the cost.

### Disputes

| Project | River | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| Salal | Chenab | Settled bilaterally, 1978 |
| Wullar Barrage / Tulbul | Jhelum | Unresolved since the 1980s |
| **Baglihar** | Chenab | **Neutral Expert (Raymond Lafitte), 2007:** largely upheld India's design with changes |
| **Kishanganga** | Neelum/Jhelum tributary | **Court of Arbitration, 2013:** India may divert water for power but must release a minimum flow and may not draw the reservoir down below dead storage for flushing |
| **Kishanganga and Ratle** designs | Jhelum / Chenab | From 2016 Pakistan sought a Court of Arbitration and India a Neutral Expert; the World Bank started both in 2022; India boycotted the Court |

### The crisis since 2023

- **January 2023 and August 2024:** India served notices seeking to renegotiate the treaty, citing changed circumstances.
- **23 April 2025:** the day after the Pahalgam attack, India announced it was holding the treaty **"in abeyance"** until Pakistan ended support for cross-border terrorism. It stopped sharing data, flushed reservoirs on the Chenab, and announced faster work on projects (Tulbul, Pakal Dul, Kiru, Ratle, a Chenab–Ravi link).
- **Pakistan's response:** called any diversion of its water "an act of war"; said the treaty has no suspension clause; raised the matter at the World Bank, the UN and the Court.
- **Court of Arbitration (The Hague, administered by the Permanent Court of Arbitration):** in a **Supplemental Award on Competence (27 June 2025)** it held that India's "abeyance" did not affect its competence. In an **Award on Issues of General Interpretation (8 August 2025)** it held that the general rule of the treaty is that India shall "let flow" the waters of the western rivers for Pakistan's unrestricted use, and it set out the design limits on India's run-of-river plants. Further rulings followed: a clarification (November 2025), an award on maximum pondage (15 May 2026), and an **Award on the Status of the Indus Waters Treaty with an Order on Interim Measures (31 August 2026)**. India does not take part and rejects the Court's authority. Pakistan has welcomed each ruling ([PCA press release](https://pca-cpa.org/en/news/pca-press-release-pca-case-no-2023-01-proceedings-under-the-indus-waters-treaty-islamic-republic-of-pakistan-v-republic-of-india-3/); [Dawn](https://www.dawn.com/news/2000952/pakistan-hails-pcas-decision-in-iwt-proceedings-says-it-affirms-limits-on-indias-water-control-capability)). **Read the August 2026 award's summary before the exam; I have its title but not its contents.**
- **Status at November 2026:** unchanged. India's foreign ministry repeated on 21 November 2026 that there was "no change" in its position; Pakistan calls the move illegal. A Pakistani minister attending an SCO forum in New Delhi said he had not been invited to any bilateral talks ([News on AIR](https://www.newsonair.gov.in/india-says-indus-waters-treaty-to-remain-in-abeyance-until-pakistan-ends-support-to-terrorism); [The Nation](https://www.nation.com.pk/20-Nov-2026/india-s-unilateral-move-indus-waters-treaty-denounced)).

### What can India actually do?

- **Short term:** limited. It lacks the storage to stop the western rivers; it can change the timing of flows by filling and flushing existing dams, which hurts sowing in Pakistan and creates flood or drought scares; it can withhold flood data.
- **Long term:** if it builds storage and diversion works over ten to fifteen years, the threat becomes real.
- **Pakistan's cards:** the law (the Vienna Convention on treaties; no suspension clause); the World Bank's role; the Court's awards; international opinion; China's position upstream of India on the Brahmaputra and Sutlej; and its own stated red line.

## 3. Regional dimension II: Afghanistan and the Kabul River

- The Kabul River and its tributaries (Kunar/Chitral) bring roughly 16–17 MAF into Pakistan — about a tenth or more of its flow. The Kunar rises in Chitral, enters Afghanistan and returns.
- **There is no water treaty with Afghanistan.**
- Afghan plans for dams (Shahtoot near Kabul with past Indian help; a Kunar dam announced by the Taliban) could cut flows to the Peshawar valley and Warsak.
- With the two countries in open conflict in 2025–26, talks are not in sight. The Taliban's Qosh Tepa canal on the Amu Darya shows their intent to use water.
- **Need:** a basin agreement on data, minimum flows and joint projects, perhaps with World Bank or Chinese help.

## 4. Domestic dimension: provinces and water

### The Water Apportionment Accord, 1991

| Province | Share (MAF) |
|---|---|
| Punjab | 55.94 |
| Sindh | 48.76 |
| Khyber Pakhtunkhwa | 5.78 (plus 3.00 for ungauged civil canals) |
| Balochistan | 3.87 |
| **Total** | **114.35** (117.35 with the civil canals) |

- Signed 16 March 1991; approved by the CCI. **IRSA** (Indus River System Authority, 1992) implements it.
- It assumed new dams that were never built, so actual supplies are below the Accord figures.
- **The quarrel:** how to share shortages. Punjab prefers historical use (the "three-tier formula"); Sindh insists on the Accord's paragraph 2 shares. Sindh also demands a guaranteed flow below Kotri (10 MAF was discussed) to stop the sea eating into the delta.
- Balochistan complains that Sindh does not pass on its share. Mistrust over measurement persists; telemetry has failed repeatedly.

### Kalabagh Dam

- Proposed on the Indus in Punjab (about 6 MAF storage, 3,600 MW). Studied since the 1950s; ready by the 1980s.
- **Opposed** by resolutions of the Sindh, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and Balochistan assemblies: Sindh fears less water and a dying delta; Khyber Pakhtunkhwa fears flooding of Nowshera and waterlogging; all distrust Punjab's control of the canals.
- It has become a symbol of the trust deficit. No government has proceeded.

### The Cholistan canals (2024–25)

- A plan for six new canals, including one for Cholistan under the Green Pakistan Initiative, set off protests across Sindh in early 2025. The PPP threatened to leave the coalition.
- On **28 April 2025 the CCI decided that no new canal would be built without consensus** among the provinces and shelved the plan.

### Why dams are delayed (2019)

1. **Provincial distrust** (Kalabagh above all).
2. **Money:** Diamer-Bhasha alone costs many billions of dollars; lenders hesitated because Gilgit-Baltistan is disputed territory.
3. **Political discontinuity:** each government re-orders priorities; projects start and stall.
4. **Weak institutions:** WAPDA's capacity, slow approvals, litigation, land acquisition and resettlement.
5. **Security and geology** at the sites.
6. **Preference for quick thermal power** in the 1990s and 2010s.
7. **Disputes with India** tying up attention.

**Costs of delay:** floods wasted, droughts unbuffered, cheap hydropower forgone and replaced by imported fuel, silt eating existing storage.

**Under way:** **Diamer-Bhasha** (about 4,500 MW and 6.4 MAF live storage; completion expected near the end of the decade), **Dasu** (4,320 MW in two stages), **Mohmand** (about 800 MW; flood protection for Peshawar valley). After April 2025 the government spoke of accelerating storage as a national-security priority.

### Other domestic problems

- Waste: flood irrigation; thirsty crops (sugarcane, rice) priced as if water were free; canal water charges (*abiana*) recover a fraction of costs.
- Pollution: untreated sewage and industrial waste.
- Climate: glacier retreat, erratic monsoons, floods in 2010, 2022 and 2025.
- Governance: water is split across federal and provincial bodies; the 2018 policy is weakly implemented.

## 5. Analysis

1. **Scarcity is as much about management as about supply.** Pakistan gets less output per unit of water than almost any major farming country.
2. **Dams alone will not solve it.** Storage is needed, but conservation and pricing give faster and cheaper gains.
3. **Internal distrust weakens the external case.** A country whose provinces cannot agree on measurement is poorly placed to face an upstream neighbour.
4. **The treaty crisis is political.** Its legal answer is clear; its solution requires a wider thaw with India.
5. **Climate change** makes historical flow assumptions unreliable for every agreement, internal and external.

## 6. Way forward

**With India:** insist on the treaty's own machinery; keep the legal and diplomatic case active (World Bank, Court of Arbitration, UN); seek restoration of data-sharing as a first step; be open to discussing climate and environmental flows within the treaty framework; never link water with force first.

**With Afghanistan:** a Kabul River agreement when relations allow; joint hydropower.

**At home**

1. Complete Bhasha, Dasu and Mohmand; build small and medium dams and recharge works.
2. Line canals and watercourses; laser land-levelling; drip and sprinkler systems with subsidies.
3. Price water sensibly; shift cropping away from the thirstiest crops.
4. Real-time **telemetry** at every barrage, run by a body all provinces trust.
5. Update the 1991 Accord's operating rules through the CCI; guarantee environmental flow below Kotri.
6. Regulate groundwater.
7. Treat and reuse urban wastewater; harvest rain; fix city supply systems.
8. Flood-plain zoning and early warning.
9. Implement the National Water Policy with a strong National Water Council.
