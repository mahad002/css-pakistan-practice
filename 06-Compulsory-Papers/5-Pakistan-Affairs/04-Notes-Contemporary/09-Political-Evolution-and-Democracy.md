## 1. Phases since 1971

| Phase | Character |
|---|---|
| 1971–77 | Elected civilian government; 1973 Constitution; populism and centralisation |
| 1977–88 | Martial law; Islamisation; non-party politics; presidential dominance through the 8th Amendment |
| 1988–99 | Alternating PPP and PML-N governments; four dismissals; ended by a coup |
| 1999–2008 | Military-led government with a managed parliament |
| 2008–18 | Democratic transition: two completed parliaments, the 18th Amendment, the first handover between elected governments |
| 2018–22 | "Hybrid" arrangement under the PTI government |
| 2022–present | Polarisation between PTI and the rest; disputed 2024 election; constitutional changes (26th, 27th); rising executive and military weight |

## 2. Features of the political system

- Federal, parliamentary, bicameral; written constitution; fundamental rights; independent judiciary in principle.
- In practice: a strong executive; a weak legislature (heavy use of ordinances, thin debate, rushed amendments); a politically active judiciary until 2024 and a reshaped one since; an influential military; weak local government.

### Which system suits Pakistan? (2018)

| Parliamentary | Presidential |
|---|---|
| Chosen by consensus in 1973; represents a diverse federation; gives small provinces a voice through coalitions and the Senate | Tried under Ayub (1962) and in effect under Zia and Musharraf; identified with authoritarian rule |
| Executive accountable to the house every day | Fixed term gives stability and lets a leader pick experts |
| Weakness: unstable coalitions, floor-crossing, bargaining | Weakness: a president from the largest province would deepen the others' sense of exclusion; winner takes all |

**Judgement:** the problem has not been the form but its working: interrupted terms, weak parties, no local government. The parliamentary federal system fits a multi-ethnic country; it needs to be allowed to run and to be reformed from within.

## 3. Political culture (2020)

Political culture is the set of attitudes and habits with which people and leaders approach politics.

1. **Personalised and dynastic:** loyalty to leaders and families (Bhutto-Zardari, Sharif, and regional families) more than to programmes.
2. **Patron–client ties:** voters trade support for access to jobs, police and courts (*thana-katcheri* politics); biradari and tribe organise votes.
3. **"Electables":** locally powerful candidates who move between parties.
4. **Confrontation over compromise:** winners monopolise, losers reject results; street agitation, long marches and sit-ins.
5. **Reliance on non-elected arbiters:** parties look to the military or courts to settle disputes.
6. **Religion in politics:** as a source of legitimacy and of mobilisation.
7. **Ethnic and provincial identity** shaping party support (PPP in Sindh, PML-N in central Punjab, PTI in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, nationalist parties in Balochistan).
8. **Low trust and conspiracy thinking;** weak tolerance of dissent.
9. **Newer features:** an urban middle class and youth mobilised through social media; populism; a vibrant if pressured media; women voters and candidates in growing numbers; judicial and legal activism.

## 4. Why democracy has not taken root (2023)

1. **A late and interrupted start:** no constitution for nine years, no general election for twenty-three, three long periods of military rule.
2. **Civil–military imbalance** (file 04).
3. **Weak parties:** no internal elections; family control; no research or policy capacity; dependence on electables.
4. **Feudal and tribal structures** (2024): landlords and sardars control votes, police and local administration in parts of Sindh, south Punjab and Balochistan. But note the limits of this argument: Pakistan is now about 39% urban, and business families and urban professionals are as prominent as landlords.
5. **Dynastic politics** (2024): blocks merit and renewal; turns parties into inheritances. Counter-point: dynasties exist across South Asia; voters keep choosing them because they supply recognition and continuity where institutions are weak.
6. **Judicial role:** validation of takeovers; disqualification of prime ministers (2012, 2017).
7. **Constitutional engineering:** 58(2)(b), the LFO, and since 2024 the 26th and 27th Amendments.
8. **Disputed elections:** 1977, 1990, 2002, 2018, 2024.
9. **No local government:** provinces resist devolving power; local bodies flourish mostly under military rulers.
10. **Low literacy, poverty and inequality:** citizens dependent on patrons.
11. **Intolerance and zero-sum rivalry** among politicians; accountability used as a weapon (NAB).
12. **Security environment:** wars and insurgencies justify exceptional powers.
13. **Economic dependence** on external patrons who preferred dealing with strongmen.
14. **Poor governance by elected governments,** which erodes public faith in the system.

**Counter-points worth making:** every period of military rule ended with a public demand for democracy; the 1973 Constitution has survived; the 18th Amendment was passed by consensus; parliaments completed terms in 2013, 2018 and 2023; voter turnout and youth participation are substantial. Democracy is contested, not absent.

## 5. Regional and nationalist parties (2022)

| Party | Base |
|---|---|
| Awami National Party (ANP) | Pashtun nationalism; heir of the Khudai Khidmatgars and NAP |
| Pakhtunkhwa Milli Awami Party (PkMAP) | Pashtuns of Balochistan |
| Balochistan National Party (Mengal); National Party | Baloch nationalism within the federation |
| MQM-Pakistan | Urdu-speaking urban Sindh |
| Sindhi nationalist groups (various) | Rural Sindh; small vote |
| Balochistan Awami Party; GDA | Province-level alliances |
| JUI-F | Religious party with a Pashtun belt base |

**Role:** they give smaller nationalities a voice; turn grievances into bargaining within the system rather than militancy; hold the balance in coalitions; drove provincial autonomy (the 18th Amendment, the NFC Award, the renaming of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa). **Risks:** narrow agendas, blackmail in coalitions, links in some cases to violence (Karachi in the 1990s–2000s). **Necessity:** in a federation they are a safety valve. When they are squeezed out — by engineering or by violence — the space is taken by armed groups, as in Balochistan.

## 6. Political stability and the economy (2021)

- Investors need predictable policy. Pakistan has had 24 or so prime ministers and repeated policy reversals.
- Each political crisis coincided with an economic one: 1996–99, 2007–08, 2017–18, 2022–23 (when default was narrowly avoided amid the PTI–PDM confrontation).
- IMF programmes are abandoned before elections and resumed after.
- Comparison: Bangladesh (until 2024), Vietnam and India grew under policy continuity.
- But the relation runs both ways: a stagnant economy breeds instability. And "stability" imposed without legitimacy does not reassure investors for long.

## 7. Current position (to 23 Nov 2026)

- **Government:** a PML-N-led coalition with the PPP's support. Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif; President Asif Ali Zardari. The coalition has had a two-thirds majority since the reserved-seats review of June 2025.
- **Opposition:** PTI and its allies in the *Tehreek-i-Tahaffuz-i-Aeen-i-Pakistan* (TTAP), led in Parliament by Mahmood Khan Achakzai. PTI's founder **Imran Khan has been in prison since August 2023**; he was sentenced to 14 years in the Al-Qadir Trust case (January 2025) and 17 years in a second Toshakhana case (December 2025). In March 2026 an anti-terrorism court sentenced dozens of PTI figures to ten years in the GHQ-attack case ([Dawn](https://www.dawn.com/news/1962237); [Dawn](https://www.dawn.com/news/1979447)). In November 2026 PTI raised concerns about his health and access to him ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/11/pakistans-pti-demands-access-to-jailed-khan-after-overnight-hospital-visit)).
- **Khyber Pakhtunkhwa:** talks between the federal government and PTI broke down on 4 October 2026 and PTI began province-wide protests for Khan's release. On **11 October 2026** the President proclaimed an **emergency and governor's rule** under Arts. 232 and 234, citing the provincial government's failure against militancy. Chief Minister **Sohail Afridi** and some ministers were taken into custody. A joint sitting of Parliament approved the measure. Petitions in the Peshawar High Court were taken off its list in early November after an order of the Federal Constitutional Court ([Al Jazeera explainer](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/12/what-do-emergency-and-governor-rule-in-pakistans-khyber-pakhtunkhwa-mean); [Tribune](https://tribune.com.pk/story/2639251/phc-strikes-off-petitions-against-governors-rule)). Governor's rule can run for two months at a time, up to six months.
- **Constitutional change:** the 27th Amendment (November 2025); talk of a 28th to create new provinces, opposed by the PPP for Sindh ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/17/is-pakistan-about-to-carve-out-new-provinces-inside-the-controversial-plan)).
- **Civic space:** PECA amendments (2025); restrictions on assemblies; the government cites security and disinformation, critics cite shrinking freedoms.
- **Local government:** elections overdue in Punjab and Islamabad **[verify]**.

**Two readings of the present.** *Government and supporters:* the country has stabilised its economy, won diplomatic standing, and dealt firmly with those who attacked state institutions on 9 May 2023. *Opposition and many independent observers:* the largest party has been kept from power, the judiciary restructured, and a province's elected government suspended, so stability lacks consent.

## 8. Reforms for a stable polity (2024)

**Constitutional and legal**

1. An **Election Commission** trusted by all: consensus appointments, financial autonomy, technology with audit trails; an agreed caretaker mechanism.
2. **Constitutional protection for local government:** fixed terms, direct election, a share of provincial funds (a Provincial Finance Commission with force).
3. **Intra-party democracy** and transparent party finance under the Elections Act.
4. Limits on **ordinances** and on rushed constitutional amendments (committee stage, public hearings).
5. **Judicial appointments** insulated from the executive of the day; clear jurisdictions.
6. An even-handed **accountability** law and body.
7. Consider proportional elements in the electoral system to reduce winner-takes-all outcomes.

**Political**

8. A **new charter of democracy and of the economy** among all major parties, including PTI: accept results, no use of institutions against rivals, continuity in core economic policy.
9. **Dialogue and release of political prisoners** through due process; an end to trials of civilians in military courts.
10. **Parliament as the forum:** strong committees; question time; opposition chairing the Public Accounts Committee.
11. **Civil–military coordination** through formal bodies under the cabinet.
12. Civic education; a free and responsible media.
