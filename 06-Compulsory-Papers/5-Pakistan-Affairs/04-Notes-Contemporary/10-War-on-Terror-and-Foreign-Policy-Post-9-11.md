# 10. Pakistan and the US War on Terror; Foreign Policy after 9/11

Syllabus items XIII and XIV. Asked in 2016 (post-9/11 foreign policy), 2018 (Pakistan–US relations since 2001), 2020 (US–Taliban peace process), 2022 (ideology versus national interest; post-withdrawal mistrust), 2023 (consequences of joining the war on terror).

## 1. Foundations of Pakistan's foreign policy

**Guiding statement.** Jinnah (broadcast of February 1948): friendliness and goodwill towards all nations; no aggressive designs; honesty and fair play in dealings; support for the oppressed.

**Constitution.** Art. 40: strengthen bonds with the Muslim world; support the common interests of Asia, Africa and Latin America; promote international peace; settle disputes peacefully.

**Determinants**

| Determinant | Effect |
|---|---|
| Security: India above all | Alliances, defence spending, the nuclear programme |
| Geography | Afghanistan, China, the Gulf, the Arabian Sea |
| Ideology | Ties with the Muslim world; Palestine; Kashmir |
| Economy | Need for aid, loans, markets, remittances, energy |
| Domestic structure | Who makes policy: Foreign Office, Prime Minister, the military |
| International system | Cold War, unipolar moment, US–China rivalry |

**Phases:** neutrality (1947–53) → Western alliances (1954–62) → bilateralism and China (1962–71) → non-alignment and the Muslim world (1972–79) → front-line state (1979–89) → isolation and sanctions (1990–2001) → war on terror (2001–21) → geo-economics and multi-alignment (2021– ).

## 2. Pakistan and the war on terror

### The decision of September 2001

After 9/11 the USA demanded cooperation against al-Qaeda and the Taliban, whom Pakistan had recognised. General Musharraf agreed within days.

**Why:** fear of being treated as an adversary; the risk of India offering bases instead; protecting the nuclear assets and the Kashmir cause; ending sanctions and winning economic relief.

**What Pakistan gave:** airspace, bases (Shamsi, Jacobabad), land and air supply routes into Afghanistan, intelligence, the arrest and handover of several hundred al-Qaeda suspects, and from 2004 its own army operations in the tribal areas.

### Phases of Pakistan–US relations since 2001 (2018)

| Period | Character | Key events |
|---|---|---|
| 2001–07 | Close cooperation | Sanctions lifted; debt rescheduled; **major non-NATO ally (2004)**; F-16 sales; Coalition Support Fund |
| 2008–10 | "Do more" | Drone strikes escalate; **Kerry–Lugar–Berman Act (2009)**: $7.5 billion of civilian aid over five years with conditions the military resented; Af-Pak strategy; strategic dialogue |
| 2011 | Breakdown | Raymond Davis (January); **Abbottabad raid killing Osama bin Laden (2 May)**; **Salala attack (26 November; 24 Pakistani soldiers killed)**; NATO supply routes closed for seven months; Shamsi base vacated |
| 2012–16 | Repair and drift | Routes reopened after a US apology; drawdown in Afghanistan; aid falls; US tilt to India |
| 2017–18 | Rupture | Trump's South Asia strategy (August 2017); security aid suspended (January 2018) |
| 2018–21 | Transactional | Pakistan facilitated the US–Taliban talks → **Doha Agreement (29 February 2020)**; US withdrawal and Taliban takeover (**August 2021**) |
| 2021–24 | Low point | No leader-level contact; Pakistan blamed in Washington for the Taliban's win; the "cipher" controversy (2022) fed anti-American feeling |
| 2025–26 | Reset | Cooperation on IS-K; Trump's credit-taking for the May 2025 ceasefire, which Pakistan welcomed and India rejected; trade deal with a 19% tariff; minerals MoU; Pakistan as go-between in the Iran war |

### Balance sheet (2023)

**Short-term gains**

- About $33 billion in US assistance and reimbursements over 2002–18 (of which roughly $14 billion was Coalition Support Fund repayment for Pakistan's own costs).
- Debt relief and rescheduling; removal of nuclear and coup-related sanctions.
- Military equipment; growth of 6–7% in 2004–07.
- International rehabilitation of a military government.

**Long-term costs**

- Over **80,000 Pakistani lives** and economic losses the government puts above **$150 billion**.
- The birth of the **TTP** (2007); suicide bombing in every major city; attacks on schools, mosques, markets and military bases.
- Displacement of millions from Swat and the tribal areas.
- **Drone strikes** (over 400 between 2004 and 2018) and the Abbottabad and Salala incidents: repeated breaches of sovereignty.
- Radicalisation and sectarian violence; damage to tourism, investment and the country's image.
- Dependence: aid postponed reform.
- A relationship of mutual suspicion: Washington accused Pakistan of a "double game"; Pakistanis felt used and abandoned.
- After 2021 the Afghan problem returned to Pakistan alone.

**Was there an alternative?** Refusal in 2001 would have risked isolation and possibly attack. The fairer criticism is of how the alliance was managed: no parliamentary debate, no written terms, secret permissions, and no plan for the tribal areas.

### After the US withdrawal: the mistrust (2022)

| US grievances | Pakistani grievances |
|---|---|
| Sanctuary for the Afghan Taliban and the Haqqani network during the war | Sacrifices unacknowledged; scapegoated for a US failure |
| Pakistan's closeness to China | The US built up India as a counterweight and ignored Kashmir |
| Nuclear and missile concerns | Discriminatory sanctions; no civil nuclear deal |
| Space for anti-US sentiment | Left alone with the TTP and with US weapons abandoned in Afghanistan |

**Basis for a healthier relationship:** counter-terrorism (IS-K), trade (the USA is Pakistan's largest export market), investment in minerals, energy and technology, education, climate, the diaspora — a relationship not routed through Afghanistan or India.

## 3. Foreign policy after 9/11: the wider picture (2016)

| Relationship | Development |
|---|---|
| **China** | From "all-weather friendship" to economic partnership: **CPEC (2015)**, the largest source of arms, JF-17 and J-10 aircraft, nuclear power plants, diplomatic cover at the UN and FATF |
| **India** | Stand-off 2001–02 → composite dialogue 2004–08 → Mumbai 2008 → sporadic contact → rupture 2019 → conflict May 2025 |
| **Afghanistan** | Support for the Bonn process; strained ties with Karzai and Ghani; facilitation of Doha; hopes of 2021 turned into open conflict by 2025–26 |
| **Iran** | Border management; the unfinished gas pipeline; strikes and counter-strikes in January 2024; mediation in 2026 |
| **Saudi Arabia and the Gulf** | Workers and remittances; deposits and oil on credit; stayed out of the Yemen war by a parliamentary resolution (2015); **Strategic Mutual Defence Agreement (September 2025)** |
| **Russia** | From Cold War hostility to cooperation: defence agreement (2014), joint exercises, energy, SCO |
| **Turkey, Azerbaijan** | Defence and diplomatic partnership |
| **Multilateral** | SCO membership (2017); FATF grey list (2018–22); UN Security Council seat (2012–13 and 2025–26); peacekeeping |
| **Doctrine** | From geo-strategy to **geo-economics** (National Security Policy 2022): connectivity, trade and development partnerships |

## 4. Ideology and emotion versus national interest (2022)

**The charge:** Pakistan made foreign policy on sentiment — pan-Islamic solidarity, the Afghan jihad, Kashmir at any price, refusing trade with India, non-recognition of Israel — and neglected economic interest.

**Evidence for it**

- The early hope of leading a Muslim bloc brought little return.
- "Strategic depth" in Afghanistan produced blowback.
- Trade with India and regional connectivity were sacrificed to the dispute.
- Policy on the Arab–Iranian rivalry was pulled by sectarian and financial ties.
- Economic diplomacy was an afterthought until recently.

**Evidence against it**

- The alliances of 1954–55, 1979 and 2001 were cold calculations of security and aid, and often cut against ideology (siding with the West over Suez; fighting co-religionists after 2001).
- Friendship with communist China was pure realism.
- Staying out of Yemen (2015) and balancing Riyadh and Tehran were interest-based.
- For a state founded on an idea, identity is part of the national interest.

**Judgement:** the real weakness was a **narrow, security-only definition of interest**, set by a small circle without debate, and a neglect of economics — rather than too much ideology.

## 5. Current position (to Nov 2026)

- **USA:** best relations in over a decade, resting on personal rapport at the top, minerals and counter-terrorism, and Pakistan's usefulness on Iran. Fragile: no formal trade agreement yet; sanctions on missile entities remain; Congress is cool ([CSIS](https://www.csis.org/analysis/minerals-diplomacy-meets-market-reality-case-pakistan); [Dawn](https://www.dawn.com/news/1974722)).
- **China:** steady; some friction over the security of Chinese workers, power-sector arrears and Pakistan's US opening.
- **Gulf:** the Saudi defence pact; investment pledges.
- **Iran war:** Pakistan mediating; exposed to spill-over.
- **Standing:** UN Security Council member; SCO chair; Board of Peace member.

## 6. Analysis and way forward

1. **Avoid single-patron dependence.** Each alliance ended in disappointment because it rested on one issue.
2. **Balance Washington and Beijing** openly; refuse zero-sum choices.
3. **Economics at the centre:** market access, investment, labour agreements, technology.
4. **Parliamentary oversight** of major commitments (the Saudi pact, any Gaza deployment).
5. **Neighbours first:** no foreign policy gain offsets two hostile borders.
6. **A professional Foreign Office** with a clear lead role.
7. **Soft power:** peacekeeping, mediation, diaspora, culture, sport.
