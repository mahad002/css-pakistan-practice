## 3. The IMF programme

- Pakistan has gone to the IMF more than twenty times since 1958.
- **Stand-By Arrangement**, July 2023 ($3 billion, nine months) — averted default.
- **Extended Fund Facility (EFF):** approved **25 September 2024**, 37 months, about **$7 billion**.
- **Resilience and Sustainability Facility (RSF):** approved 9 May 2025, about $1.4 billion, for climate resilience.
- **Third EFF review and second RSF review completed 8 May 2026**; about $1.2–1.3 billion released; total disbursed about **$4.8 billion** ([IMF press release](https://www.imf.org/en/news/articles/2026/05/08/pr-26147-pakistan-imf-completes-3rd-rev-of-extended-arrangement-under-eff-and-2nd-rev-arrang-rsf)). The programme runs to late 2027.

### Main conditions

1. A primary surplus each year; no supplementary grants without Parliament.
2. Raise tax-to-GDP by about three percentage points: tax agriculture (provinces legislated in 2025), retail and property; end exemptions.
3. Cost-recovery energy tariffs; cap circular debt; no new subsidies.
4. A market-determined exchange rate; tight monetary policy.
5. State-owned enterprise reform and privatisation; a sovereign wealth fund under rules.
6. A National Fiscal Pact shifting spending duties to provinces; provincial surpluses.
7. Governance and anti-corruption diagnostics; asset declarations by officials.
8. No new tax amnesties or special-zone incentives.

### IMF conditions: stabiliser or strait-jacket? (2026 question)

| Helps | Hurts |
|---|---|
| Averted default; rebuilt reserves; brought inflation down | Austerity slows growth and jobs; poverty has risen |
| Forces reforms that governments avoid (taxing agriculture, retail, property) | Higher power and gas tariffs and indirect taxes fall on the poor and on industry |
| Unlocks other lenders (World Bank, ADB, friendly countries, markets) | Each programme has ended with the same weaknesses; reforms are reversed when the money stops |
| Imposes fiscal discipline and transparency | Limits policy freedom; revenue targets met by squeezing existing taxpayers |

**Judgement:** the IMF treats the symptoms (a payments crisis). The causes are domestic: low exports, low savings, a narrow tax base, energy losses and elite privileges. Sustainability depends on whether Pakistan owns the reforms.

## 4. The structural problems

### 4.1 Low and unstable growth

- Boom–bust cycles: every growth spurt (2004–07, 2016–18, 2021–22) ended in a balance-of-payments crisis because it was driven by consumption and imports.
- Average growth of about 3% over the last decade barely exceeds population growth of 2.55%.

### 4.2 Fiscal weakness

- **Tax-to-GDP about 10–11%**, among the lowest in the region. Salaried people and organised industry are over-taxed; agriculture, wholesale and retail trade, and real estate are under-taxed.
- Heavy reliance on indirect taxes, which are regressive.
- Interest payments are the largest item of spending.
- State-owned enterprises lose hundreds of billions of rupees a year (power distribution companies, railways, steel; a 75% stake in PIA was sold in December 2025 to a consortium led by the Arif Habib group for Rs 135 billion).
- The NFC leaves the federation with most of the debt and defence burden and the provinces with money but weak tax effort.

### 4.3 External weakness

- **Exports about $30–32 billion**, around 10% of GDP, concentrated in textiles and rice, and in a few markets (USA, EU, UK, China, UAE).
- Imports of energy, edible oil, machinery and raw materials rise with every growth spurt.
- Remittances fill the gap; they depend on the Gulf.
- External debt above $130 billion; repayments of over $20 billion a year, met by rollovers from China, Saudi Arabia and the UAE.
- Foreign direct investment is low (about $2 billion a year).

### 4.4 Low savings and investment

- Investment about 13–14% of GDP; national savings similar. Neighbours invest 30% or more.

### 4.5 Energy

- High tariffs, capacity payments, circular debt (about Rs 1.7–1.8 trillion in power at mid-2026). See file 14.

### 4.6 Human capital and population

- Population growth of 2.55%; 25 million children out of school; stunting in about 40% of children under five; low female labour participation (about a quarter).

### 4.7 Agriculture and industry

- Low productivity; water stress; cotton decline; premature de-industrialisation. See `03-Notes-History/13-Land-and-People.md`.

### 4.8 Governance and elite capture (2023 special)

- The UNDP's National Human Development Report 2021 estimated that privileges for elite groups — the corporate sector, large landowners, the political class, the military's businesses — cost about **$17.4 billion a year**, roughly 6% of GDP.
- Forms: tax exemptions, subsidised inputs, protected markets, preferential access to land and credit, amnesties.
- Effects: low revenue, misallocated capital, weak competition, and loss of public trust.
- Scholars to cite: Ishrat Husain (*Pakistan: The Economy of an Elitist State*); Akbar Zaidi (*Issues in Pakistan's Economy*); Hafiz Pasha (*Growth and Inequality in Pakistan*).

### 4.9 Political instability and security (2021)

- Policy reversals with each government; investors wait.
- Terrorism in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and Balochistan raises costs and threatens CPEC and mining.

### 4.10 Climate

- The 2022 floods cost about $30 billion; the 2025 floods about Rs 822 billion. A country in debt must rebuild repeatedly.

### 4.11 Aid, grants and loans (2022 question)

- Aid has followed strategic usefulness: 1950s–60s (pacts), 1980s (Afghan jihad), 2001–10 (war on terror). Each surge funded consumption and postponed reform.
- In the last decade grants shrank and commercial loans, CPEC debt, bonds and central-bank deposits from friendly states grew. External debt roughly doubled between 2013 and 2023.
- Borrowed money built power plants and roads, which helped growth, but repayments in dollars were not matched by exports.
- **Lesson:** loans help only when invested in capacity that earns or saves foreign exchange.

## 5. What has improved since 2023

- Default avoided; reserves rebuilt from under $4 billion (early 2023) to about $17 billion.
- Inflation down from 38% to single digits; interest rates cut from 22% to about half that.
- Primary surpluses; a current-account surplus in FY25.
- Credit-rating upgrades in 2025.
- Agricultural income tax laws passed by all provinces.
- IT exports and remittances at record levels.
- Investment pledges from the Gulf, China and the USA (minerals), handled through the SIFC.
- Government plan: **Uraan Pakistan** (December 2024), built on "5 Es" — exports, e-Pakistan, environment, energy and infrastructure, equity — aiming at a $1 trillion economy by 2035.

## 6. Way forward

1. **Broaden the tax base:** tax all income alike (agriculture, retail, property); digitise FBR; cut rates on the compliant.
2. **Export-led growth:** stable exchange rate policy, lower tariffs on inputs, new sectors (IT, engineering, pharmaceuticals, food, minerals), new markets.
3. **Fix energy:** renegotiate capacity contracts, privatise distribution, cut losses, shift to local fuels and renewables.
4. **Reform or sell state enterprises.**
5. **Rewrite the fiscal bargain** between centre and provinces through the NFC: share burdens (BISP, higher education, disaster costs) as well as revenue; reward provincial tax effort and population control.
6. **Invest in people:** schooling, skills, health, women's work.
7. **Raise savings and investment:** pension and capital-market reform; policy stability.
8. **End elite privileges:** publish and phase out tax expenditures and subsidies.
9. **A charter of economy** among parties, so that core policies survive elections.
10. **Climate resilience** built into every project.
11. **Regional trade** when politics permit.
