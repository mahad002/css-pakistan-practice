# 6. Non-Traditional Security Threats; the Role of Non-State Actors

Syllabus item VIII. Asked in 2016 (with the Afghan war), 2018 (non-state actors versus hostile states) and 2021 (fifth-generation warfare). Overlaps with files 08, 17 and 18.

## 1. Concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Traditional security** | Defence of territory and sovereignty against the armed forces of other states |
| **Non-traditional security (NTS)** | Threats to the survival and well-being of people and the state that are not mainly military and often cross borders: terrorism, climate change, water, food and energy shortage, disease, population pressure, economic crisis, cyber attack, organised crime, forced migration, disinformation |
| **Human security** | Security of the individual: "freedom from fear and freedom from want" (UNDP Human Development Report 1994, led by Dr Mahbub ul Haq) |
| **Securitisation** | The Copenhagen School (Barry Buzan, Ole Wæver): an issue becomes a security issue when leaders present it as an existential threat and the public accepts extraordinary measures |
| **Non-state actor (NSA)** | Any organised actor that is not a state: violent (militant, insurgent, criminal groups) or non-violent (NGOs, companies, media, religious networks, lenders) |
| **Hybrid / fifth-generation warfare** | Pursuit of strategic aims through a mix of proxies, economic pressure, cyber operations, lawfare and information campaigns, staying below the level of open war |

Pakistan's **National Security Policy (January 2022)** adopted a citizen-centred definition with economic security at its core.

## 2. Violent non-state actors in Pakistan

| Type | Groups | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Religious militant | **TTP** (formed December 2007; the state calls it *Fitna al-Khawarij*); Hafiz Gul Bahadur group; Lashkar-i-Islam; **Islamic State–Khorasan (IS-K)**; al-Qaeda remnants | Sanctuaries in Afghanistan since 2021; US weapons left behind in 2021 are in use |
| Sectarian | Lashkar-i-Jhangvi; Sipah-i-Sahaba offshoots; Zainabiyoun links | Kurram district saw heavy sectarian fighting in 2024–25 |
| Ethno-nationalist | **BLA** (including its Majeed Brigade), **BLF**, the BRAS alliance; Sindhi groups (SRA) | The state alleges Indian backing (*Fitna al-Hindustan*) |
| India-focused groups | Lashkar-i-Taiba, Jaish-i-Muhammad — banned in Pakistan | The source of international pressure (FATF) and of crises with India |
| Criminal | Smugglers, drug networks, kidnappers, river-belt (*kacha*) gangs, land mafias | Fund and overlap with militancy |

### Scale of the threat

- Militant violence fell sharply after 2014 and rose again from 2021.
- **2025** was the deadliest year in a decade: think tanks counted over 1,000 attacks and total violence-linked deaths (militants, security forces, civilians) in the range of 3,400 ([Dawn on PICSS](https://www.dawn.com/news/1963742); [Dawn on CRSS](https://www.dawn.com/news/1964537)).
- The **Global Terrorism Index 2026** ranked Pakistan the country most affected by terrorism in 2025, for the first time.
- Over 90% of violence is in **Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and Balochistan**.
- Landmark attacks: Army Public School (16 December 2014); Peshawar Police Lines mosque (30 January 2023); **Jaffar Express hijacking (11 March 2025)**; coordinated BLA attacks across Balochistan (**31 January 2026**).

### Roots (2016 question: is the Afghan war the cause?)

**Yes, in large part:** the 1980s jihad brought weapons, drugs, madrassa networks and trained fighters; the Taliban years gave them a rear base; the post-2001 war pushed militants into the tribal areas; the 2021 Taliban victory revived the TTP.

**But not only:** state use of proxies; Zia-era sectarian policies and the Saudi–Iranian rivalry; decades without rights or services in FATA and Balochistan; weak policing and courts; youth unemployment; unregulated seminaries; outside sponsorship.

### Are non-state actors a greater threat than hostile states? (2018)

| For "yes" | For "no" |
|---|---|
| They have killed over 80,000 Pakistanis since 2001; the state estimates economic losses above $150 billion | The May 2025 conflict showed that state-to-state war remains possible, and far more destructive |
| They strike daily, inside cities, against soft targets | Nuclear deterrence caps state threats; no such cap exists for the gravest state crisis going wrong |
| They erode the writ of the state, investment (CPEC, mining) and social cohesion | Many violent non-state actors are instruments of hostile states — the two threats are linked |
| They cannot be deterred by conventional or nuclear forces | |

Best answer: the threats are **intertwined**. Non-state actors are the main day-to-day threat to life and the economy; hostile states are the gravest potential threat and often work through them.

## 3. Non-violent non-traditional threats

| Threat | Pakistan's exposure |
|---|---|
| **Climate change** | Among the most affected countries: floods of 2010, 2022 (33 million affected, about $30 billion) and 2025; heatwaves; glacial melt; smog in Punjab |
| **Water scarcity** | Under 1,000 cubic metres per person; storage for 30 days; the Indus Waters Treaty in abeyance since April 2025 |
| **Food insecurity** | About two-fifths of young children stunted; crop losses in floods; dependence on imported edible oil and pulses |
| **Energy insecurity** | Imported fuels; circular debt; exposure to Gulf conflict |
| **Population** | 241.5 million, growing at 2.55% |
| **Health** | Polio still endemic (with Afghanistan); COVID-19; hepatitis; dengue; weak primary care |
| **Economic insecurity** | Debt, low reserves, inflation shocks; poverty at 28.9% |
| **Cyber and information** | Data breaches; attacks on banks and utilities; disinformation campaigns (the EU DisinfoLab's "Indian Chronicles", 2020, documented a long-running network of fake outlets targeting Pakistan); deepfakes |
| **Migration and refugees** | Decades of hosting Afghans; repatriation since October 2023; human smuggling to Europe |
| **Drugs and crime** | On the trafficking route from Afghanistan; several million drug users |
| **Extremism and polarisation** | Sectarian hate speech, mob violence over blasphemy allegations |

## 4. The state's response

| Instrument | Content |
|---|---|
| Military operations | Rah-i-Rast (Swat, 2009), Rah-i-Nijat (South Waziristan, 2009), **Zarb-i-Azb** (June 2014), **Radd-ul-Fasaad** (February 2017), **Azm-i-Istehkam** (approved June 2024), intelligence-based operations |
| **National Action Plan** (January 2015; 20 points; revised 2021) | Military courts, ban on militias, action on hate speech and terror financing, madrassa registration, FATA reform, Karachi and Balochistan measures, criminal-justice reform |
| Institutions | **NACTA** (Act of 2013); provincial Counter-Terrorism Departments; apex committees; a National Intelligence Fusion and Threat Assessment Centre (2025) |
| Policies | National Internal Security Policy (2014; 2018–23); National Security Policy (2022); a national policy on preventing violent extremism (2024) |
| Finance | Completed two FATF action plans; removed from the grey list in **October 2022** |
| Border | Fencing of the Afghan and Iranian borders; one-document (passport and visa) regime at crossings |
| Law | Anti-Terrorism Act 1997 (amended); PECA 2016 (amended 2025); National Cyber Security Policy 2021; the National Cyber Crime Investigation Agency |
| Climate and disasters | NDMA; National Climate Change Policy; National Adaptation Plan (2023); Living Indus; Recharge Pakistan; the IMF's RSF |
| Social | BISP; health cards; polio campaigns |

### Why results fall short

1. The kinetic parts of the Action Plan were done; the non-kinetic parts (courts, police, madrassas, hate speech, NACTA's role) lagged.
2. Sanctuaries across the Afghan border.
3. Civil–military and centre–province disagreement — most visibly over operations in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, which fed into governor's rule in October 2026.
4. Merged districts: promised funds and services after 2018 arrived slowly.
5. Balochistan treated mainly as a security problem.
6. Thin civilian capacity; reliance on the army.
7. NTS threats get speeches, not budgets: under 1% of GDP each for health and education.

## 5. Fifth-generation and hybrid warfare (2021)

**What it looks like:** online disinformation and fake outlets; funding of separatists and militants; economic coercion (FATF lobbying, trade measures); lawfare; cyber intrusion; exploiting ethnic, sectarian and political divisions.

**How to respond**

1. Close internal fault lines — the best defence, because hybrid war exploits real grievances.
2. Credible state communication: fast, factual, open to scrutiny. Censorship and shutdowns damage credibility.
3. Media literacy; independent fact-checking.
4. Cyber defences for critical infrastructure; data protection law.
5. Economic resilience: reserves, diversified trade, compliance with financial standards.
6. Diplomacy and legal action to expose hostile networks.
7. Avoid labelling domestic dissent as foreign warfare; that deepens alienation.

## 6. Way forward

- A whole-of-government approach led by civilians: police, prosecution and courts at the front; the army in support.
- Implement the full Action Plan, including the neglected points.
- Political settlement and development in Balochistan and the merged districts.
- A verifiable understanding with Kabul on the TTP.
- Fund human security: schooling, primary health, nutrition, family planning.
- Climate adaptation: early warning, flood-plain zoning, water storage, resilient crops.
- Regional cooperation on climate, water, disease and disasters through the SCO and, if revived, SAARC.
