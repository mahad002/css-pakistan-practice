# 18. Proxy Wars: The Role of External Elements

Syllabus item XXV. Asked in 2020 (define proxy war; external factors in internal security). A short topic. It overlaps with files 06, 16 and 17; this file gives the concept and organises the examples.

## 1. Concept

- **Proxy war:** a conflict in which an outside power pursues its aims by supporting a local actor — a state, a militia, an insurgent or a political group — with money, arms, training, sanctuary or propaganda, instead of fighting directly.
- **Why states use proxies:** lower cost and risk; deniability; nuclear deterrence makes direct war too dangerous; domestic opinion resists casualties.
- **Why they are dangerous:** proxies have their own aims and outlive their sponsors' purposes ("blowback"); they prolong wars; civilians pay.
- Related ideas: sub-conventional warfare, hybrid warfare, the "stability–instability paradox" (nuclear stability at the top encourages low-level conflict underneath).
- Cold War examples: Korea, Vietnam, Angola, Afghanistan. Recent ones: Syria, Yemen, Libya, Ukraine before 2022.

## 2. Why Pakistan is exposed

- **Location** at the junction of rival powers and regions.
- **Internal fault lines** — ethnic, sectarian, political, economic — which outsiders can exploit.
- **Weak governance** in border regions.
- **A history of being a base** for others' wars, and of using proxies itself.
- **Nuclear deterrence** in South Asia, which pushes rivalry below the conventional level.

## 3. Proxy conflicts on and around Pakistan's soil

| Arena | External actors | How it played out |
|---|---|---|
| **Afghan jihad, 1979–89** | USA, Saudi Arabia, China and others against the USSR | Pakistan was the conduit. Legacy: weapons, drugs, militant networks, refugees |
| **Afghan civil war, 1990s** | Pakistan and Saudi Arabia (Taliban) against Iran, Russia, India (Northern Alliance) | Regional powers fought through Afghan factions |
| **Saudi–Iranian rivalry** | Saudi Arabia and Gulf donors; Iran | From the 1980s funding flowed to rival sectarian seminaries and groups (Sipah-i-Sahaba and Lashkar-i-Jhangvi; Tehrik-i-Jafaria and Sipah-i-Muhammad). Thousands died in sectarian attacks. Recruitment of Pakistanis for the Syrian war (Zainabiyoun) is a later echo |
| **India–Pakistan** | Each accuses the other | **Pakistan's charge:** Indian support for the Mukti Bahini (1971), Baloch separatists and the TTP; the arrest of serving Indian naval officer **Kulbhushan Jadhav** in Balochistan (March 2016; the ICJ in 2019 ordered review of his sentence and consular access, without annulling the conviction); disinformation networks; targeted killings in Pakistan reported by foreign media in 2024. **India's charge:** Pakistani support for militancy in Punjab (1980s) and Kashmir (since 1989) and for groups behind attacks in India |
| **Afghanistan as a base, 2001–21 and after** | Pakistan accused Afghan and Indian intelligence of backing the TTP and Baloch groups; Kabul and Washington accused Pakistan of sheltering the Afghan Taliban | Mutual use of sanctuaries. Since 2021 the TTP operates from Afghan soil; in November 2026 Kabul accused Pakistan of supporting anti-Taliban groups |
| **Balochistan** | Alleged Indian support; sanctuaries in Afghanistan and Iran; diaspora networks in Europe; great-power interest in Gwadar | Attacks on CPEC, Chinese nationals and mining; the state now calls the Baloch militant groups *Fitna al-Hindustan* |
| **US–China competition** | — | Pressure and incentives around CPEC, ports, minerals, technology; information campaigns on both sides |
| **War on terror** | USA; al-Qaeda | Pakistan's territory used for others' war: drone strikes, CIA contractors, militant sanctuaries |
| **Middle East wars** | Iran, Israel, Gulf states, USA | Risk of sectarian spill-over and of Pakistan's soil or airspace being drawn in; Jaish al-Adl and the January 2024 exchange with Iran |

## 4. External factors in Pakistan's internal security (2020)

1. **Sanctuary across borders** for the TTP, IS-K and Baloch groups.
2. **Funding and arms** from hostile agencies and from private donors abroad.
3. **Leftover weapons** from the US withdrawal.
4. **Foreign funding of sectarian institutions.**
5. **Disinformation and psychological operations.**
6. **Economic coercion:** lobbying at the FATF; pressure through lenders.
7. **Transnational ideology and recruitment:** al-Qaeda, IS.
8. **Smuggling, drugs and money-laundering networks.**

**The honest caveat:** external hands work only where internal grievances exist. Blaming outsiders for everything hides domestic failures of governance and justice. Examiners reward this balance.

## 5. Effects on Pakistan

- Tens of thousands of lives; a militarised society.
- Sectarian and ethnic polarisation.
- Economic loss; threat to CPEC and mining investment.
- A security-centred state and strained civil liberties.
- International image: accusations of sponsoring militancy; the FATF grey list (2018–22).
- Strained relations with every neighbour except China.

## 6. Current position (to Nov 2026)

- **Western border:** open fighting with the Afghan Taliban in 2025–26, with each side accusing the other of using proxies.
- **Balochistan:** escalating BLA and BLF attacks (31 January 2026; spring 2026). The government blames India; analysts also stress local grievances ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/2/2/how-balochistan-attacks-threaten-pakistans-promises-to-china-trump)).
- **India:** after May 2025 both states accuse each other of terrorism at every forum, including the SCO summit at Bishkek (September 2026). Pakistan has proposed a joint or neutral mechanism to examine evidence.
- **Iran war:** Pakistan has so far kept sectarian spill-over contained and positioned itself as mediator.

## 7. Way forward

1. **Close the internal gaps:** political settlement in Balochistan, services and justice in the merged districts, sectarian harmony (the *Paigham-i-Pakistan* declaration of 2018 by ulema of all schools).
2. **No proxies of our own.** A clear policy that no group may use Pakistani soil against any country; act against all militant groups without distinction.
3. **Border control:** fencing, biometric crossings, joint mechanisms with Iran and (when possible) Afghanistan.
4. **Choke the money:** enforce terror-financing and charity regulation; scrutiny of foreign funding to seminaries and NGOs under transparent rules.
5. **Intelligence coordination** among civilian and military agencies through NACTA.
6. **Diplomacy and law:** present evidence at the UN and bilaterally; use the SCO's anti-terror structure; pursue cases like Jadhav's through the ICJ.
7. **Balanced foreign policy** so that Pakistan is not the battlefield of Saudi–Iranian or US–Chinese rivalry.
8. **Counter-narrative and media literacy** against hostile information operations.
9. **Regional agreements** on non-interference, starting with crisis mechanisms with India and Kabul.
