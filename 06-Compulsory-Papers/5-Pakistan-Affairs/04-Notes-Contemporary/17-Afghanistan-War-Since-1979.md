# 17. The War in Afghanistan since 1979: Impact on Pakistan and Challenges after 2014

Syllabus item XXIV. Asked in 2016 (the war and its effects), 2020 (the US–Taliban process), 2021 ("Afghan-owned, Afghan-led") and 2026 (Afghan instability and Pakistan's security). With open conflict in 2026 it will stay on the paper.

## 1. Four wars in one

| Phase | Dates | Summary |
|---|---|---|
| **Soviet war** | 1979–89 | Communist coup (**Saur Revolution**, April 1978); **Soviet invasion, 24–27 December 1979**; mujahideen resistance backed by the USA, Saudi Arabia, China and others through Pakistan; **Geneva Accords, 14 April 1988**; Soviet withdrawal completed **15 February 1989** |
| **Civil war and the Taliban** | 1989–2001 | Najibullah fell (April 1992); mujahideen factions fought for Kabul despite the Peshawar (1992) and Islamabad (1993) accords; the **Taliban** emerged from Kandahar (1994), took **Kabul (27 September 1996)**, recognised by Pakistan, Saudi Arabia and the UAE; hosted al-Qaeda |
| **US and NATO war** | 2001–21 | After 9/11, **Operation Enduring Freedom (7 October 2001)**; Bonn Agreement (December 2001); governments of Hamid Karzai and Ashraf Ghani; Taliban insurgency from 2003; troop surge (2009–11); NATO's combat mission ended **December 2014**; talks at Murree (2015); **Doha Agreement (29 February 2020)**; Taliban entered **Kabul on 15 August 2021**; last US troops left on 30 August |
| **Taliban rule and conflict with Pakistan** | 2021– | The Islamic Emirate, unrecognised by most of the world (Russia recognised it in July 2025); bans on girls' secondary and higher education; the TTP's revival; Pakistan–Afghanistan fighting in 2025–26 |

## 2. Pakistan's role in each phase

- **1979–89:** front-line state. General Zia rejected a first US offer as "peanuts", then accepted larger packages. The ISI distributed arms and trained fighters; seven mujahideen parties were based in Peshawar. Motives: fear of a Soviet push to the warm waters and of an Indian–Soviet–Afghan squeeze; Islamic solidarity; US aid and tolerance of the nuclear programme.
- **1990s:** search for a friendly government in Kabul ("strategic depth"); support for the Taliban.
- **2001–21:** ally of the USA while accused of sheltering the Afghan Taliban's leadership; facilitator of talks (the Murree round, the release of Mullah Baradar in 2018, the Doha process). Pakistan's line was that there was no military solution and that peace must be **"Afghan-owned and Afghan-led"**.
- **Since 2021:** first advocate for engagement with the Taliban; then, as attacks mounted, their adversary.

## 3. Impact on Pakistan

| Area | Impact |
|---|---|
| **Refugees** | Over 3 million in the 1980s — then the world's largest refugee population; about 600,000 more after 2021. Pressure on jobs, housing, forests and services in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Balochistan and Karachi. Since October 2023 Pakistan has sent back well over a million undocumented Afghans and, from 2025, holders of Afghan Citizen Cards and other documents |
| **Weapons** | The "Kalashnikov culture": leakage from the arms pipeline in the 1980s; US equipment left behind in 2021 now used by the TTP and Baloch groups |
| **Drugs** | The heroin trade; millions of addicts; trafficking routes through Balochistan |
| **Militancy** | Jihadi organisations and training camps; fighters later turned on Pakistan; the **TTP** (2007); sectarian groups armed and emboldened |
| **Sectarianism** | Saudi- and Iranian-backed networks; Shia–Sunni killings from the mid-1980s |
| **Terrorism at home** | More than 80,000 dead since 2001; attacks on schools, shrines, markets, mosques and military bases |
| **Economy** | Short-term aid in the 1980s and 2000s; long-term losses the government puts above $150 billion; lost investment and tourism; smuggling under the transit trade |
| **Politics** | Strengthened the military's hold on policy (1977–88, 1999–2008); religious parties gained influence; the tribal areas were destabilised |
| **Society** | Madrassa growth; intolerance; displacement of millions during operations in Swat and Waziristan |
| **Foreign relations** | Dependence on and friction with the USA; drone strikes and raids; tension with Kabul's governments, with Iran in the 1990s, and with Russia until the 2010s |
| **Border** | The Durand Line dispute sharpened; fencing since 2017; repeated closures of Torkham and Chaman |
| **Positive effects** | Strategic importance and aid in two periods; military experience; cover for the nuclear programme in the 1980s; cultural and trade ties with Afghans |

## 4. Challenges after 2014

1. **Security vacuum** after NATO's drawdown; IS-K appeared (2015).
2. **The TTP across the border** after Zarb-i-Azb (2014) pushed it into Afghanistan.
3. **Mistrust with Kabul** under Ghani; Indian influence in Afghanistan.
4. **Border management:** fencing, crossings, clashes.
5. **Refugee fatigue.**
6. **US blame** and aid suspension (2018).
7. **Peace process:** Pakistan's leverage over the Taliban was real but limited, as later events showed.

## 5. Since August 2021: from hope to conflict

### Why the expected "friendly Kabul" did not appear

- The Afghan Taliban and the TTP share ideology, history and battlefield ties; the Taliban will not fight their former comrades.
- The Taliban reject the Durand Line as firmly as any earlier government and resist the fence.
- They deny that the TTP operates from Afghan soil, or describe it as Pakistan's internal matter.
- Kabul-brokered talks between Pakistan and the TTP (2021–22) allowed fighters to return and regroup; the TTP ended its ceasefire in **November 2022**.
- Pakistan's own leverage shrank once the Taliban no longer needed sanctuary.

### Escalation

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| 2022–24 | Rising attacks in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and Balochistan; Pakistani air strikes in Khost, Kunar and Paktika (April 2022, March and December 2024) |
| October 2023 | Illegal Foreigners Repatriation Plan |
| May 2025 | Pakistan and Afghanistan agreed to exchange ambassadors after a China-hosted meeting |
| **October 2025** | Strikes in Kabul attributed to Pakistan; Taliban attacks on border posts (11 October); a week of heavy fighting; Pakistan closed the border; **ceasefire at Doha (19 October)** brokered by Qatar and Turkey; follow-up talks in Istanbul failed in November |
| **February 2026** | After further attacks and strikes, the Taliban attacked Pakistani posts (26 February); Pakistan bombed targets in **Kabul, Kandahar and Paktia (27 February)**; the defence minister spoke of "open war". The UN reported civilian deaths |
| March 2026 | Fighting continued, including strikes on major cities. A pause for **Eid al-Fitr** was arranged at the request of Saudi Arabia, Qatar and Turkey |
| **1–7 April 2026** | **Urumqi talks** hosted by China. Both agreed to avoid escalation and keep talking. Pakistan's demands: declare the TTP a terrorist organisation, dismantle its infrastructure, provide verifiable proof. The Taliban were reported willing to mention the TTP in a text but not to call it terrorist |
| May–September 2026 | The "Urumqi process" continued through China; Pakistan largely gave up the bilateral track |
| **Late October – November 2026** | Border clashes resumed (Angoor Adda, Torkham); a TTP ambush killed ten policemen (11 November); Kabul accused Pakistan of backing anti-Taliban groups (22 November); Pakistan demanded action on sanctuaries |

### Effects on Pakistan now (2026 question)

- **Internal security:** 2025 was the deadliest year in a decade; violence is concentrated in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and Balochistan. One think tank found attacks fell in late 2025 after the border was closed, which supports Pakistan's claim about cross-border movement.
- **Politics:** disagreement between the federal government and the PTI-led provincial government over military operations and talks with Kabul was one of the stated grounds for governor's rule in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (October 2026).
- **Economy:** closed crossings halt trade worth billions and hurt traders on both sides; the route to Central Asia is blocked.
- **Diplomacy:** dependence on China, Qatar, Turkey and Saudi Arabia as mediators; India has re-engaged Kabul (the Taliban foreign minister visited New Delhi in October 2025 and India upgraded its mission), which Pakistan sees as encirclement.
- **Humanitarian:** mass returns of Afghans into a country in crisis; criticism from the UN and rights groups.
- **Counter-terrorism:** a two-front problem with Balochistan; pressure on the army and police.

## 6. Analysis

1. **Blowback.** Policies of the 1980s and 1990s built the networks that now attack Pakistan. "Strategic depth" delivered strategic vulnerability.
2. **Military means have limits.** Air strikes punish but do not dismantle the TTP; they also rally Afghan opinion behind the Taliban and kill civilians.
3. **The Taliban are not a proxy.** They pursue their own interests, play Pakistan against India and others, and are now recognised by Russia and courted by China and the Gulf.
4. **Leverage is economic and diplomatic:** transit trade, recognition, the SCO, and the influence of China and Qatar.
5. **The problem is also internal.** The TTP recruits where governance, justice and jobs are missing. The merged districts' reforms stalled.
6. **"Afghan-owned, Afghan-led" was sound in principle** but never produced an inclusive settlement; the Taliban took all. Stability built on one group remains fragile.
7. **Regional approach.** Every neighbour — China, Iran, the Central Asian states, Russia — fears militancy from Afghanistan. That shared interest is Pakistan's best diplomatic asset.

## 7. Policy measures

**Towards Kabul**

1. A verifiable arrangement on the TTP: relocation away from the border, disarmament, monitoring by guarantors (China, Qatar, Turkey).
2. Keep channels open even during clashes; restore ambassadors' work.
3. Separate trade and humanitarian matters from the security dispute as far as possible; reopen crossings under agreed rules.
4. A regional compact through the SCO and the neighbours' format: recognition and aid in return for counter-terrorism and inclusive governance.
5. A realistic line on the border: joint management even without formal recognition.

**At home**

6. Intelligence-led, police-centred counter-terrorism; strengthen CTDs and prosecution; finish the National Action Plan.
7. Deliver the merger promises: courts, police, schools, the promised funds.
8. Political consensus on Khyber Pakhtunkhwa; restore its elected government; involve local elders.
9. Refugee policy that is lawful, phased and humane, with documentation for those who stay.
10. Border technology and a visa regime that keeps legal movement easy.
11. A public, parliamentary Afghan policy in place of ad hoc decisions.
