# 2. Nuclear Programme of Pakistan: Safety, Security and International Concerns

Syllabus item IV. **Not asked since 2016, so it is due.** The May 2025 conflict and the 2025 changes to the command structure give examiners fresh angles.

## 1. Background

### Why Pakistan went nuclear

1. **Security against India:** wars of 1948 and 1965; the loss of East Pakistan in 1971; India's larger conventional forces.
2. **India's nuclear test** of 18 May 1974 ("Smiling Buddha").
3. **Unreliable allies:** arms embargoes in 1965 and 1971.
4. **National standing** and technological self-reliance.

### Timeline

| Year | Event |
|---|---|
| 1956 | Pakistan Atomic Energy Commission (PAEC) set up for peaceful uses |
| 1965 | Bhutto, as foreign minister: if India builds the bomb, Pakistan will "eat grass or leaves, even go hungry" but get one of its own; research reactor PARR-1 at PINSTECH |
| 1972 | **Multan meeting (20 January):** Bhutto asked scientists to build a weapon. KANUPP power reactor opened at Karachi (Canadian supply). Munir Ahmad Khan headed PAEC |
| 1974 | India's test; Canada and others cut off nuclear cooperation |
| 1976 | **Dr A.Q. Khan** set up the uranium enrichment project at **Kahuta** (later Khan Research Laboratories). France withdrew from a reprocessing-plant deal (1978) under US pressure |
| 1979 | US aid cut under the Symington Amendment; restored after the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan |
| 1983 | First cold tests (PAEC) |
| 1985 | **Pressler Amendment:** US aid conditional on a yearly certificate that Pakistan had no bomb |
| 1990 | Certificate withheld; sanctions; F-16s paid for but not delivered |
| 1998 | India tested on 11 and 13 May. Pakistan tested at **Chagai on 28 May** (five devices) and **Kharan on 30 May**. 28 May is *Youm-i-Takbir*. Sanctions followed |
| 1999 | Lahore Declaration included nuclear confidence-building measures |
| 2000 | **National Command Authority (NCA)** established (announced February 2000); **Strategic Plans Division (SPD)** as its secretariat |
| 2001 | **Pakistan Nuclear Regulatory Authority (PNRA)** |
| 2004 | The **A.Q. Khan network** exposed (transfers to Iran, Libya, North Korea); Khan confessed on television and was pardoned; Export Control Act passed |
| 2008 | India got a waiver from the Nuclear Suppliers Group after its deal with the USA; Pakistan did not |
| 2010 | NCA Act |
| 2011–13 | **Nasr** short-range missile tested; doctrine described as **full-spectrum deterrence** |
| 2016 | Pakistan applied for NSG membership |
| 2017 | **Ababeel** (multiple warheads) and **Babur-3** (submarine-launched cruise missile) tested |
| 2024 | US sanctions (December) on the National Development Complex and firms over long-range missile work |
| 2025 | **May conflict with India**; **Army Rocket Force Command** announced (August) for conventional missiles; **27th Amendment** created the Chief of Defence Forces and a **Commander of the National Strategic Command** |

### Doctrine

- **Credible minimum deterrence**, India-specific.
- **Full-spectrum deterrence:** capability at strategic, operational and tactical levels to close the space India's "Cold Start" doctrine sought for limited war under the nuclear threshold.
- **No "no-first-use" pledge.** Pakistan keeps the option of first use against a conventional attack that threatens its existence. General Khalid Kidwai outlined four thresholds in 2002: loss of large territory, destruction of large parts of the forces, economic strangulation, and internal destabilisation.
- Pakistan proposes a **Strategic Restraint Regime** for South Asia: nuclear restraint, conventional balance, and dispute settlement. India has not accepted.

### Forces (open-source estimates)

- About **170 warheads** (SIPRI Yearbook 2026: unchanged from the year before; India about 180–190).
- Land: Shaheen-I, II and **III** (about 2,750 km), Ghauri, Ghaznavi, Abdali, **Nasr** (about 60–70 km), Ababeel, Babur cruise missile.
- Air: aircraft with the **Ra'ad** cruise missile.
- Sea: Babur-3 under development — the third leg of a triad.

### Civil nuclear power

- Reactors at **Chashma** (C-1 to C-4) and Karachi (**K-2 and K-3**, Chinese Hualong One, about 1,100 MW each). KANUPP-1 retired in 2021. **C-5** (about 1,200 MW) under construction. All are under IAEA safeguards.
- Nuclear supplies a significant share of electricity (about 3,500 MW installed).
- Also used in medicine (cancer hospitals run by PAEC), agriculture and industry.

## 2. Safety and security

"Safety" means preventing accidents. "Security" means preventing theft, sabotage or unauthorised use.

| Layer | Arrangement |
|---|---|
| **Command and control** | NCA chaired by the Prime Minister, with an Employment Control Committee and a Development Control Committee. SPD manages operations and security |
| **Custody** | Warheads reported to be stored separately from delivery systems in peacetime; two-person and three-person rules; permissive action links (codes) |
| **Personnel** | Personnel Reliability Programme: screening and monitoring of everyone with access |
| **Physical security** | A dedicated SPD security force of tens of thousands; layered site defence; counter-intelligence |
| **Regulation** | PNRA licenses and inspects civil facilities; a Centre of Excellence for Nuclear Security offers training recognised by the IAEA |
| **Export control** | Export Control Act 2004; Strategic Export Control Division; control lists aligned with the NSG, MTCR and Australia Group |
| **International** | Party to the Convention on Nuclear Safety, the Convention on Physical Protection of Nuclear Material (and its amendment), and the conventions on early notification and assistance; implements UNSC Resolution 1540; took part in the Nuclear Security Summits (2010–16) |
| **With India** | Agreement not to attack each other's nuclear installations (signed 1988, in force 1991; lists exchanged every 1 January); agreement on advance notice of ballistic missile tests (2005); hotlines |

The NTI Nuclear Security Index has recorded steady improvement in Pakistan's regulations for securing weapons-usable material.

## 3. International concerns and Pakistan's replies

| Concern | Pakistan's reply |
|---|---|
| **Proliferation:** the A.Q. Khan network | It was the act of individuals, shut down in 2004; export controls and oversight were rebuilt; no leak since |
| **Terrorists:** attacks on bases (GHQ 2009, Mehran 2011, Kamra 2012) show reach | None reached nuclear assets; security is layered and separate; two decades without an incident |
| **Insider threat and extremism** | Reliability screening; compartmentalisation |
| **Tactical weapons:** Nasr lowers the threshold and may require delegated control in war | Control stays centralised; the weapons exist to deter limited war, and so make war less likely |
| **Fastest-growing arsenal**; blocking the Fissile Material Cut-off Treaty (FMCT) talks | A cut-off that ignores existing stocks would freeze India's advantage; Pakistan wants a treaty that covers stocks |
| **Outside the NPT and CTBT** | The NPT would admit Pakistan only as a non-weapon state. Pakistan will sign the CTBT if India does and has observed a test moratorium since 1998 |
| **Long-range missiles:** US officials said in December 2024 that Pakistan was developing a capability to strike beyond South Asia | The programme is India-specific; the sanctions were discriminatory |
| **Political instability and escalation risk** | Command structures have functioned through every crisis and change of government |
| **Discrimination:** the 2008 NSG waiver for India | Pakistan seeks criteria-based NSG membership for non-NPT states |

## 4. Current position (to Nov 2026)

- **May 2025:** the first exchange of sustained missile, drone and air strikes between two nuclear states. Both stopped short of the nuclear level. India's leaders said afterwards that they would not accept "nuclear blackmail"; Pakistan said its deterrent held and forced the ceasefire. Analysts on both sides see a lower threshold for conventional strikes and a more dangerous next crisis ([Al Jazeera, May 2026](https://aljazeera.com/news/2026/5/10/two-wins-two-losses-what-india-pakistan-have-learned-a-year-after-war)).
- **Command changes:** the 27th Amendment (November 2025) abolished the Chairman Joint Chiefs post and created a Commander of the National Strategic Command, appointed on the recommendation of the Chief of Defence Forces. How this fits with the NCA chaired by the Prime Minister is a live question **[verify the detail of the implementing law]**.
- **Arsenal:** SIPRI's 2026 Yearbook puts Pakistan at about 170 warheads, with continued work on delivery systems and fissile material.
- **Saudi defence agreement (September 2025):** raised questions abroad about "extended deterrence". Pakistan says its nuclear doctrine is unchanged and India-specific.
- **Iran:** attacks on Iran's nuclear sites in 2025 and 2026 sharpened Pakistani concern about the precedent of striking safeguarded facilities.
- **Arms control:** no India–Pakistan dialogue; the yearly exchange of lists of nuclear installations continued in January 2026 **[verify]**.

## 5. Analysis

1. **Deterrence has prevented all-out war** since 1998 (Kargil, 2001–02, 2008, 2019, 2025 all stayed limited). It has not prevented crises; each one has climbed higher.
2. **The stability–instability paradox:** safety at the nuclear level may tempt both sides to risk more below it — sub-conventional attacks on one side, "punitive" strikes on the other.
3. **New technologies** (drones, hypersonic and cruise missiles, missile defence, cyber) shorten decision time and blur conventional and nuclear systems.
4. **Security has improved; perception lags.** The real risks are escalation and miscalculation more than theft.
5. **Cost:** the programme is cheap relative to conventional forces, but economic weakness is itself a security risk.
6. **Civil nuclear power** is a strong card: clean baseload electricity under full safeguards.

## 6. Way forward

1. Keep deterrence credible but minimum; avoid an open-ended arms race.
2. Restore crisis-management links with India: hotlines, missile-test and exercise notifications, an agreement on preventing incidents at sea and in the air, and talks on new technologies.
3. Keep political control visible: regular NCA meetings; clarity on the new command posts.
4. Keep improving security and say so: transparency where it does not harm secrecy.
5. Press for criteria-based access to the NSG and civil nuclear trade; expand safeguarded nuclear power.
6. Engage on the FMCT on terms that address existing stocks.
7. Strengthen the economy, which underwrites everything else.
