# 10. Constitutional History: 1949 to 2025

The Objectives Resolution, the constitutions of 1956, 1962 and 1973, and every amendment up to the 27th. Checked to 23 Nov 2026: **27 is the latest amendment passed.** A 28th (new provinces and federal restructuring) was being discussed but had not been tabled.

Asked: centre–province relations (2018, 2019), 18th Amendment (2022, 2023 special, 2024, 2026), judiciary and constitutional development (2017), 27th Amendment (2026). Read with `04-Notes-Contemporary/19-Constitutional-and-Legal-Debates.md` and `21-Federalism-18th-Amendment-NFC.md`.

---

## 1. Interim arrangements, 1947–1956

- **Government of India Act 1935**, adapted, plus the **Indian Independence Act 1947**.
- The Governor-General kept wide powers; Section 92A (added 1948) let the centre take over a province.
- The Constituent Assembly was both constitution-maker and federal legislature.

## 2. Objectives Resolution, 12 March 1949

Moved by Liaquat Ali Khan on 7 March; passed 12 March.

**Main principles**

1. Sovereignty over the universe belongs to Allah alone; the authority He has delegated to the state of Pakistan is to be exercised through its people as a sacred trust.
2. The state shall exercise its powers through the **chosen representatives of the people**.
3. Democracy, freedom, equality, tolerance and social justice, **as enunciated by Islam**, shall be fully observed.
4. Muslims shall be enabled to order their lives in accordance with the Quran and Sunnah.
5. **Minorities** shall be free to profess and practise their religions and develop their cultures.
6. A **federation** with autonomous units.
7. Fundamental rights guaranteed.
8. **Independence of the judiciary** fully secured.

**Debate**

- Non-Muslim members (S.C. Chattopadhyaya, B.K. Datta and others) objected that it mixed religion with politics and departed from Jinnah's 11 August 1947 speech. All their amendments were defeated.
- Supporters (Liaquat, Shabbir Ahmad Usmani, Zafarullah Khan) replied that it ruled out theocracy, since authority lay with the people's representatives.

**Later status:** preamble to the 1956, 1962 and 1973 constitutions. In **1985** it became a substantive part of the constitution as **Article 2A**. In that text the word "freely" was dropped from the minorities clause; the **18th Amendment (2010) restored it**.

## 3. The Constitution of 1956

In force **23 March 1956**. Abrogated **7 October 1958**.

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Length | 234 articles, 13 parts, 6 schedules |
| Name of state | Islamic Republic of Pakistan |
| System | Federal, **parliamentary** |
| Legislature | **One house:** National Assembly of 300, half from each wing (**parity**), plus 10 women's seats |
| President | Head of state; must be a Muslim, at least 40; elected by the national and provincial assemblies |
| Prime Minister | Head of government, responsible to the Assembly |
| Federation | Two provinces (East and West Pakistan); federal, provincial and concurrent lists; residuary powers with the provinces |
| Languages | Urdu and Bengali |
| Islamic provisions | No law repugnant to Islam; an organisation for Islamic research; directive principles (riba, zakat, alcohol) |
| Rights and courts | Enforceable fundamental rights; independent judiciary; Supreme Court |
| Electorate | Left to Parliament (joint electorates were later adopted) |

**Why it failed:** no election was held under it; the President interfered with cabinets; parties were weak and shifting; parity and One Unit were resented in both wings.

---

## 4. The Constitution of 1962

Ayub Khan set up a Constitution Commission under Justice **Shahabuddin** (1960–61). It recommended a presidential system with direct elections and safeguards. Ayub kept the presidential form and dropped most of the safeguards. Announced 1 March 1962; in force **8 June 1962**. Abrogated **25 March 1969**.

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Length | 250 articles, 12 parts, 3 schedules |
| System | **Presidential.** No prime minister, no vice-president |
| President | Muslim, at least 35; five-year term; chose ministers who were not answerable to the Assembly; could issue ordinances, declare emergency, veto bills, dissolve the Assembly (and then face re-election himself) |
| Election | **Indirect.** The President and assemblies were elected by an electoral college of **80,000 Basic Democrats** (40,000 from each wing; raised later to 120,000) |
| Legislature | One house: 156 members (75 from each wing plus 6 women); later enlarged |
| Federation | In name. Governors were the President's appointees; the centre could legislate on any matter in the "national interest" |
| Name | "Republic of Pakistan" at first; "Islamic" restored by the First Amendment (1963) |
| Rights | Not enforceable at first; made so by the First Amendment (1963) |
| Islamic bodies | Advisory Council of Islamic Ideology; Islamic Research Institute |
| Other | Islamabad the capital, Dhaka the seat of the legislature; Urdu and Bengali; Supreme Judicial Council for removing judges |

**Assessment:** it gave stability and continuity of policy for a few years. It concentrated power in one man, shut out the urban middle class and East Pakistan, and collapsed with its author in 1969.

---

## 5. 1969–1973

- **25 March 1969:** Yahya Khan abrogated the 1962 Constitution.
- **Legal Framework Order, 30 March 1970:** one person one vote; National Assembly of 313 (300 general + 13 women; East Pakistan 169 including 7 women); One Unit dissolved (1 July 1970); the Assembly to frame a constitution in 120 days.
- After the war of 1971: ***Asma Jilani v. Government of Punjab* (1972)** declared Yahya a usurper and overruled *Dosso*. Bhutto lifted martial law on 21 April 1972 under an **Interim Constitution**.

---

## 6. The Constitution of 1973

A committee of all parties in the Assembly (chaired first by Mahmud Ali Kasuri, then **Abdul Hafeez Pirzada**) drafted it after the **constitutional accord of 20 October 1972**. Passed **10 April 1973** without a dissenting vote, authenticated 12 April, in force **14 August 1973**. Its consensus is its great strength: every party in the Assembly, including the opposition, signed.

### Salient features

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Length | 280 articles, 12 parts; originally 6 schedules (5 remain) |
| System | **Federal, parliamentary.** Prime Minister is chief executive; President is head of state and acts on advice (Art. 48) |
| Legislature | **Two houses.** National Assembly by population; **Senate** with equal seats for the provinces |
| Present strength | National Assembly **336** (266 general, 60 women, 10 non-Muslims). Senate **96** (reduced from 104 after the FATA merger) |
| Islam | State religion (Art. 2); President and Prime Minister must be Muslims; Council of Islamic Ideology (Arts. 228–230); all laws to conform to Islam (Art. 227) |
| Rights | Fundamental rights (Arts. 8–28); Principles of Policy (Arts. 29–40) |
| Federal bodies | **Council of Common Interests** (Arts. 153–154); **National Economic Council** (Art. 156); **National Finance Commission** (Art. 160) |
| Judiciary | Supreme Court, high courts; Federal Shariat Court added 1980; Federal Constitutional Court added 2025 |
| Amendment | Two-thirds of the total membership of each house (Art. 239) |
| High treason | Art. 6: abrogating or subverting the constitution by force |
| Language | Urdu the national language (Art. 251) |
| Emergency | Arts. 232–237; governor's rule under Art. 234 |

### Suspensions

- **5 July 1977 – 30 December 1985:** held "in abeyance" by General Zia; revived through the Revival of the Constitution Order (March 1985) and the 8th Amendment.
- **12 October 1999 – 2002/03:** held in abeyance by General Musharraf; revived under the Legal Framework Order and the 17th Amendment.
- **3 November – 15 December 2007:** emergency and a Provisional Constitution Order; declared unconstitutional by the Supreme Court on 31 July 2009.

### 8th Amendment (November 1985)

- **Art. 58(2)(b):** the President could dissolve the National Assembly at his discretion if he judged that government could not be carried on under the constitution. Governors got the same power in the provinces.
- The President gained discretion to appoint service chiefs, governors and the Chief Election Commissioner.
- **Art. 270A** validated all martial law orders and acts since 5 July 1977, including the Revival of the Constitution Order, which had made the Objectives Resolution Art. 2A and inserted the Federal Shariat Court provisions.
- Senate enlarged to 87.
- **Effect:** a semi-presidential system. Four governments were dismissed under 58(2)(b): Junejo (1988), Benazir Bhutto (1990), Nawaz Sharif (1993), Benazir Bhutto (1996).

### 13th and 14th Amendments (1997)

Passed by Nawaz Sharif's two-thirds majority. The 13th restored parliamentary supremacy over the President. The 14th tied members to their party leaders. Critics said the two together removed every check on the Prime Minister.

### 17th Amendment (December 2003)

A deal between General Musharraf's government and the religious alliance MMA.

- Validated the **Legal Framework Order 2002** and acts since October 1999.
- **Revived Art. 58(2)(b)**, with a condition: a dissolution must be referred to the Supreme Court within 15 days.
- Musharraf was to give up his army post by 31 December 2004 (he did not until November 2007).
- Seats for women (60 in the National Assembly) and the enlarged houses introduced by the LFO were kept.

### 18th Amendment (April 2010)

Drafted by an all-party committee under Senator **Raza Rabbani**; passed unanimously; signed 19 April 2010. It changed about 100 articles.

| Area | Change |
|---|---|
| Parliamentary system | **Art. 58(2)(b) removed.** President acts on the Prime Minister's advice; appoints service chiefs and governors on advice. Limit on prime-ministerial terms removed |
| Past military rule | 17th Amendment and LFO repealed; Zia's name removed from the text; **Art. 6** widened: courts cannot validate treason |
| **Provincial autonomy** | **Concurrent Legislative List abolished** (47 subjects); about 17 federal ministries devolved, including education, health, labour, environment, culture, social welfare, local government |
| CCI | Chaired by the Prime Minister; must meet every 90 days; permanent secretariat; more subjects in Federal List Part II |
| NFC | **Art. 160(3A):** a province's share cannot be less than in the previous award |
| Natural resources | **Art. 172(3):** oil and gas owned jointly and equally by the federation and the province. Art. 161: net hydel profits and gas royalties to provinces |
| Provinces | NWFP renamed **Khyber Pakhtunkhwa**; spellings "Balochistan" and "Sindh" |
| New rights | **Art. 10A** fair trial; **Art. 19A** right to information; **Art. 25A** free and compulsory education for ages 5–16 |
| Judiciary | **Art. 175A:** Judicial Commission and Parliamentary Committee to appoint judges; Islamabad High Court created |
| Elections | Chief Election Commissioner appointed by consensus between government and opposition |
| Parties | Art. 63A: the party head, not the parliamentary leader, declares defection; intra-party elections no longer required by the constitution |
| Local government | **Art. 140A:** provinces must set up elected local governments and devolve power to them |

### 25th Amendment (May 2018)

- **Merged FATA with Khyber Pakhtunkhwa** and the provincially administered tribal areas with their provinces. Art. 247 omitted; the Frontier Crimes Regulation ended.
- National Assembly reduced from 342 to **336**; Senate from 104 to **96**; Khyber Pakhtunkhwa Assembly enlarged to 145.

### 26th Amendment (21 October 2024)

Passed in a late-night session with the help of JUI-F and a few opposition defectors; PTI opposed.

| Area | Change |
|---|---|
| Chief Justice of Pakistan | Chosen by a **Special Parliamentary Committee** (12 members: 8 MNAs, 4 senators) from the **three most senior** judges, not automatically the most senior. **Fixed three-year term** |
| Constitutional benches | Created in the Supreme Court (Art. 191A) and allowed in high courts (Art. 202A); judges nominated by the Judicial Commission. Constitutional cases go only to them |
| Judicial Commission | Reconstituted with members of Parliament (13 members); it also evaluates high court judges' performance |
| Suo motu | The Supreme Court may not act on its own beyond what a petition asks; Art. 184(3) cases go to the constitutional bench |
| New right | **Art. 9A:** right to a clean, healthy and sustainable environment |
| Riba | To be eliminated as far as practicable by **1 January 2028** |

First result: Justice **Yahya Afridi**, third in seniority, became Chief Justice on 26 October 2024. Supporters called it a restoration of Parliament's role; critics (bar associations, the International Commission of Jurists) called it a blow to judicial independence.

### 27th Amendment (13 November 2025)

Cabinet approval and Senate introduction 8 November; Senate vote 10 November; National Assembly 12 November (234 for, 4 against, opposition boycott); Senate again and presidential assent **13 November 2025**.

| Area | Change |
|---|---|
| **Federal Constitutional Court (FCC)** | A new court for constitutional interpretation, fundamental-rights cases and disputes between governments. Its decisions bind all courts including the Supreme Court. Judges serve to **68**; its chief justice has a three-year term. Equal provincial representation. First chief justice: **Justice Aminuddin Khan**, sworn in 14 November 2025 |
| Supreme Court | Becomes the top court for civil and criminal appeals; loses constitutional and suo motu jurisdiction |
| Transfer of judges | High court judges can be transferred between provinces on the Judicial Commission's recommendation without their consent; refusal means retirement |
| **Art. 243: Chief of Defence Forces (CDF)** | The Chief of the Army Staff is also CDF. The post of Chairman Joint Chiefs of Staff Committee was abolished from 27 November 2025. A **Commander of the National Strategic Command** is appointed on the CDF's recommendation |
| Five-star ranks | Field Marshal, Marshal of the Air Force and Admiral of the Fleet keep rank, uniform and privileges **for life**, with constitutional protection and removal only by a process like impeachment |
| Immunity | Lifetime immunity from criminal proceedings for the President (with an exception if he later holds public office) and for five-star officers |
| Provinces | Provincial cabinet size limit raised for smaller assemblies (from 11% to 13%). Early drafts touching the NFC, education and population were dropped or deferred after PPP objections [verify details] |

**What followed:** Supreme Court Justices **Mansoor Ali Shah** and **Athar Minallah** resigned on 13 November 2025; a Lahore High Court judge followed. Field Marshal **Asim Munir** was notified as the first CDF on 5 December 2025 for five years. The paper of February 2026 asked whether the amendment strengthens or weakens constitutionalism.

**Two views**

- *For:* a specialised court will clear the Supreme Court's backlog (over 50,000 cases); many democracies have constitutional courts; the Charter of Democracy (2006) promised one; unified command suits modern war, as May 2025 showed.
- *Against:* judges of the new court were first appointed by the executive; the old court's independence was the target; passed in days without debate; lifelong immunity conflicts with equality before law (Art. 25); it tilts the civil–military balance further (Chatham House, ICJ, bar groups).

Sources: [Dawn on the FCC chief justice](https://www.dawn.com/news/1955004); [Geo: salient features](https://www.geo.tv/latest/632690-what-the-27th-amendment-is-all-about-salient-features-explained); [Dawn on the CDF notification](https://www.dawn.com/news/1959310); [Chatham House](https://www.chathamhouse.org/2025/12/pakistans-27th-constitutional-amendment-moves-it-one-step-closer-authoritarian-rule).

### A possible 28th Amendment (status at 23 Nov 2026)

Ministers have spoken since mid-2026 of a package that could create **new provinces** and restructure the federation. No bill has been tabled. The PPP opposes any division of Sindh. See `04-Notes-Contemporary/21-Federalism-18th-Amendment-NFC.md`.

## 8. Three constitutions compared

| | 1956 | 1962 | 1973 |
|---|---|---|---|
| System | Parliamentary | Presidential | Parliamentary |
| Houses | One | One | Two |
| Election | Direct (never held) | Indirect, Basic Democrats | Direct adult franchise |
| Federation | Two provinces, parity | Two provinces, strong centre | Four provinces, Senate, CCI, NFC |
| Head of state | President, limited | President, dominant | President, ceremonial (except 1985–97 and 2003–10) |
| Made by | Second Constituent Assembly | One man's commission and cabinet | Elected Assembly, by consensus |
| Life | 2.5 years | 7 years | Since 1973, with interruptions |

## 9. Pattern to draw out in answers

1. Power has swung between **President and Prime Minister** (8th → 13th → 17th → 18th).
2. Power has swung between **centre and provinces** (One Unit → 1973 → 18th Amendment → current pressure to recentralise).
3. Since 2024 the swing is between **Parliament/executive and the judiciary** (26th, 27th).
4. Amendments made by consensus (1973 text, 18th) have lasted. Those made in haste or under pressure (8th, 17th) were later undone.
