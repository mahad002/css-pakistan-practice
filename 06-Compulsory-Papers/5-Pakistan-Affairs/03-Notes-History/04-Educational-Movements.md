# 4. Educational Movements: Aligarh, Deoband, Nadwa and the Regional Institutions

Syllabus item I names Aligarh, Deoband, Nadwah, Sindh Madrassah and Islamia College Peshawar. Aligarh was asked in 2016. A question on Aligarh, or a comparison of Aligarh and Deoband, is overdue.

Background first: read `05-1857-and-Aftermath.md` section 4 for the condition of Muslims after 1857. In short, Muslims were blamed for the rising, shut out of jobs, and hostile to English education. Two answers emerged: **adjust and modernise** (Aligarh), or **withdraw and preserve** (Deoband). Nadwa tried to bridge them.

---

## A. Sir Syed Ahmad Khan and the Aligarh Movement

### Life in brief

| Year | Event |
|---|---|
| 1817 | Born in Delhi (17 October) to a family connected with the Mughal court |
| 1838 | Joined the East India Company's judicial service |
| 1847 | *Asar-us-Sanadid*, on the monuments of Delhi |
| 1857 | Sub-judge at Bijnor; saved British lives during the rising; refused a reward of an estate |
| 1859 | *Risala Asbab-i-Baghawat-i-Hind* (Causes of the Indian Revolt); school at Moradabad |
| 1860–61 | *The Loyal Muhammadans of India* |
| 1863–64 | **Scientific Society** founded at Ghazipur (moved to Aligarh 1864); school at Ghazipur |
| 1866 | *Aligarh Institute Gazette* (Urdu and English) |
| 1867 | Urdu–Hindi controversy at Benares |
| 1869–70 | Visit to England; studied Oxford and Cambridge; wrote *Khutbat-i-Ahmadiya* in reply to William Muir's *Life of Mahomet* |
| 1870 | Journal ***Tahzib-ul-Akhlaq*** begun |
| 1875 | **MAO School** opened at Aligarh (24 May) |
| 1877 | Foundation of the **Muhammadan Anglo-Oriental College** laid by Viceroy Lord Lytton (8 January) |
| 1878 | Member, Viceroy's Legislative Council |
| 1886 | **Muhammadan Educational Conference** founded |
| 1887–88 | Speeches at Lucknow and Meerut against Muslim participation in Congress; United Indian Patriotic Association (1888) |
| 1893 | Muhammadan Anglo-Oriental Defence Association |
| 1898 | Died at Aligarh (27 March) |
| 1920 | MAO College became **Aligarh Muslim University** |

### Three aims of the movement

1. **Reconcile the British and the Muslims.**
2. **Persuade Muslims to take modern (Western) education.**
3. **Keep Muslims out of agitational politics until they were strong enough.**

### Work under each aim

**1. Reconciliation**

- *Asbab-i-Baghawat-i-Hind* argued that the rising was not a Muslim plot. Its causes were British: no Indian voice in the legislative council, laws and missionary activity that Indians saw as attacks on their religion, mismanagement of the army, and ignorance of Indian conditions.
- *Loyal Muhammadans of India* recorded Muslims who had helped the British.
- *Tabyin-ul-Kalam*, a commentary on the Bible, showed common ground between Islam and Christianity.
- He argued that Muslims could eat with Christians (*Ahkam-i-Ta'am-i-Ahl-i-Kitab*).

**2. Education**

- The Scientific Society translated English works on science and history into Urdu.
- MAO College combined Western learning with Islamic instruction, on the residential model of Oxford and Cambridge. Its English principals were Henry Siddons (the first), **Theodore Beck** (1883–99), Theodore Morison and W.A.J. Archbold.
- The Muhammadan Educational Conference met each year in a different city and spread the message. The Muslim League was founded at its 1906 session in Dhaka.
- *Tahzib-ul-Akhlaq* attacked social evils and urged rational thinking.

**3. Religious thought**

- He held that the "Word of God" (the Quran) and the "Work of God" (nature) cannot conflict. His Quranic commentary took a rationalist line. Orthodox ulema called him a *nechari* (naturalist) and issued fatwas against him.
- To calm fears, he left religious teaching at the college to orthodox scholars.

**4. Politics**

- **Urdu–Hindi controversy, 1867.** Hindu leaders at Benares demanded that Hindi in Devanagari replace Urdu in courts. Sir Syed told the commissioner, Mr Shakespeare, that he was now convinced the two communities could not work together as one nation. Altaf Husain Hali records this in *Hayat-i-Javed*. It is treated as the first modern statement of Muslim separateness.
- **Opposition to Congress (founded 1885).** Western-style elections in a country of unequal communities would mean rule by the majority. Competitive examinations would favour the better-educated Hindus. Muslims, he said, should stay out.
- **Demand for separate representation.** In the Viceroy's Council (1883) he argued that simple elections were unsuited to India and that communities needed their own representatives.

### Colleagues ("the Aligarh school")

Nawab Mohsin-ul-Mulk and Nawab Viqar-ul-Mulk (later League founders); Altaf Husain Hali (*Musaddas*, 1879); Shibli Nomani (historian, later of Nadwa); Deputy Nazir Ahmad (novelist); Maulvi Chiragh Ali; Maulvi Zakaullah; Syed Mahmud (his son, a judge).

### Impact of Aligarh

| Field | Effect |
|---|---|
| Educational | A generation of English-educated Muslims for government service and the professions; a model copied in Karachi, Lahore and Peshawar |
| Political | Aligarh graduates and trustees led the Simla Deputation and founded the Muslim League in 1906. Later leaders included Maulana Muhammad Ali Jauhar, Shaukat Ali, Liaquat Ali Khan, Khawaja Nazimuddin and Sardar Abdur Rab Nishtar. Jinnah called Aligarh the arsenal of Muslim India |
| Ideological | The idea of Muslims as a separate political community with its own interests |
| Literary | Simple modern Urdu prose; Hali, Shibli, Nazir Ahmad |
| Social | Reduced hostility to the West; encouraged reform of customs |
| Economic | Opened jobs and a Muslim middle class |

### Criticisms

- Too loyal to the British.
- Served the upper and middle classes (the *ashraf*) of north India; did little for the poor.
- **Little interest in women's education**; Sir Syed thought men should be educated first.
- His theology divided Muslims.
- By keeping Muslims out of politics for two decades, he delayed their political training (the counter-view: he gave them time to recover).

### Was Aligarh only educational? (2016 question)

No. Its means were educational. Its effects were political: it built the class that led Muslim politics, it stated the idea of two communities with separate interests, and it won the principle of separate representation. Education was the method; the survival of the Muslim community as a political force was the aim.

## B. Dar-ul-Uloom Deoband

### Founding

- Founded on **30 May 1866** at Deoband (Saharanpur district, UP) by **Maulana Muhammad Qasim Nanautavi** and **Maulana Rashid Ahmad Gangohi**. Both were disciples of Haji Imdadullah and had taken part in the 1857 fighting at Shamli.
- First teacher: Mulla Mahmud. First student: **Mahmud-ul-Hasan** (later *Shaikh-ul-Hind*).

### Aims and features

- Preserve Islamic learning and the Hanafi tradition in the line of Shah Waliullah.
- Syllabus based on the *Dars-i-Nizami*: Quran, hadith, fiqh; little modern science or English.
- **Independent of government.** Run on public donations, with a fixed syllabus, examinations and a hostel — new features for an Indian madrassa.
- Reform of custom: against shrine practices and innovations.

### Political role

- Anti-British from the start, in contrast to Aligarh.
- **Silk Letter Movement (Reshmi Rumal Tehrik), about 1913–16:** Mahmud-ul-Hasan and **Ubaidullah Sindhi** planned a rising with Afghan and Turkish help. The letters were intercepted; Mahmud-ul-Hasan was interned in Malta (1917–20).
- **Jamiat Ulema-i-Hind (1919)** grew from Deoband and worked with Congress. **Maulana Husain Ahmad Madani** argued for composite nationalism.
- A minority supported the League: **Maulana Ashraf Ali Thanvi**, **Maulana Shabbir Ahmad Usmani** (founded **Jamiat Ulema-i-Islam**, 1945), **Mufti Muhammad Shafi**, **Zafar Ahmad Usmani**. Usmani raised Pakistan's flag in Karachi on 14 August 1947 and backed the Objectives Resolution.

### Aligarh and Deoband compared

| | Aligarh | Deoband |
|---|---|---|
| Founded | 1875 (school), 1877 (college) | 1866 |
| Aim | Modern education; jobs; political survival | Religious learning; faith and culture |
| Attitude to the British | Cooperation | Opposition |
| Syllabus | English, sciences, with religious instruction | Traditional religious sciences |
| Funding | Donations plus government grants | Public donations only |
| Politics | Separate Muslim politics → Muslim League | Majority with Congress; a minority with the League |
| Product | Officials, lawyers, politicians | Ulema, imams, teachers |

Relations were cool but not hostile. Mahmud-ul-Hasan tried to bring the two closer; Jamia Millia Islamia (1920) was partly a result of that effort.

## C. Nadwat-ul-Ulama, Lucknow

- The council of ulema called **Nadwat-ul-Ulama** was formed in **1894 at Kanpur**. Its moving spirit was **Maulana Muhammad Ali Mungeri**; Maulvi Abdul Ghafoor is also named among the founders.
- The **Dar-ul-Uloom Nadwa** opened at **Lucknow in 1898**.
- **Aim:** a middle path. Reform the old syllabus, add English and some modern subjects, improve Arabic, and reduce quarrels among ulema of different schools.
- **Maulana Shibli Nomani** joined in 1904–05 after leaving Aligarh and shaped its syllabus and its journal *Al-Nadwa*. He left in 1913 and founded **Dar-ul-Musannifin** (Shibli Academy) at Azamgarh in 1914.
- **Syed Sulaiman Nadvi** completed Shibli's *Sirat-un-Nabi* and later helped with the Objectives Resolution in Pakistan. **Abul Hasan Ali Nadvi** was its best-known later scholar.
- The British government gave land and a grant, which some ulema criticised.
- **Assessment:** it produced fine scholars and writers of Arabic and Urdu, but never matched the mass influence of Deoband or the political weight of Aligarh.

## D. Anjuman Himayat-i-Islam, Lahore

- Founded **1884** in Lahore (first meeting at a mosque inside Mochi Gate). First president: **Khalifa Hameed-ud-Din**.
- **Aims:** answer Christian missionaries and Arya Samaj preaching; educate Muslim boys and girls; care for orphans; publish Islamic literature.
- **Work:** primary schools for girls and boys; orphanages; **Islamia College, Railway Road, Lahore (1892)**; later Islamia College for Women and other colleges; Himayat-i-Islam Press.
- Its annual sessions were major events. **Iqbal** recited *Nala-i-Yateem* (1900) and other early poems there and later presided over the Anjuman. Hali, Nazir Ahmad and Sir Syed's associates spoke there.
- **Political role:** Islamia College students were the backbone of the Punjab Muslim Students Federation. They served as volunteers at the 1940 Lahore session and campaigned in the 1945–46 elections.

---

## E. Sindh Madrassat-ul-Islam, Karachi

- Founded **1 September 1885** by **Khan Bahadur Hasan Ali Effendi**, with encouragement from Syed Ameer Ali's Central National Muhammadan Association and on the Aligarh model.
- First modern Muslim school in Sindh. Sindh was then part of the Bombay Presidency and its Muslims were far behind in education.
- **Jinnah** studied there (from 1887) and left part of his estate to it in his will. He opened it as a **college in 1943**.
- Became a university in 2012.
- **Political role:** produced Sindh's Muslim leadership; supported the separation of Sindh from Bombay (achieved 1936). Sir Abdullah Haroon, Sir Ghulam Hussain Hidayatullah and others were linked to it.

---

## F. Islamia College, Peshawar

- Founded **1913** by **Sahibzada Abdul Qayyum Khan** with the support of the Chief Commissioner, **Sir George Roos-Keppel**.
- The foundation stone of the college mosque was laid by **Haji Sahib of Turangzai**, a respected anti-British religious leader — a sign of wide support.
- **Aim:** modern education with Islamic teaching for the Pathans, to replace feuding with learning.
- **Jinnah** visited in 1936, 1945 and 1948 and named it in his will, alongside Aligarh and Sindh Madrassah.
- **Political role:** its students campaigned for the League in the 1945–46 elections and in the **NWFP referendum of July 1947**. It became the base of the University of Peshawar (1950).

---

## G. Other institutions worth one line

- **Central National Muhammadan Association (1877)** — Syed Ameer Ali, Calcutta; political representation of Muslims.
- **Anjuman-i-Islam, Bombay (1874)** — Badruddin Tyabji.
- **Muhammadan Literary Society, Calcutta (1863)** — Nawab Abdul Latif.
- **Jamia Millia Islamia (1920)** — founded at Aligarh during the Khilafat/non-cooperation period; moved to Delhi.

## H. Overall assessment

- Together these institutions ended Muslim isolation after 1857.
- Aligarh, the Anjuman, Sindh Madrassah and Islamia College supplied the **leaders and workers** of the Pakistan Movement.
- Deoband and Nadwa preserved **religious learning**; a section of their ulema gave the movement religious legitimacy.
- The split between "modern" and "traditional" education that began here continues in Pakistan's school and madrassa systems. That link is useful in a question on education today.
