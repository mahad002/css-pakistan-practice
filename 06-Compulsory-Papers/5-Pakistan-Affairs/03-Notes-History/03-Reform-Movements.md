# 3. Reform Movements: Sirhindi, Shah Waliullah, Syed Ahmad Shaheed, the Faraizis

Syllabus item I names the first three. Asked: Shah Waliullah 2016, Sirhindi 2020 and 2024. A reformer question is the most common history question after ideology. Shah Waliullah and Syed Ahmad Shaheed are now the most "due".

A common thread to use in any answer: **each movement defended the separate identity of Indian Muslims at a moment when it was at risk.** Sirhindi defended it against absorption under Akbar. Shah Waliullah against political collapse. Syed Ahmad against Sikh rule and social decay. The Faraizis against landlord and colonial pressure in Bengal.

---

## A. Shaikh Ahmad Sirhindi (Mujaddid Alf Sani), 1564–1624

### Life

- Born 1564 at Sirhind (East Punjab). Claimed descent from Caliph Umar.
- Educated at Sirhind and Sialkot. Spent time at Akbar's capital, Agra, where he met Abul Fazl and Faizi.
- In 1599 he became a disciple of **Khwaja Baqi Billah** in Delhi and joined the **Naqshbandi** Sufi order.
- Jahangir imprisoned him in **Gwalior Fort** in 1619 for about a year. He was then released, honoured, and kept with the royal camp for a time.
- Died 1624 at Sirhind. The title *Mujaddid Alf Sani* means "renewer of the second millennium" of Islam.

### The setting: Akbar's religious policy

- Akbar followed **Sulh-i-Kul** (peace with all). He abolished the jizya (1564), married Rajput princesses, and held debates in the Ibadat Khana (from 1575).
- The **Mahzar of 1579** gave the emperor authority to decide between jurists.
- In 1582 he announced the **Din-i-Ilahi**, a court creed that mixed elements of several religions.
- Orthodox opinion held that under Akbar Islamic practices were mocked, mosques neglected, and the ulema divided between worldly courtiers and helpless critics. Popular Sufism had absorbed many local customs.

### What Sirhindi did

1. **Defended prophethood and the Shariat.** His early treatise *Isbat-un-Nubuwwat* argued that prophetic guidance is necessary and final. He taught that Sufism (tariqat) must stay within the Shariat; it is its servant, not its rival.
2. **Wahdat-ul-Shuhud against Wahdat-ul-Wujud.** Ibn Arabi's doctrine of "unity of being" had been read as saying that God and creation are one, which blurred the line between Islam and other faiths. Sirhindi answered with "unity of witness": the mystic's feeling of oneness is a subjective experience, not reality. The Creator and creation are distinct.
3. **Opposed bid'at** (innovation in religion) and customs taken from Hindu practice.
4. **Worked on the nobility.** He wrote letters to leading nobles of Akbar's and Jahangir's courts, such as Shaikh Farid Bukhari, Khan-i-Azam and Abdur Rahim Khan-i-Khanan, urging them to restore respect for Islam at court.
5. **Maktubat-i-Imam Rabbani.** His collected letters (more than 500, in three volumes) are his main work and spread his teaching across India, Central Asia and the Ottoman lands.
6. **Refused the prostration** (sajda-i-tazimi) before Jahangir, which led to his imprisonment. He preached to fellow prisoners.
7. **Built an organisation.** He sent trained disciples (khalifas) to many towns. The Naqshbandi-Mujaddidi order became the most influential reformist order in India.
8. **Asserted separate identity.** He insisted that Islam and kufr are distinct, called for the restoration of jizya and respect for Islamic symbols. Writers on the Pakistan Movement see here an early statement of what became the Two-Nation Theory.

### Impact

| Short term | Long term |
|---|---|
| Jahangir's later attitude was more respectful to orthodox practice | Shah Jahan and especially Aurangzeb moved state policy towards orthodoxy; Aurangzeb was linked to the Mujaddidi order through Sirhindi's son Khwaja Muhammad Masum |
| Checked the influence of Din-i-Ilahi, which did not survive Akbar | The Mujaddidi line shaped Shah Waliullah's circle and later reformers |
| Reformed Sufism from within | Gave Indian Islam a clear boundary; a source for later Muslim separatism |

### Critical views

- **Traditional view (I.H. Qureshi, S.M. Ikram, Aziz Ahmad):** he saved Indian Islam from being absorbed into its Hindu environment.
- **Revisionist view (Yohanan Friedmann, *Shaykh Ahmad Sirhindi*, 1971):** in his own time he was known mainly as a Sufi thinker. His political influence on Jahangir and the court has been overstated by later writers. Din-i-Ilahi had very few followers and would have faded anyway.
- **Criticism of tone:** his language about non-Muslims and Shias was harsh and is criticised as divisive.

A balanced line: his immediate political effect is debated, but his long-term effect on Muslim religious thought and self-definition is not.

## B. Shah Waliullah, 1703–1762

### Life

- Born 21 February 1703 in Delhi, four years before Aurangzeb's death. Real name Qutbuddin Ahmad.
- Son of **Shah Abdur Rahim**, founder of the **Madrassa Rahimiyya** and one of the compilers of *Fatawa-i-Alamgiri*.
- Began teaching at the madrassa at about 17, after his father died (1719).
- Went to the Hijaz for Hajj and study in 1730–32 (dates vary by a year in the sources). Studied hadith at Madina under Shaikh Abu Tahir al-Kurdi.
- Returned to Delhi in 1732 and spent thirty years teaching and writing. Died 1762.

### The setting

- Political: the empire was breaking up. Marathas, Sikhs, Jats and Rohillas were rising. Nadir Shah sacked Delhi in 1739.
- Religious: Shia–Sunni conflict, quarrels between jurists and Sufis, ignorance of the Quran among ordinary Muslims (few understood Arabic), blind following of old rulings.
- Social and economic: extravagance at weddings and funerals, heavy taxes on peasants and artisans, an idle nobility.

### His work

**1. Religious reform**

- **Translated the Quran into Persian** (*Fath-ur-Rahman*, completed about 1737–38). Persian was the language of educated Muslims. Many ulema opposed this at first. His sons later made Urdu translations: **Shah Rafiuddin** (literal) and **Shah Abdul Qadir** (idiomatic, *Muzih-ul-Quran*).
- Wrote *Al-Fauz-ul-Kabir* on the principles of Quranic interpretation.
- Promoted the study of **hadith**; wrote commentaries on the *Muwatta* of Imam Malik in Arabic (*Musawwa*) and Persian (*Musaffa*).
- Argued for **ijtihad** and against blind taqlid. Tried to reconcile the four schools of law.
- **Reconciled the Sufi dispute:** he argued that Wahdat-ul-Wujud and Wahdat-ul-Shuhud differed in words more than in substance.
- **Eased Shia–Sunni tension:** *Izalat-ul-Khifa an Khilafat-ul-Khulafa* defended the first four caliphs in a scholarly rather than abusive way and refused to call Shias non-Muslims.

**2. Intellectual work**

- ***Hujjatullah-il-Baligha*** — his greatest book. It explains the wisdom and social purposes behind Islamic rules, and covers belief, worship, economics and politics.
- He wrote over 50 works in Arabic and Persian.

**3. Social and economic ideas**

- A just society needs **economic balance** (he used the term *irtifaqat* for stages of social organisation). When a ruling class lives in luxury on the labour of peasants and craftsmen, the state decays.
- Criticised extravagant customs, the ban on widow remarriage, and un-Islamic rituals.
- Called for fair wages, light taxes and productive work by all classes.

**4. Political role**

- Wrote letters to rulers and nobles (Najib-ud-Daula of Rohilkhand, Nizam-ul-Mulk, and others) urging them to unite and restore order.
- Invited **Ahmad Shah Abdali** of Afghanistan to check Maratha power. Abdali, with Najib-ud-Daula and Shuja-ud-Daula, defeated the Marathas at the **Third Battle of Panipat, 14 January 1761**.
- The victory broke Maratha hopes of ruling Delhi. But Abdali went home, the Mughals could not use the breathing space, and the British were the real gainers.

### Legacy

- His sons continued the school. **Shah Abdul Aziz** issued the 1803 fatwa that India had become **Dar-ul-Harb** after the British took Delhi.
- **Syed Ahmad Shaheed** and **Shah Ismail** (his grandson) came out of this school.
- Deoband (religious learning), Aligarh (rational reinterpretation) and the Ahl-i-Hadith all trace ideas to him. Iqbal regarded him as the first Muslim who felt the urge of a new spirit.

### Assessment

| Strength | Limit |
|---|---|
| Diagnosed the decline as moral, intellectual, economic and political together | Wrote in Arabic and Persian; reached the elite more than the masses |
| Made the Quran accessible | The political plan failed: Panipat weakened the Marathas but did not revive Muslim power |
| Sought unity among sects and schools | Could not build an organisation in his lifetime |
| Left a school that produced the next century's movements | Some historians (e.g. S.A.A. Rizvi) question how much the Abdali letters influenced events |

## C. Syed Ahmad Shaheed Barelvi, 1786–1831, and the Jihad Movement

### Life

- Born 1786 at **Rae Bareli** (Awadh).
- In 1806 went to Delhi and became a disciple of **Shah Abdul Aziz**.
- Served for some years (about 1810–17) in the cavalry of **Amir Khan of Tonk**, where he learned warfare. Left when Amir Khan made terms with the British.
- Returned to Delhi. **Shah Ismail** (grandson of Shah Waliullah) and **Maulvi Abdul Hai** became his followers.
- Toured north India preaching reform. Performed Hajj in 1821–23 with several hundred followers.

### Ideas

- His movement was called **Tariqa-i-Muhammadiya** (the way of the Prophet): pure monotheism, rejection of shrine-worship and innovations, simple living.
- Key texts: ***Sirat-i-Mustaqim*** (his teachings, compiled by Shah Ismail and Abdul Hai) and Shah Ismail's ***Taqwiyat-ul-Iman***.
- British writers called the movement "Wahhabi". It had parallels with the Arabian movement but grew from Shah Waliullah's Indian school.

### The Jihad Movement

**Why against the Sikhs.** Under Ranjit Singh's rule in Punjab and the frontier, Muslims complained of restrictions on the call to prayer, misuse of mosques, and harsh treatment. The frontier also offered a Muslim tribal population and a base outside British India.

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| 1826 | Left Rae Bareli with followers; travelled through Rajasthan, Sindh, Balochistan and Afghanistan to reach the Peshawar valley. Base near Nowshera |
| December 1826 | First clash at **Akora** — success in a night attack; then Hazro |
| January 1827 | Declared **Imam** (Amir-ul-Mominin) at Hund; many Pathan chiefs gave allegiance |
| 1827 | Battle of Saidu: lost because Yar Muhammad Khan, a Peshawar sardar, deserted after reportedly being bought by the Sikhs; an attempt was made to poison Syed Ahmad |
| 1830 | Captured **Peshawar**. Introduced Shariat rule and collected ushr. Reforms such as ending the sale of brides and enforcing tax offended tribal custom and chiefs |
| Late 1830 | Many of his officials were killed in a planned rising by tribesmen. He left the valley for Hazara |
| **6 May 1831** | **Battle of Balakot.** Sikh forces under Sher Singh surprised the mujahideen. Syed Ahmad, Shah Ismail and several hundred followers were killed |

### Why it failed

1. Betrayal and unreliability of local chiefs (Yar Muhammad, Sultan Muhammad).
2. The mujahideen were outsiders in Pathan society; their reforms cut across tribal custom (Pashtunwali).
3. A small, poorly armed volunteer force against Ranjit Singh's European-trained army.
4. No secure supply line from India.
5. Sikh use of money and intrigue.

### Importance

- The first organised, armed movement by Indian Muslims to set up an Islamic polity. It was a people's movement, not a prince's war.
- It joined religious reform to political action and drew volunteers from Bengal to the frontier.
- The network survived. Under the Patna leaders (Wilayat Ali and Inayat Ali) it fought the British on the frontier until the Ambela campaign (1863). The "Wahhabi trials" followed in the 1860s.
- It gave later generations a model of sacrifice. I.H. Qureshi and others treat it as a forerunner of the Pakistan Movement.

## D. The Faraizi Movement and Titu Mir (Bengal)

### Haji Shariatullah, 1781–1840

- Born in Faridpur district, East Bengal. Lived about twenty years in the Hijaz and returned around 1818–20.
- Began the **Faraizi Movement**: Muslims must perform their *faraiz* (obligatory duties) and give up un-Islamic customs picked up from Hindu neighbours.
- Declared India under British rule **Dar-ul-Harb**; therefore Friday and Eid congregational prayers should be suspended.
- Replaced the terms pir and murid with *ustad* and *shagird* (teacher and student) to stress equality.
- His followers were mostly poor Muslim peasants under Hindu landlords and British indigo planters. The landlords resisted him and had him expelled from Dhaka.

### Dudu Mian (Mohsinuddin Ahmad), 1819–1862

- Son of Shariatullah. Turned the movement into an organised peasant resistance.
- Taught that **land belongs to God**; no one has a right to levy illegal taxes on it. Peasants should refuse cesses such as the tax for Hindu festivals and the "beard tax".
- Divided East Bengal into circles, each under a *khalifa*; ran village courts.
- Arrested several times; detained during 1857. Died 1862. The movement weakened afterwards.

### Titu Mir (Mir Nisar Ali), 1782–1831

- From West Bengal (24 Parganas). Met Syed Ahmad Barelvi during Hajj.
- Organised peasants against Hindu zamindars who taxed beards and mosques.
- Built a **bamboo fort at Narkelberia**. British troops destroyed it in November 1831; he was killed.

### Importance of the Bengal movements

- Religious revival and economic protest ran together.
- They gave Bengali Muslim peasants a sense of community and rights.
- This awakening is part of the background to Muslim political activity in Bengal: the partition of 1905 and the founding of the League at Dhaka in 1906.

## E. Comparing the movements

| | Sirhindi | Shah Waliullah | Syed Ahmad Shaheed | Faraizis |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Period | c. 1599–1624 | 1732–62 | 1818–31 | c. 1818–62 |
| Threat faced | Absorption under Akbar's policy | Political collapse and disunity | Sikh rule; social decay | Landlords, planters, un-Islamic customs |
| Method | Letters to nobles; Sufi network | Scholarship; translation; letters to rulers | Preaching, then armed jihad | Preaching and peasant organisation |
| Audience | Nobility and Sufis | Scholars and rulers | Common people across north India | Bengali peasants |
| Result | Shifted the court's direction over time | Founded a school of thought | Military failure; lasting inspiration | Social awakening in Bengal |

**Shared weakness:** none could reverse the political decline. **Shared result:** they kept alive a distinct Muslim identity and a tradition of reform that Aligarh and the Muslim League later built on.

## Likely questions

1. Evaluate the role of Shaikh Ahmad Sirhindi in the revival of Islam and Muslim identity. *(2020, 2024; model answer 2.)*
2. Shah Waliullah saved the Muslims of India from political and religious decline. Discuss. *(2016)*
3. Assess the aims, course and failure of the Jihad Movement of Syed Ahmad Shaheed. What was its lasting importance?
4. Describe the Faraizi Movement. How did it combine religious and economic aims?
5. Compare the approaches of Shah Waliullah and Sir Syed Ahmad Khan to Muslim revival.

**Skeleton for Q5:** context of each (1730s collapse; post-1857 defeat) → diagnosis (religious and moral decay vs educational and political backwardness) → method (return to Quran and hadith, ijtihad, political alliance vs Western education, loyalty, rational theology) → common ground (both wanted ijtihad and unity) → results → judgement: complementary responses to different centuries.
