# 2. Advent of Islam, Muslim Rule and its Decline

Syllabus item I: "Muslim rule in the Sub-Continent, its downfall and efforts for renaissance". Rarely a full question on its own since 2016, but it is the background to the reform movements and to "evolution of Muslim identity" (asked 2021). It is also a rich source of MCQs.

## 1. How Islam reached the subcontinent

Three routes. Use all three in an answer.

| Route | When | Detail |
|---|---|---|
| Traders | From the 7th century | Arab merchants settled on the Malabar coast (Kerala) and in Sri Lanka. The Mappila (Moplah) Muslims descend from this contact. Peaceful and commercial |
| Conquest | 711–712 | Muhammad bin Qasim, sent by Hajjaj bin Yusuf (governor of Iraq under the Umayyad caliph Walid I), took Debal, Nerun, Sehwan and defeated Raja Dahir at Rawar. He went on to Multan. Sindh became "Bab-ul-Islam" (gateway of Islam) |
| Sufis | 11th century onward | The main reason for mass conversion. Ali Hujwiri (Data Ganj Bakhsh, Lahore, d. c. 1072–77), Moinuddin Chishti (Ajmer, d. 1236), Bahauddin Zakariya (Multan, Suhrawardi order, d. 1262), Fariduddin Ganj Shakar (Pakpattan, d. 1265), Nizamuddin Auliya (Delhi, d. 1325), Shah Jalal (Sylhet), Lal Shahbaz Qalandar (Sehwan, d. 1274) |

**Why Muhammad bin Qasim's rule mattered.** He treated Hindus and Buddhists as protected people (zimmis), kept local officials in post, and left temples standing. This set an early pattern of rule by accommodation.

## 2. Muslim rule in outline

| Period | Rulers | Points to remember |
|---|---|---|
| 712 onward | Arab rule in Sindh and Multan | Under the Umayyads, then Abbasids, then local dynasties |
| 977–1186 | Ghaznavids | Mahmud of Ghazni led 17 expeditions (1000–1027); Somnath 1025–26. Punjab annexed; Lahore became a centre of Persian culture. Al-Biruni wrote *Kitab-ul-Hind* |
| 1186–1206 | Ghorids | Muhammad Ghori lost the First Battle of Tarain (1191) and won the Second (1192) against Prithviraj Chauhan. This opened north India |
| 1206–1526 | Delhi Sultanate | Five dynasties: Slave/Mamluk (1206–90; Qutbuddin Aibak, Iltutmish, Razia, Balban), Khalji (1290–1320; Alauddin Khalji's market controls), Tughlaq (1320–1414; Muhammad bin Tughlaq, Firoz Shah), Sayyid (1414–51), Lodhi (1451–1526) |
| 1526–1857 | Mughal Empire | Babur beat Ibrahim Lodhi at the First Battle of Panipat (21 April 1526). Humayun (1530–40, 1555–56). Sher Shah Suri (1540–45: Grand Trunk Road, rupiya, land revenue system). Akbar (1556–1605). Jahangir (1605–27). Shah Jahan (1628–58). Aurangzeb (1658–1707) |

### The six great Mughals at a glance

| Emperor | Reign | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Babur | 1526–30 | Founder; *Tuzuk-i-Baburi* |
| Humayun | 1530–40, 1555–56 | Lost to Sher Shah; returned with Persian help |

## 3. Legacy of Muslim rule

This is what "historical aspects" of ideology means. Muslims did not see themselves as outsiders in India. They had shaped it.

| Field | Contribution |
|---|---|
| Political and administrative | A unified empire across most of the subcontinent; provincial administration (subahs); the mansabdari ranking system; Sher Shah's and Todar Mal's land revenue systems; a postal network; a common coinage |
| Legal | Qazi courts; codification under Aurangzeb (*Fatawa-i-Alamgiri*); in practice, personal law for each community |
| Social | A message of equality that drew converts from lower castes; Sufi khanqahs open to all; purdah, new dress, cuisine and manners |
| Language and literature | Persian as the court language; the growth of **Urdu** from the contact of Persian, Arabic, Turkish and local dialects; historical writing (Barani, Abul Fazl, Badauni) |
| Architecture and art | Qutb Minar, Fatehpur Sikri, Taj Mahal, Lahore Fort, Badshahi and Wazir Khan mosques, Shalimar; miniature painting; gardens |
| Education | Madrassas and maktabs; patronage of scholars |
| Economy | Roads, sarais, canals; India as a leading textile exporter; new crafts |
| Religious thought | Two strands: an inclusive Sufi strand (Chishtis, Dara Shikoh) and a reformist strand insisting on the boundaries of Islam (Sirhindi, Aurangzeb, Shah Waliullah). Both shaped later Muslim identity |

**Exam use.** When asked how a separate Muslim identity evolved, begin here: a community with its own law, language, art and memory of rule existed before the British arrived. British rule and Hindu revivalism then made that identity political.

## 4. Decline of the Mughal Empire

Aurangzeb died in 1707. Within fifty years the empire was a shell.

### Causes of decline

**Internal**

1. **Weak successors and wars of succession.** No fixed law of succession. Between 1707 and 1719 several emperors were made and unmade by nobles (the Sayyid brothers).
2. **Size and overstretch.** Aurangzeb's 25 years in the Deccan drained the treasury and kept him away from the north.
3. **Aurangzeb's policies.** Debated. One view (Jadunath Sarkar) blames his religious policy for alienating Rajputs, Sikhs, Marathas and Jats. Another (I.H. Qureshi; later historians such as M. Athar Ali and Satish Chandra) stresses structural problems and notes that he employed more Hindu nobles than his predecessors. Present both.
4. **Crisis of the jagirdari/mansabdari system.** Too many nobles, too few productive jagirs. Nobles squeezed peasants; revenue fell; peasant revolts followed.
5. **Decay of the nobility.** Factions (Irani, Turani, Hindustani) fought each other. Luxury replaced service.
6. **Military backwardness.** No navy. Artillery and infantry drill fell behind Europe.
7. **Intellectual stagnation.** Little interest in the science, printing and seafaring that were transforming Europe.
8. **Economic strain.** Heavy taxation, decline of trade security, and wealth carried away by invaders.

**External**

9. **Regional powers.** Marathas (Shivaji, then the Peshwas), Sikhs in Punjab, Jats near Agra, Rohillas; governors who became independent in Hyderabad (1724), Bengal and Awadh.
10. **Invasions.** Nadir Shah of Persia sacked Delhi in 1739 and took the Peacock Throne and the Koh-i-Noor. Ahmad Shah Abdali invaded several times between 1748 and 1767.
11. **European companies.** The English East India Company (chartered 1600) moved from trade to rule.

### Milestones of British expansion

| Year | Event |
|---|---|
| 1757 | Battle of Plassey (23 June). Clive defeated Nawab Siraj-ud-Daula of Bengal with the help of Mir Jafar |
| 1761 | Third Battle of Panipat (14 January). Abdali defeated the Marathas. Neither the Mughals nor the Marathas could now stop the British |
| 1764 | Battle of Buxar. The Company defeated Mir Qasim, Shuja-ud-Daula and Shah Alam II |
| 1765 | Treaty of Allahabad. The Company got the diwani (revenue rights) of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa |
| 1799 | Tipu Sultan of Mysore killed at Seringapatam (4 May) in the Fourth Anglo-Mysore War |
| 1803 | The British took Delhi; the emperor became a pensioner. Shah Abdul Aziz declared India Dar-ul-Harb |
| 1843 | Sindh annexed after the battles of Miani and Dabo (Sir Charles Napier) |
| 1849 | Punjab annexed after the Second Anglo-Sikh War |
| 1856 | Awadh annexed |
| 1857 | War of Independence; end of the Mughal dynasty |

### Resistance by Muslim rulers

- **Siraj-ud-Daula** (Bengal) — defeated at Plassey, 1757.
- **Haider Ali and Tipu Sultan** (Mysore) — four Anglo-Mysore wars (1767–99). Tipu modernised his army, used rockets, sought French and Ottoman help.
- **Hafiz Rahmat Khan** (Rohilkhand) — killed 1774.

## 5. Effects of the decline on Muslims

| Area | Effect |
|---|---|
| Political | Loss of power, first to Marathas and Sikhs, then to the British |
| Economic | Loss of state jobs and army service; the Permanent Settlement of Bengal (1793) created a new class of mostly Hindu zamindars; resumption of rent-free land grants (1828 onward) ruined Muslim schools and families |
| Educational and cultural | Persian replaced by English as the official language (1837); Macaulay's Minute (1835) made English education the path to jobs; Muslims stayed away |
| Religious | Missionary activity; un-Islamic customs; sectarian division |
| Psychological | A sense of loss, which produced two responses: revival (Shah Waliullah, Syed Ahmad, Faraizis) and adjustment (Sir Syed) |

W.W. Hunter's *The Indian Musalmans* (1871) described the Muslims of Bengal as a community ruined under British rule. It is a useful reference for the condition of Muslims before Aligarh.

## 7. Likely questions

1. Discuss the causes of the downfall of Muslim rule in the subcontinent.
2. What were the main contributions of Muslim rule to the society and culture of the subcontinent?
3. Critically evaluate the evolution of Muslim identity in the subcontinent. *(2021)*
4. "The decline of the Mughals was due less to Aurangzeb than to the system he inherited." Discuss.

**Skeleton for Q3:** arrival and conversion (traders, conquest, Sufis) → identity under Muslim rule (law, language, culture) → defence of identity (Sirhindi, Shah Waliullah) → loss of power and reform movements → 1857 and Aligarh (identity becomes political) → Urdu–Hindi, separate electorates → Iqbal and Jinnah (identity becomes national) → assessment: identity was formed over centuries by religion, culture and politics together.
