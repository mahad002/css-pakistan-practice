# 1. Ideology of Pakistan

Syllabus item I. Asked in 2016, 2018, 2021, 2023 and 2026. This is the single most reliable history topic.

## 1. What "ideology" means

An ideology is a set of shared beliefs and aims that gives a group its sense of who it is and what it wants to build. It answers three questions: who are we, what do we want, and how should we organise our collective life.

**Ideology of Pakistan, in one sentence:** the Muslims of the subcontinent were a separate nation with their own faith, culture, history and social order, and therefore had the right to a state where they could shape their collective life according to their own values.

It has three linked parts:

| Part | Content |
|---|---|
| Identity | Muslims of India were a nation, not a minority (Two-Nation Theory) |
| Territory | That nation needed a homeland in the Muslim-majority areas |
| Purpose | The state should reflect Islamic principles of justice, equality and welfare, and protect minorities |

Do not write that the ideology is simply "Islam". The ideology is a **political claim built on a religious and cultural identity**. That distinction lets you handle Iqbal, Jinnah and the later debates properly.

## 2. Sources of the ideology

1. **Islam as a complete social order.** A shared belief (tawhid, prophethood), law, and community (ummah).
2. **A separate historical experience.** Muslims arrived as traders, rulers and Sufis from 712 onward. They ruled for centuries and built a distinct Indo-Muslim culture.
3. **Cultural difference.** Language and script (Urdu/Persian script against Hindi/Devanagari), dress, food, festivals, heroes, law of marriage and inheritance, and social structure (no caste in principle).
4. **Minority fears under representative government.** Once the British introduced elections, numbers mattered. Muslims were about a quarter of India. Majority rule looked like permanent Hindu rule.
5. **Economic and educational backwardness after 1857.** Muslims lost land, jobs and patronage. Protection of jobs, language and seats became political demands.
6. **Hindu revivalism.** Arya Samaj (1875), the cow-protection movement, Shuddhi and Sangathan in the 1920s, and the Hindi campaign made many Muslims feel they would not be treated as equals.

## 3. The Two-Nation Theory

**Meaning.** Hindus and Muslims were two nations by every test of nationhood: religion, law, culture, history, social life. They could not be merged into a single Indian nation by a common electorate.

### How the idea developed

| Stage | Who | What they said or did |
|---|---|---|
| Early roots | Al-Biruni (11th century, *Kitab-ul-Hind*) | Noted that Hindus and Muslims differed in everything from belief to custom |
| Religious defence | Shaikh Ahmad Sirhindi (d. 1624) | Opposed the merging of Islam with Hindu practice under Akbar |
| Political survival | Shah Waliullah (d. 1762) | Tried to restore Muslim power and unity |
| Modern statement | Sir Syed Ahmad Khan | After the Urdu–Hindi dispute of 1867 he began to speak of Muslims as a separate *qaum* (nation). In 1887–88 he opposed Muslim participation in Congress because elections would mean majority rule |
| Constitutional form | Simla Deputation 1906; separate electorates 1909 | Muslims recognised as a separate political community |
| Territorial form | Iqbal, Allahabad 1930; Chaudhry Rahmat Ali, 1933 | A Muslim state in the north-west; the name "Pakistan" |
| Political programme | Jinnah, Lahore 1940 | Muslims are a nation and must have a homeland |

### Arguments for the theory (as made at the time)

- Religion in India shaped law, food, marriage, and daily contact. The two communities lived side by side but did not merge.
- The Congress governments of 1937–39 showed what majority rule might look like.
- Separate electorates had already accepted the principle.

### Criticisms and alternative views

Give these in an exam answer. It shows balance.

| Critic | Argument |
|---|---|
| Congress (Gandhi, Nehru) | India was one nation; religion does not make a nation; communalism was a product of British "divide and rule" |
| Maulana Abul Kalam Azad (*India Wins Freedom*) | Partition would leave Indian Muslims weaker and would not solve the minority problem |
| Maulana Husain Ahmad Madani (Deoband, *Muttahida Qaumiyat aur Islam*, 1938) | Nations are formed by homeland; Muslims and Hindus could be one nation politically while remaining separate religious communities. Iqbal publicly rejected this |
| After 1971 | Critics said the separation of East Pakistan disproved the theory. The reply: Bangladesh did not rejoin India; it stayed a separate Muslim-majority state. What failed was the management of the federation, not the idea that Muslims were distinct from the Hindu majority |
| Modern historians | Ayesha Jalal argues that Jinnah used the demand for Pakistan to win an equal share of power at the all-India centre, and that a fully separate state was not his first aim. Others (Anita Inder Singh, I.H. Qureshi, Sikandar Hayat) hold that separation was the aim from 1940 |

## 4. Allama Iqbal (1877–1938)

### His thought in brief

- **Khudi (selfhood).** The individual and the community must develop their own strength and personality. A community that imitates others dies.
- **Islam as a social order.** Religion is not a private matter in Islam. Faith and polity are linked. If Islam is reduced to private belief, the Muslim community loses its basis.
- **Rejection of territorial nationalism.** Nationalism based on land and race divides Muslims and puts country in the place of faith. He set out this view in poems such as *Wataniyat* and in his 1938 reply to Maulana Madani.
- **Ijtihad.** In *The Reconstruction of Religious Thought in Islam* (lectures given 1928–29, published 1930; enlarged Oxford edition 1934) he argued for reinterpreting Islamic law through an elected Muslim assembly. This is his answer to how a modern Muslim state would work.
- **Spiritual democracy and social justice.** He called for a society based on equality, solidarity and freedom, and was critical of both capitalism and atheistic socialism.

### The Allahabad Address, 29 December 1930

Presidential address to the 21st session of the All-India Muslim League.

Main points:

1. India is a continent of human groups belonging to different races, languages and religions. European-style democracy cannot be applied without recognising communal groups.
2. Islam is the most important force that formed the Muslim community of India. The Muslim demand for "a Muslim India within India" is therefore justified.
3. The well-known passage: he wished to see Punjab, the North-West Frontier Province, Sind and Baluchistan joined into a single state, and saw a consolidated north-west Indian Muslim state as the final destiny of Muslims, at least of north-west India, "within the British Empire, or without" it.
4. Such a state would give Islam a chance to free itself from the stamp of Arab imperialism and to bring its law and culture closer to the modern spirit.
5. It would also defend India's north-west frontier.

**Points to note for analysis:**

- He spoke of the north-west only. Bengal was not included in 1930.
- The words "within or without the British Empire" and "within the body-politic of India" show he had a large autonomous Muslim unit in mind, possibly inside a loose Indian federation. Historians differ on whether he was asking for a sovereign state in 1930.
- By 1937 his position was firmer. In letters to Jinnah (28 May and 21 June 1937) he asked why the Muslims of north-west India and Bengal should not be treated as nations entitled to self-determination, and urged a separate federation of Muslim provinces. Here Bengal is included.
- He pressed Jinnah to lead: he described Jinnah as the only Muslim in India to whom the community could look for safe guidance.

### Iqbal's other political work

- Member, Punjab Legislative Council, 1926–30.
- Attended the Second and Third Round Table Conferences (1931, 1932).
- President, Punjab Muslim League; supported Jinnah against the Unionists.
- Died 21 April 1938, two years before the Lahore Resolution.

## 5. Quaid-i-Azam Muhammad Ali Jinnah (1876–1948)

### From unity to separation

| Period | Position |
|---|---|
| 1906–1920 | Congress member; joined the Muslim League in 1913 while staying in Congress. Architect of the Lucknow Pact 1916. Called the "ambassador of Hindu–Muslim unity" (a phrase from G.K. Gokhale's praise, popularised by Sarojini Naidu) |
| 1920 | Left Congress at Nagpur over Gandhi's non-cooperation methods |
| 1927–29 | Delhi Proposals; rejected the Nehru Report; Fourteen Points (March 1929) |
| 1930–34 | Round Table Conferences; lived in London; returned to lead the League in 1934 |
| 1937–39 | Congress ministries convinced him that safeguards on paper were worthless |
| 1940 | Lahore Resolution: Muslims are a nation |
| 1947 | Governor-General; laid out principles for the state |

### Key statements (know the date and place)

| Date | Occasion | Substance |
|---|---|---|
| 22 March 1940 | Presidential address, Lahore | Hindus and Muslims belong to two different religious philosophies, social customs and literatures. They neither intermarry nor interdine. The problem in India is not inter-communal but international. Muslims are a nation by any definition and must have their homelands, territory and state |
| 8 March 1944 | Aligarh | Pakistan started the moment the first non-Muslim was converted to Islam in India, long before Muslims established their rule (the point: Muslim nationhood rests on faith, not on a recent quarrel) |
| 11 August 1947 | First address to the Constituent Assembly, Karachi | Law and order is the first duty of government. Curb bribery, black-marketing, nepotism. Citizens are free to go to their temples, mosques or any place of worship; religion, caste or creed has nothing to do with the business of the state. In time Hindus would cease to be Hindus and Muslims cease to be Muslims "not in the religious sense… but in the political sense as citizens of the State" |
| 25 January 1948 | Karachi Bar Association | Rejected the claim that the constitution would not be based on Shariat; Islamic principles are as applicable today as 1,300 years ago |
| February 1948 | Broadcast to the people of the USA | Pakistan's constitution would be democratic, embodying the essential principles of Islam. Pakistan was not going to be a theocratic state ruled by priests with a divine mission. Non-Muslims would have the same rights as other citizens |
| 21 March 1948 | Dhaka | Urdu would be the state language; warned against provincialism |
| 14 June 1948 | Staff College, Quetta | Reminded officers of their oath and that executive authority flows from the head of government; the armed forces serve the constitution |
| 1 July 1948 | Opening of the State Bank | The Western economic system had created problems for humanity; Pakistan should present an economic system based on Islamic ideas of equality and social justice |

### The debate over Jinnah's vision

Two readings exist. Present both.

- **A Muslim-majority state with equal citizenship and no theocracy.** Rests on 11 August 1947 and the February 1948 broadcast. Argued by, among others, Justice Muhammad Munir (*From Jinnah to Zia*) and Ayesha Jalal.
- **A state guided by Islamic principles.** Rests on his many references to Islamic social justice, the 1948 Bar Association and State Bank speeches, and his pre-1947 campaign. Argued by I.H. Qureshi, Sharif al Mujahid and others.

A fair synthesis: Jinnah wanted a **modern democratic state for a Muslim nation, inspired by Islamic principles of equality, justice and fair play, with full rights for minorities, and not run by clerics.** He saw no conflict between these.

## 6. Iqbal and Jinnah compared (asked in 2026)

| | Iqbal | Jinnah |
|---|---|---|
| Role | Thinker and poet; gave the idea its philosophical base | Lawyer and statesman; turned the idea into a state |
| Starting point | Islam as a social order; the self (khudi) of the community | Constitutional rights and safeguards for a minority that was really a nation |
| Method | Lectures, poetry, the Allahabad Address, letters | Negotiation, party organisation, elections, constitutional argument |
| On nationalism | Rejected territorial nationalism on religious and philosophical grounds | Used the language of national self-determination: Muslims are a nation and so have a right to a state |
| Territory | North-west India (1930); added Bengal by 1937 | North-west and north-east zones (1940); one state by 1946 |
| On the state | Muslim state where ijtihad through an assembly would renew Islamic law; "spiritual democracy" | Democratic constitution on Islamic principles of justice; equal citizenship; no theocracy |
| On economy | Strong concern with poverty and the Muslim peasant; critical of capitalism | Islamic social justice; warned against exploitation by landlords and capitalists (Delhi, 1943) |
| Common ground | Muslims are a distinct nation; safeguards within a Hindu-majority centre would fail; the Muslim-majority provinces must be the base of a Muslim polity; the state must serve ordinary people | |

**Line of influence.** Iqbal's 1937 letters pushed Jinnah towards the Muslim-majority provinces as the base of Muslim politics. Jinnah later said that Iqbal's views had led him to the same conclusions. Jinnah published the letters in 1943 with a foreword saying so.

**Difference in emphasis, not a contradiction.** Iqbal asked what a Muslim community is and what it should become. Jinnah asked how that community could secure power and rights in a constitution. One is the vision; the other is the politics.

## 7. Ideology after 1947

| Step | Significance |
|---|---|
| Objectives Resolution, 12 March 1949 | Sovereignty belongs to Allah; authority is exercised by the people through chosen representatives as a sacred trust; democracy, freedom, equality, tolerance and social justice as set out by Islam; minorities free to profess and practise religion |
| 1956 Constitution | "Islamic Republic"; president must be a Muslim; no law repugnant to Islam |
| 1962 Constitution | Dropped "Islamic" at first; restored by the First Amendment (1963); Advisory Council of Islamic Ideology |
| 1973 Constitution | Islam the state religion (Art. 2); Council of Islamic Ideology (Arts. 228–230); Objectives Resolution made a substantive part as Art. 2A in 1985 |
| Zia period | Hudood Ordinances 1979, Federal Shariat Court 1980, zakat and ushr; the phrase "ideology of Pakistan" entered law and textbooks |

**Scholarly note.** The phrase "ideology of Pakistan" was not in common use before 1947. It became official language later, especially from the 1960s. Critics such as K.K. Aziz (*The Murder of History*) argue that textbooks turned a political history into a fixed creed. You can mention this in a critical question, in one or two sentences.

## 8. Why the ideology still matters

- It is the constitutional basis of the state (Preamble, Arts. 2, 2A, 31, 227).
- It is the base of national identity across ethnic groups.
- It shapes foreign policy positions (Kashmir, Palestine, ties with the Muslim world).
- Its promise of social justice and equal citizenship is a standard for judging the state's performance.
