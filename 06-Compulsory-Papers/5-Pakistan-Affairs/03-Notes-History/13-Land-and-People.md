# 13. Land and People of Pakistan

Syllabus item II: geography, society, natural resources, agriculture, industry and education — "characteristics, trends and problems". Asked nine times since 2016: national culture (2017), HEC (2017, 2021), population (2019), natural resources (2019), agriculture (2023), industry (2023), minerals (2023 special), geostrategic position (2023, 2023 special).

Figures are the commonly cited ones. Sources differ slightly on border lengths and river lengths; use "about". Economic figures are from the Economic Survey 2025–26 as reported in the press (June 2026) unless stated. Update them from the 2026–27 Survey before the exam.

## 1. Location and geostrategic importance

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Area | 796,096 sq km (excluding Azad Kashmir and Gilgit-Baltistan) |
| Position | Roughly 24°–37° N, 61°–77° E; South Asia, at the meeting point with Central Asia, West Asia and China |
| Borders (approximate) | Afghanistan about 2,600 km (Durand Line, 1893); India about 2,900 km including the Line of Control and Working Boundary; Iran about 900 km; China about 600 km |
| Coast | About 1,050 km on the Arabian Sea (Sindh and Balochistan). Ports: Karachi, Port Qasim, Gwadar |
| Sea zone | Exclusive Economic Zone of about 240,000 sq km, extended by about 50,000 sq km of continental shelf (UN decision, 2015) |
| Provinces by area | Balochistan 347,190 sq km (44%); Punjab 205,344; Sindh 140,914; Khyber Pakhtunkhwa 101,741 (after the FATA merger); Islamabad 906 |

### Why the location matters (2023 and 2023 special)

1. **Gateway to Central Asia and western China:** the shortest route to the sea for both; CPEC and Gwadar.
2. **Near the Strait of Hormuz** and the Gulf's oil lanes.
3. **Neighbour of two great powers** (China, India) and of Afghanistan and Iran.
4. **Bridge** between South Asia, West Asia and Central Asia: pipelines (TAPI, Iran–Pakistan), power lines (CASA-1000), trade corridors.
5. **A nuclear-armed Muslim state** with the world's fifth-largest population.

### The costs of location

- A front-line role in other people's wars (1950s pacts, 1979–89, 2001–21).
- Spill-over from Afghanistan: refugees, militancy, weapons, drugs.
- Rivalry on two borders drives defence spending.
- Great-power competition (USA–China) pulls in opposite directions.
- Effect on institutions: a security-first state, a strong military, and aid dependence. This is the link the 2023 special question wanted.

## 2. Physical features

| Region | Features |
|---|---|
| **Northern mountains** | Three ranges meet: **Himalayas** (Nanga Parbat, 8,126 m), **Karakoram** (K2, 8,611 m, the world's second-highest peak) and **Hindu Kush** (Tirich Mir, 7,708 m). Five of the world's fourteen peaks above 8,000 m. Glaciers: Siachen, Baltoro, Biafo, Batura — the largest ice mass outside the poles, and the source of the Indus |
| **Western mountains** | Safed Koh, Sulaiman and Kirthar ranges. Passes: **Khyber**, Kurram, Tochi, Gomal, **Bolan**. Khunjerab Pass (about 4,700 m) links to China |
| **Potwar Plateau and Salt Range** | Between the Indus and Jhelum; oil, gas, rock salt at **Khewra** |
| **Balochistan Plateau** | Dry basins and ranges; Chagai hills; Makran coast |
| **Indus Plain** | Upper (Punjab, with its doabs) and Lower (Sindh); the Indus delta. The country's farms, cities and people are concentrated here |
| **Deserts** | **Thar** (Sindh), **Cholistan** (Bahawalpur), **Thal** (between the Indus and Jhelum), **Kharan** (Balochistan) |

### Rivers and water works

- **Indus** (about 3,000 km), rising in Tibet. Eastern tributaries: **Jhelum, Chenab, Ravi, Sutlej** (with the Beas), joining at Panjnad. Western tributaries: **Kabul**, Swat, Kurram, Gomal.
- Balochistan: Hingol, Dasht, Hub, Zhob.
- Lakes: Manchar, Keenjhar, Saif-ul-Malook, Attabad (formed by a landslide in 2010).
- **Dams:** Tarbela (Indus, 1976), Mangla (Jhelum, 1967), Warsak (Kabul), Ghazi-Barotha, Neelum–Jhelum; under construction: **Diamer-Bhasha, Dasu, Mohmand**.
- **Barrages on the Indus:** Jinnah, Chashma, Taunsa, Guddu, **Sukkur** (1932), Kotri.
- The Indus Basin Irrigation System is the largest contiguous canal system in the world.

### Climate

- Mostly arid and semi-arid. Most of the country gets under 250 mm of rain a year.
- Two rain sources: the **summer monsoon** (July–September) and **western disturbances** in winter.
- Extremes: Jacobabad and Sibi among the hottest places on earth; Skardu and Ziarat in deep cold.
- **Climate vulnerability:** Pakistan emits under 1% of global greenhouse gases but is among the countries most damaged by climate disasters (floods of 2010, 2022 and 2025; heatwaves; glacial lake outbursts). See `04-Notes-Contemporary/20-Social-Problems.md`.

## 3. People and society

### Population (7th census, 2023)

| Item | Figure |
|---|---|
| Total | **241.49 million** (fifth-largest in the world) |
| Growth rate 2017–23 | **2.55% a year** — among the highest in Asia |
| By province | Punjab 127.7 m; Sindh 55.7 m; Khyber Pakhtunkhwa 40.9 m; Balochistan 14.9 m; Islamabad 2.4 m |
| Urban share | About 39% |
| Age | About 64% under 30 |
| Religion | Muslims about 96%; Hindus, Christians, Ahmadis, Sikhs, Parsis, Kalash and others about 4% |
| Mother tongues | Punjabi about 37%, Pashto 18%, Sindhi 14%, Saraiki 12%, Urdu 9%, Balochi 3%, then Hindko, Brahui, Kashmiri, Shina, Balti and others |

### Population as a problem (2019) and as an asset (2023)

**Why growth stays high:** early marriage, low female education and employment, preference for sons, weak family-planning services (contraceptive use about one-third of couples), religious and social resistance, and population-based shares in the NFC and in seats, which give provinces no reason to slow growth.

**Effects:** pressure on water, food, housing, schools, jobs and cities; more than two million young people enter the job market each year; per-head gains are eaten up.

**The youth bulge can be a dividend** if the young are educated, skilled and employed: IT and freelancing, vocational training, overseas work, entrepreneurship. Without that it is a source of unrest.

**What to do:** treat population as a national emergency through the CCI; lady health workers and services at the doorstep; girls' secondary schooling; change the NFC formula to reward results; involve ulema, as Bangladesh, Iran and Indonesia did.

### Society and culture: unity in diversity (2017, 2025)

**Sources of diversity:** language, ethnicity (Punjabis, Pashtuns, Sindhis, Baloch, Saraikis, Muhajirs, Kashmiris, Brahuis, Hazaras, Gilgitis, Baltis and more), sect, tribe and biradari, rural and urban ways of life.

**Sources of unity:** Islam; Urdu as a link language; a shared history (the Indus civilisation, Sufi tradition, the freedom movement); food, dress (shalwar kameez), music (qawwali, folk) and cricket; national institutions; internal migration and intermarriage in the cities; mass media.

**Social structure:** family- and kin-centred; rural society shaped by landholding in Sindh and south Punjab and by tribe in Balochistan and the former tribal areas; a growing urban middle class; wide gender gaps.

Developed further in `04-Notes-Contemporary/11-Ethnic-Issues-and-National-Integration.md`.

## 4. Natural resources (2019, 2023 special)

| Resource | Where | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **Land** | About 22–23 million hectares cultivated, four-fifths irrigated | Waterlogging, salinity, shrinking farm size |
| **Water** | Indus system; glaciers; groundwater | Per-head availability has fallen from about 5,000 cubic metres (1951) to under 1,000; storage for about 30 days |
| **Natural gas** | **Sui** (Balochistan, discovered 1952), Qadirpur, Mari, Kandhkot, Uch, and fields in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa | Reserves declining; LNG imported since 2015 |
| **Oil** | Potwar, lower Sindh, Kohat–Karak | Production meets under a fifth of demand |
| **Coal** | **Thar** (about 175 billion tonnes of lignite), Lakhra, Sonda, Salt Range, Chamalang, Duki | Thar now fuels several power plants |
| **Copper and gold** | **Reko Diq** and **Saindak** (Chagai, Balochistan) | Reko Diq: Barrick 50%, Balochistan 25%, federal state companies 25%; first production planned for 2028, with delay risks after the 2026 attacks |
| **Salt** | Khewra, Warcha, Kalabagh | Among the world's largest rock-salt deposits |
| **Chromite** | Muslim Bagh (Balochistan) | |
| **Iron ore** | Kalabagh, Chiniot, Dilband, Nokkundi | Mostly low grade |
| **Gypsum, limestone, marble, granite** | Salt Range, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Balochistan | Base of the cement industry |
| **Gemstones** | Swat (emerald), Hunza (ruby), Gilgit-Baltistan and Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (peridot, topaz, aquamarine) | |
| **Rare earths and critical minerals** | Claimed in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Gilgit-Baltistan, Balochistan | US–Pakistan MoU (September 2025); reserves not yet proven commercially |
| **Forests** | About 5% of the land | Among the lowest in the region; deforestation |
| **Fisheries and coast** | Arabian Sea | Under-developed "blue economy" |
| **Sun and wind** | Balochistan and Sindh (sun); Gharo–Jhimpir corridor (wind) | Rooftop solar has grown very fast since 2023 |
| **Hydropower** | Potential about 60,000 MW; about one-sixth developed | |

**Why minerals are under-used:** insecurity in Balochistan and Khyber Pakhtunkhwa; disputes between centre, provinces and local people over ownership and royalties (Arts. 172(3), 161); contract instability (the Reko Diq arbitration award of about $6 billion in 2019, settled in 2022); weak geological data; little processing, so ore is exported raw; poor roads, rail and power at the sites; lack of capital and technology; environmental and community concerns. Mining is still only about 2% of GDP.

**How to use them better:** settle ownership and royalty rules through the CCI; give local communities jobs and a share; build processing at home; honour contracts; map resources properly; connect mines to rail (the Reko Diq–Gwadar and ML-3 lines); keep policy stable across governments.

## 5. Agriculture (2023)

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Share of GDP | About 23–24% |
| Share of employment | About 37% |
| Growth in FY2026 | 2.89% |
| Sub-sectors | **Livestock** is over 60% of agricultural value; crops; fisheries; forestry |
| Seasons | **Kharif** (sown April–June, harvested October–December): rice, cotton, sugarcane, maize. **Rabi** (sown October–December, harvested April–May): wheat, gram, barley, mustard |
| Major crops | Wheat, rice (a leading export), cotton (base of the textile industry), sugarcane, maize |
| Other | Kinnow, mango, dates, potatoes, onions |

### Why agriculture has lagged and food imports have grown

1. **Water:** flood irrigation wastes more than half the water; canal losses; tail-end shortages; falling water tables.
2. **Low yields:** poor seed (especially cotton), little research, weak extension services.
3. **Small and divided holdings** on one side; large estates run by absentee landlords on the other; incomplete land reform (the Federal Shariat Court's appellate bench ruled against ceilings in 1989).
4. **Climate:** floods (2022 and 2025 destroyed crops across Sindh and Punjab), heat and erratic rain.
5. **Cotton collapse:** output roughly halved from its 2014–15 peak because of pests, poor seed and the shift to sugarcane and rice.
6. **Policy distortions:** support prices that favoured wheat and sugarcane; the sudden end of public wheat procurement in 2024 hurt growers.
7. **Costly inputs and little credit** for small farmers; middlemen take much of the margin.
8. **Post-harvest losses** from poor storage and transport.
9. **Population growth** raises demand faster than output.
10. **Urban sprawl** over good farmland.

Result: Pakistan imports edible oil, pulses, tea, raw cotton and, in bad years, wheat. Food and agricultural imports run to several billion dollars a year.

**Way forward:** drip and sprinkler irrigation with proper water pricing; certified climate-resilient seed; research funding; cooperative and corporate farming on fair terms; storage and cold chains; crop zoning; oilseed promotion; crop insurance; digital land records; livestock breeding and export standards.

## 6. Industry (2023)

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Share of GDP | About 19–20%; manufacturing about 12–13% |
| Growth in FY2026 | 3.51%; manufacturing had its best year in four |
| Main industries | **Textiles** (over half of goods exports), food and sugar, cement, fertiliser, chemicals, pharmaceuticals, steel, automobiles (mostly assembly), leather, sports goods and surgical instruments (Sialkot), cutlery (Wazirabad), fans (Gujrat) |
| Services | About 58% of GDP; IT and IT-enabled exports are the fastest-growing export |

### A short history

- 1947: almost no industry. 1950s: import substitution; PIDC. 1960s: rapid growth under Ayub, concentrated ownership. 1972–76: nationalisation. 1980s: denationalisation begins. 1990s onward: privatisation, liberalisation. 2000s: consumer-led growth. 2010s: energy crisis, then CPEC power plants. 2020s: high energy prices and import curbs.

### Obstacles to industrial development

1. **Energy:** among the highest power and gas tariffs in the region; unreliable supply.
2. **Low investment and savings:** investment about 13–14% of GDP, against 30% or more in India and Bangladesh.
3. **Macroeconomic instability:** stop-go cycles, high interest rates, a volatile rupee, IMF programmes.
4. **Narrow base:** dependence on low-value textiles; little engineering, electronics or chemicals.
5. **Policy:** frequent changes in taxes and tariffs; high tax on the documented sector while much of trade, property and agriculture escapes; protection that rewards assembly over manufacturing.
6. **Legacy of nationalisation** and loss-making state enterprises.
7. **Skills and technology:** low spending on research; weak vocational training.
8. **Infrastructure and logistics:** rail in poor shape; port costs.
9. **Law and order**, contract enforcement, red tape.
10. **Smuggling and under-invoicing**; misuse of Afghan transit trade.
11. **Political instability.**

**Way forward:** competitive energy pricing; a stable ten-year industrial and tariff policy; special economic zones that actually operate (CPEC Phase II); credit and support for small and medium firms; export diversification into IT, engineering, pharmaceuticals, food processing and minerals; technical education linked to industry; privatise or restructure state enterprises.

## 7. Education (2017, 2021, 2026)

| Indicator | Figure |
|---|---|
| Literacy (age 10+) | About 61% in the 2023 census (men about 68%, women about 53%); the Economic Survey 2025–26 reports about 63% |
| Out-of-school children | About 25–26 million aged 5–16 — the second-highest number in the world |
| Public spending | About **0.8% of GDP** in the latest figures — a record low, far below the 4% target |
| Systems | Public schools, private schools (low-fee to elite), madrassas — three streams with different curricula and prospects |
| Constitution | **Art. 25A:** free and compulsory education for ages 5–16. Education a provincial subject since the 18th Amendment |
| Universities | Over 260 recognised institutions, against about 60 in 2002 |

### Problems

- Access: distance, poverty, child labour, girls' safety and missing facilities.
- Quality: "learning poverty" — most ten-year-olds cannot read a simple text; rote learning; teacher absence and weak training.
- Inequality: by gender, province (Balochistan lowest), rural–urban, and class.
- Governance: ghost schools, political postings, weak data, low and unspent budgets.
- Relevance: little technical and vocational training; degrees that do not lead to jobs.
- Language of instruction and curriculum disputes (Single National Curriculum, 2021).

### The Higher Education Commission (2017, 2021)

Set up in **2002**, replacing the University Grants Commission; Dr Atta-ur-Rahman was its first chairman.

**What it did:** thousands of foreign and local PhD scholarships; tenure-track system; digital library and the PERN network; quality assurance cells and university rankings; research grants and offices of research and commercialisation; curriculum revision; degree attestation; plagiarism policy; rapid growth in universities, enrolment and research papers; need-based scholarships (Ehsaas, with provincial schemes); distance learning during COVID-19.

**Criticism:** numbers over quality; "paper mills" and plagiarism scandals; unemployed PhDs; budget cuts in real terms since 2018; disputes with provincial higher-education commissions after the 18th Amendment; weak links with industry; governance crises and vacant vice-chancellor posts.
