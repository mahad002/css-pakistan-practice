# 7. The Pakistan Movement, Part 2: 1930–1940

From the Allahabad Address to the Lahore Resolution. Asked: 1937 elections and Congress ministries (2016, 2019); Two-Nation Theory and the Lahore Resolution (2016). This decade is the most examined part of the freedom movement.

**The story in one line:** the idea of a Muslim territory appeared (1930), the 1935 Act gave the provinces self-government, Congress rule (1937–39) convinced Muslims that safeguards were not enough, and in 1940 the League demanded separate states.

---

## 1. Allahabad Address, 29 December 1930

Covered in full in `01-Ideology-of-Pakistan.md`. Key points for this timeline:

- Iqbal, presiding over the League session, proposed that Punjab, NWFP, Sindh and Balochistan be joined into one Muslim state in north-west India.
- First statement from the League platform of a **territorial** solution.
- Little immediate reaction; its weight grew later.

---

## 2. Round Table Conferences, London, 1930–1932

**Why held:** the Simon Report (1930) pleased no one; the Labour government invited Indian leaders to discuss a new constitution.

| | First RTC | Second RTC | Third RTC |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dates | 12 Nov 1930 – 19 Jan 1931 | 7 Sep – 1 Dec 1931 | 17 Nov – 24 Dec 1932 |
| Congress | Absent (civil disobedience; leaders in jail) | Gandhi as sole delegate | Absent |
| Muslim delegates | Aga Khan (led), Jinnah, Maulana Muhammad Ali Jauhar, Sir Muhammad Shafi, A.K. Fazlul Haq, Sir Zafarullah Khan | Aga Khan, Jinnah, Shafi, **Iqbal**, Zafarullah | Aga Khan, Iqbal, Zafarullah. **Jinnah was not invited** |
| Outcome | Agreement on an all-India **federation**, with princes willing to join; responsible government in provinces; Sindh to be separated | Deadlock on minorities. Gandhi claimed Congress spoke for all India and refused separate electorates. Minorities signed a pact among themselves | Thinly attended; details settled |

Between the first two: the **Gandhi–Irwin Pact (5 March 1931)** ended civil disobedience and brought Gandhi to London.

Maulana Muhammad Ali Jauhar told the first conference he wished to return to a free country or die in a foreign one. He died in London on 4 January 1931 and was buried in Jerusalem.

### Communal Award, 16 August 1932

Since Indians could not agree, Prime Minister **Ramsay MacDonald** gave a decision:

- **Separate electorates** kept for Muslims and extended to Sikhs, Indian Christians, Anglo-Indians, Europeans and the Depressed Classes.
- Weightage for minorities kept.
- Muslims got 86 of 175 seats in Punjab and 119 of 250 in Bengal — the largest group in each but short of a statutory majority, even though they were the majority of the population.
- Sindh to be a separate province.

The League accepted it as the best available, while regretting the Punjab and Bengal figures. Gandhi fasted against separate electorates for the Depressed Classes; the **Poona Pact (September 1932)** with Dr Ambedkar replaced them with reserved seats in joint electorates.

**Results of the conferences:** a White Paper (March 1933), a Joint Select Committee under Lord Linlithgow, and then the Government of India Act 1935.

### Chaudhry Rahmat Ali and the name "Pakistan"

- A Cambridge student. On **28 January 1933** he issued the pamphlet ***Now or Never: Are We to Live or Perish Forever?***
- He coined **PAKSTAN** from **P**unjab, **A**fghania (NWFP), **K**ashmir, **S**indh and Baluchis**TAN**; an "i" was added later for ease of speech.
- He founded the Pakistan National Movement and rejected the federation discussed in London.
- Muslim delegates at the time treated the scheme as a student's idea. He later criticised the 1947 settlement as too small. He died at Cambridge in 1951.

## 3. Government of India Act 1935

The longest act passed by the British Parliament to that date: 321 sections and 10 schedules. Royal assent August 1935. Provincial part in force **1 April 1937**.

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **All-India Federation** | British provinces plus princely states. Never came into being, because the required number of princes did not accede |
| **Dyarchy at the centre** | Defence, foreign affairs, tribal areas reserved to the Governor-General; other subjects to ministers. Not implemented, since the federation was not formed |
| **Provincial autonomy** | Dyarchy ended in the provinces. Ministers responsible to elected legislatures ran all provincial subjects |
| **Three lists** | Federal, provincial, concurrent; residuary powers with the Governor-General |
| **Governors' special powers** | "Special responsibilities" (minorities, peace); Section 93 allowed a governor to take over the administration |
| Legislatures | Two houses at the centre; two houses in six provinces |
| New provinces | **Sindh** separated from Bombay; **Orissa** created; NWFP already a governor's province (1932). Total: 11 provinces |
| Other | **Federal Court** (set up 1937); Reserve Bank of India; Burma and Aden separated from India; separate electorates kept |
| Franchise | About one-tenth of the population (roughly 30–35 million voters) |

**Reactions**

- Jawaharlal Nehru called it a charter of slavery.
- Jinnah described the federal scheme as thoroughly rotten, fundamentally bad and totally unacceptable, but accepted the provincial part as worth working.
- Both parties decided to contest the provincial elections.

**Importance for Pakistan**

- It delivered several of the Fourteen Points: provincial autonomy, Sindh's separation, reforms in NWFP, separate electorates.
- With adaptations it served as **Pakistan's interim constitution from 1947 to 1956**. Its viceregal features — a strong head of state who could dismiss ministries (Section 92A was added in 1948) — shaped Pakistan's early politics.

## 4. The elections of 1937

**Jinnah's return.** Jinnah had settled in London after the Round Table Conferences. Liaquat Ali Khan and others urged him to come back. He was elected president of the reunited League in 1934 and returned to lead it. In 1936 he set up a **Central Parliamentary Board**. The party was weak: few funds, no organisation in the villages, strong rivals in the Muslim-majority provinces.

**Results (January–February 1937)**

| | Detail |
|---|---|
| Total provincial seats | 1,585 |
| Congress | About 711 seats. Clear majorities in Madras, UP, Bihar, CP and Orissa; largest party in Bombay. It later formed ministries in NWFP and Assam too: **eight of eleven provinces** |
| Muslim seats | About 482 |
| Muslim League | **About 109** (figures of 104–110 appear in different sources). Good results in the Muslim-minority provinces: UP 27 of 64, Bombay 20 of 29, Madras 11 of 28 |
| League in the Muslim-majority provinces | Weak: Bengal 39 of 117; Punjab 1 or 2 of 84; Sindh and NWFP none |
| Who won there | **Punjab:** Unionist Party (Sir Sikandar Hayat). **Bengal:** Krishak Praja Party (A.K. Fazlul Haq) in coalition with the League. **Sindh:** Sindh United Party and others. **NWFP:** Congress allied with the Khudai Khidmatgars (Dr Khan Sahib) |
| Congress among Muslims | Contested only 58 Muslim seats and won 26 (mostly in NWFP). It could not claim to represent Muslims either |

**The coalition question.** In UP the League expected to share power after an informal election understanding. Congress offered seats only if the League's group merged into Congress and its parliamentary board was dissolved. The League refused. Maulana Azad later wrote in *India Wins Freedom* that this refusal was a serious mistake that gave the League new life. Nehru had said there were only two forces in India, Congress and the British. Jinnah replied that there was a third: the Muslims.

## 5. Congress ministries, July 1937 – October/November 1939

### Muslim grievances

| Area | Complaint |
|---|---|
| **Symbols** | *Bande Mataram*, from Bankim Chandra's novel *Anandamath* with its anti-Muslim setting, sung in legislatures and schools. The Congress tricolour flown on public buildings as if it were the national flag |
| **Education** | **Wardha Scheme** of basic education (Gandhi's idea; committee under Dr Zakir Husain): craft-centred, no religious instruction, taught through Hindustani, with stress on non-violence and reverence for Gandhi. **Vidya Mandir Scheme** in CP: schools called "temples of learning"; Muslims objected to the name and content |
| **Language** | Promotion of Hindi in Devanagari at the expense of Urdu |
| **Religion** | Restrictions on cow slaughter; interference with the call to prayer and processions reported in several districts; communal riots in which Muslims said the police were partial |
| **Jobs and power** | Muslims complained of exclusion from services and local bodies. Congress committees interfered in district administration |
| **Politics** | The **Muslim Mass Contact Campaign** (Nehru, 1937) tried to win Muslim voters directly over the heads of the League. Refusal of coalitions |

### The League's reports

- **Pirpur Report** (late 1938) — committee under Raja Syed Muhammad Mehdi of Pirpur.
- **Sharif Report** (1939) — on Bihar.
- **A.K. Fazlul Haq, *Muslim Sufferings under Congress Rule*** (December 1939).

### The other side

- Congress denied the charges and offered an inquiry by the Chief Justice of the Federal Court, Sir Maurice Gwyer. Jinnah asked for a Royal Commission instead. Neither was held.
- British governors did not use their special powers to protect minorities, and some reported that the complaints were exaggerated.
- Historians differ. Some (for example R. Coupland) noted real cause for Muslim unease in the "Congress Raj" style of rule. Others see the reports as political documents. **What is not disputed is the effect:** large numbers of Muslims came to believe that under a Congress-ruled India they would be a permanent, powerless minority.

### How the League used the period

| Date | Step |
|---|---|
| October 1937 | **Lucknow session.** Sikandar Hayat (Punjab), Fazlul Haq (Bengal) and Sir Muhammad Saadullah (Assam) advised their Muslim members to join the League (the Sikandar–Jinnah Pact). The League became a mass party: two-anna membership, new flag, new branches |
| 1937–39 | The League won most Muslim by-elections |
| October 1938 | **Sindh Provincial Muslim League Conference, Karachi:** a resolution spoke of Hindus and Muslims as two nations and asked the League to devise a scheme of independence for Muslims |
| December 1938 | **Patna session:** the title *Quaid-i-Azam* came into general use |
| March 1939 | The League's Working Committee appointed a committee to examine partition schemes |

### The end of Congress rule

- **3 September 1939:** Britain declared war on Germany. Viceroy Linlithgow declared India at war without consulting Indian leaders.
- Congress demanded a statement of war aims and immediate self-government. Dissatisfied, its ministries **resigned in October–November 1939**.
- Jinnah called on Muslims to observe **22 December 1939** as the **Day of Deliverance**. Dr Ambedkar and some other non-Congress groups joined in.
- The British now needed Muslim cooperation in the war. The Viceroy assured Jinnah that no constitution would be imposed without Muslim consent.

### Did Congress rule lead to the demand for Pakistan? (2016, 2019)

**Yes, as the decisive trigger:** it turned a fear into an experience, united Muslims behind the League, and discredited "safeguards".

**But not the only cause:** the idea already existed (Iqbal 1930, Rahmat Ali 1933); the 1935 Act had shown the limits of minority politics; the war gave the League leverage; and Jinnah's organising work made the difference. Congress rule was the catalyst, not the origin.

## 6. The Lahore Resolution, 23 March 1940

### Background

- Many partition and zonal schemes were circulating in 1938–40 (Dr Syed Abdul Latif, Sir Sikandar Hayat, the "Aligarh scheme", Sir Abdullah Haroon's committee).
- Days before the session, on **19 March 1940**, police fired on a Khaksar procession in Lahore, killing many. Jinnah came anyway and visited the wounded.

### The session

- **27th annual session** of the League, **22–24 March 1940**, at Minto Park (now Iqbal Park), Lahore.
- **22 March:** Jinnah's presidential address — Hindus and Muslims are two nations; the Indian problem is international, not communal.
- **23 March:** the resolution was moved by **A.K. Fazlul Haq** (premier of Bengal) and seconded by **Chaudhry Khaliquzzaman** (UP). Supported by Maulana Zafar Ali Khan (Punjab), Sardar Aurangzeb Khan (NWFP), Sir Abdullah Haroon (Sindh), Qazi Muhammad Isa (Balochistan) and others.
- **24 March:** passed. A resolution on Palestine was passed at the same session.

### What it said

1. No constitutional plan would work or be acceptable to Muslims unless it followed this principle: geographically contiguous units should be marked out into regions, with territorial adjustments as needed.
2. The areas where Muslims are a majority, as in the **north-western and eastern zones** of India, should be grouped to form **"independent states"** in which the constituent units would be **autonomous and sovereign**.
3. Adequate, effective and mandatory safeguards for minorities in these units and for Muslims in other parts of India.

### Points to analyse

| Issue | Discussion |
|---|---|
| The word "Pakistan" | Not used. The Hindu press called it the "Pakistan Resolution" and the League adopted the name |
| "States" in the plural | Was it two states? In **April 1946** the League Legislators' Convention at Delhi (resolution moved by H.S. Suhrawardy) spoke of **one sovereign independent state**. Jinnah explained the plural as a drafting matter. In 1971 Bengali nationalists pointed to the original wording |
| "Autonomous and sovereign" units | Later quoted by supporters of provincial autonomy |
| Vagueness on borders | No map; "territorial readjustments" left open the partition of Punjab and Bengal |
| Jalal's reading | Ayesha Jalal argues the vagueness was deliberate: it kept Jinnah's options open, including a share of power at an all-India centre. Others reply that the 1941 Madras session made Pakistan the League's creed and that every later step aimed at a state |

### Reactions

- **Congress and Hindu Mahasabha:** rejected it. Gandhi called it the vivisection of India.
- **British:** did not accept it, but the **August Offer (1940)** promised that power would not be transferred to any system whose authority was denied by large elements of India's national life.
- **Muslims:** the demand gave them one clear goal. Within six years the League won almost every Muslim seat.

### Importance

- Changed the Muslim demand from **safeguards for a minority** to **self-determination for a nation**.
- Gave the League a programme that ordinary voters could understand.
- 23 March is Pakistan Day; the 1956 Constitution was brought into force on the same date.
