# 6. The Pakistan Movement, Part 1: 1905–1929

From the partition of Bengal to Jinnah's Fourteen Points. Asked: separate electorates (2023 special). MCQs come from here every year.

**The story in one line:** Muslims organised (1906), won separate electorates (1909), tried partnership with Congress (1916–22), saw it collapse, and by 1929 had set out their own terms.

---

## 1. Partition of Bengal, 1905

**Facts**

- Bengal presidency had about 78–80 million people and included Bihar and Orissa. It was too large for one governor.
- Viceroy **Lord Curzon** announced the partition in July 1905. It took effect on **16 October 1905**.
- New province: **Eastern Bengal and Assam**. Capital **Dhaka**. Population about 31 million, of whom about 18 million were Muslims. First Lieutenant-Governor: Sir Bampfylde Fuller.

**Why**

- British stated reason: administrative efficiency; development of neglected East Bengal and Assam.
- Congress view: "divide and rule", meant to weaken Bengali nationalism centred on Calcutta.

**Reactions**

| Muslims | Hindus |
|---|---|
| Welcomed it. A Muslim-majority province meant schools, jobs, a share in trade, and freedom from Calcutta's landlords, lawyers and merchants. Nawab Salimullah of Dhaka led the support | Opposed it strongly. Calcutta's lawyers, press and businessmen stood to lose. Called it the vivisection of the motherland |

- **Swadeshi movement:** boycott of British goods, bonfires of foreign cloth, national schools. *Bande Mataram* became its song. A terrorist wing appeared (attempts on officials' lives).
- **Annulment:** King George V announced the reversal at the **Delhi Durbar, 12 December 1911**. The capital of India moved from Calcutta to Delhi. Viceroy: Lord Hardinge.

**Importance for Muslims**

1. The Hindu agitation against a measure that helped Muslims showed that the two communities' interests clashed.
2. It pushed Muslims to organise: the Simla Deputation and the Muslim League followed within a year.
3. The annulment taught them that loyalty to the British was no guarantee. Nawab Viqar-ul-Mulk said the government's policy was like a cannon that passed over the dead bodies of Muslims without any feeling.
4. The League moved towards self-government as its goal in 1913.

---

## 2. Simla Deputation, 1 October 1906

**Background.** The Liberal government in Britain (John Morley as Secretary of State) announced plans to enlarge the councils through elections. Muslims feared that in joint electorates with a property franchise they would win almost nothing.

**Facts**

- **35 Muslim leaders** led by **Sir Aga Khan III** met Viceroy **Lord Minto** at Simla.
- Organised by Nawab **Mohsin-ul-Mulk**. The address was drafted mainly by **Syed Hussain Bilgrami**.

**Demands**

1. **Separate electorates:** Muslim voters to elect Muslim representatives.
2. **Weightage:** seats beyond their share of population, in view of their political importance and service to the empire.
3. A share in government services and on the benches of the high courts.
4. Seats in the Viceroy's Executive Council.
5. Help for a Muslim university.

Minto gave a sympathetic reply and accepted that Muslims should be represented as a community.

**Debate: "command performance"?** Congress writers, and Maulana Muhammad Ali Jauhar in 1923, suggested the British arranged the deputation; they cite letters involving Archbold, the Aligarh principal, and Minto's secretary. Most later research (for example by Syed Razi Wasti and Francis Robinson) accepts that the British were glad of it but that the initiative and the demands were Muslim, with roots in Sir Syed's arguments of the 1880s.

**Importance:** the first collective political demand by Indian Muslims; separate electorates accepted in principle; it led directly to a permanent party.

---

## 3. All-India Muslim League, 30 December 1906

**Facts**

- Founded at **Dhaka** after the session of the **Muhammadan Educational Conference**.
- Chair of the meeting: **Nawab Viqar-ul-Mulk**. Proposer: **Nawab Salimullah Khan** of Dhaka. Seconded by Hakim Ajmal Khan; supported by Maulana Muhammad Ali Jauhar and Maulana Zafar Ali Khan.
- Joint secretaries: Mohsin-ul-Mulk and Viqar-ul-Mulk. Constitution drafted by a committee with Muhammad Ali Jauhar (the "Green Book").
- First regular session: **Karachi, December 1907**, presided over by Sir Adamjee Peerbhoy.
- **Aga Khan III** was the first president (to 1913). Head office at Aligarh, then Lucknow.
- London branch: 1908, Syed Ameer Ali.

**Original aims**

1. Promote loyalty to the British government and remove misunderstandings.
2. Protect and advance the political rights and interests of Muslims.
3. Prevent hostility towards other communities, without harm to the first two aims.

**Why it was founded**

- Hindu agitation against the Bengal partition.
- Congress claimed to speak for all but was dominated by Hindus.
- The success of the Simla Deputation needed follow-up.
- The Urdu–Hindi dispute and Hindu revivalism.
- Coming elected councils.

**Change of aim, 1913.** At Lucknow the League adopted "self-government suitable to India" as its goal. **Jinnah joined in 1913** on condition that it would not conflict with his loyalty to the national cause.

---

## 4. Minto–Morley Reforms (Indian Councils Act 1909)

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Councils enlarged | Imperial Legislative Council raised to 60 additional members; provincial councils enlarged |
| Majorities | Official majority kept at the centre; non-official majorities in the provinces |
| Powers | Members could discuss the budget, move resolutions and ask supplementary questions. No control over the executive |
| Indians in the executive | One Indian on the Viceroy's Executive Council (S.P. Sinha) |
| **Separate electorates** | Muslims elected their own representatives, with weightage |

**Importance:** separate electorates gave Muslims a constitutional identity. Congress accepted them in 1916 and attacked them from 1928. Critics say they hardened communal politics. Muslim opinion held that they were the only way a minority could send real representatives. Morley himself said he was not setting India on the road to parliamentary government.

### Evolution of separate electorates (for the 2023 special question)

| Year | Step |
|---|---|
| 1883 | Sir Syed argues in the Viceroy's Council that simple election is unsuited to India |
| 1906 | Simla Deputation demands them |
| 1909 | Granted |
| 1916 | Congress accepts them in the Lucknow Pact |
| 1919 | Kept and extended to Sikhs and others |
| 1927 | League offers to give them up on conditions (Delhi Proposals) |
| 1928 | Nehru Report rejects them |
| 1929 | Fourteen Points insist on them |
| 1932 | Communal Award confirms them |
| 1935 | Built into the Government of India Act |
| 1945–46 | Elections held under them prove the League's mandate |

Did they foreshadow Pakistan? They recognised Muslims as a separate political unit, created Muslim constituencies and Muslim politicians answerable only to Muslim voters, and made the 1946 mandate measurable. They made a separate state possible. They did not make it inevitable: until 1937–40 the League used them to seek safeguards inside India.

---

## 5. Lucknow Pact, December 1916

**Why the two parties came together**

- Annulment of the Bengal partition (1911), the Balkan Wars and the Kanpur mosque incident (1913) turned Muslims against the British.
- The League's new aim of self-government matched that of Congress.
- New leaders: Jinnah was a member of both parties. He presided over the League session; A.C. Majumdar presided over Congress. Both met at Lucknow.
- The First World War raised hopes of reform.

**Terms**

1. Congress accepted **separate electorates**.
2. Muslims to have **one-third** of the elected Indian seats in the Imperial Legislative Council.
3. **Weightage:** more seats than their population share in provinces where Muslims were a minority (for example UP 30%, Bihar 25%, Bombay 33%, Madras 15%, CP 15%); in return fewer than their share where they were a majority (**Punjab 50%, Bengal 40%**).
4. No bill affecting a community to proceed if **three-fourths** of that community's members opposed it.
5. Joint demands: provincial autonomy, elected majorities, half of the Viceroy's Executive Council to be Indian, separation of judiciary and executive.

**Importance**

- The only time Congress accepted the League as the representative of Muslims and conceded separate electorates.
- Jinnah's standing rose as the "ambassador of Hindu–Muslim unity".
- **Cost:** Muslims gave up their majorities in Punjab and Bengal. This weakened them in the two provinces that mattered most and troubled the League for thirty years.

---

## 6. Montagu–Chelmsford Reforms (Government of India Act 1919)

- **Montagu Declaration, 20 August 1917:** the aim of British policy was the gradual development of self-governing institutions.
- **Dyarchy in the provinces:** *transferred* subjects (education, health, local government, agriculture) went to Indian ministers responsible to the legislature; *reserved* subjects (police, justice, revenue, finance) stayed with the governor.
- **Centre:** a two-chamber legislature (Council of State and Legislative Assembly); the executive stayed responsible to London.
- Separate electorates kept and extended. Franchise widened but still small.
- A review after ten years (hence the Simon Commission).

**Why Indians were dissatisfied:** real power stayed with governors; ministers had responsibility without money. The reforms arrived together with the **Rowlatt Act (March 1919)**, which allowed detention without trial. Jinnah resigned from the Imperial Legislative Council in protest. On **13 April 1919** General Dyer's troops fired on a crowd at **Jallianwala Bagh**, Amritsar.

---

## 7. Khilafat Movement, 1919–1924

**Cause.** The Ottoman Sultan was Caliph of the Sunni Muslim world. Turkey fought on Germany's side and lost. Indian Muslims feared that the empire would be dismembered and the holy places placed under non-Muslim control, despite British wartime assurances.

**Aims**

1. Keep the Caliph's temporal power.
2. Keep the holy places (Makkah, Madina, Jerusalem) under Muslim rule.
3. Preserve Turkey's territory.

**Leaders:** Maulana Muhammad Ali Jauhar and Maulana Shaukat Ali (the Ali brothers), Maulana Abul Kalam Azad, Hakim Ajmal Khan, Dr M.A. Ansari, Maulana Hasrat Mohani, Maulana Zafar Ali Khan, Maulana Abdul Bari of Firangi Mahal.

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| 1919 | All-India Khilafat Committee formed (Bombay); Khilafat Conference at Delhi (November). Jamiat Ulema-i-Hind founded |
| Early 1920 | Khilafat delegation under Muhammad Ali Jauhar went to Europe; no result |
| August 1920 | **Treaty of Sèvres** imposed harsh terms on Turkey. **Non-cooperation** began under Gandhi, joined to the Khilafat cause: boycott of titles, councils, courts, schools, foreign cloth |
| Summer 1920 | **Hijrat Movement:** ulema declared India Dar-ul-Harb. Many thousands (estimates start at 18,000) migrated to Afghanistan, which then closed its border. Many returned ruined |
| 1921 | Ali brothers tried at Karachi (Khaliqdina Hall) and jailed. **Moplah rising** in Malabar turned into communal violence |
| 5 February 1922 | **Chauri Chaura:** a mob burned a police station, killing 22 policemen. Gandhi called off non-cooperation without consulting Khilafat leaders |
| November 1922 | Turkish Grand National Assembly abolished the Sultanate |
| **3 March 1924** | **Mustafa Kemal abolished the Caliphate.** The movement lost its purpose |

**Jinnah's position.** He opposed non-cooperation as unconstitutional and likely to lead to disorder. He left Congress at its Nagpur session in December 1920.

**Why it failed**

- Its goal depended on events in Turkey, which Indians could not control; the Turks themselves ended the Caliphate.
- Gandhi's sudden withdrawal.
- The Hijrat disaster and the Moplah violence.
- Leaders in jail; no plan for what came next.

**Results**

| Positive | Negative |
|---|---|
| First mass political movement of Indian Muslims; ordinary people, ulema and women took part | Hindu–Muslim unity collapsed. Shuddhi and Sangathan (Hindu), Tabligh and Tanzim (Muslim) followed; serious riots through the 1920s |
| Produced a generation of Muslim political workers and speakers | Muslims lost jobs, schooling and savings |
| Showed Muslims that they could not rely on Congress or on events abroad; they needed their own party and programme | The League was sidelined for some years |

## 8. Towards a new constitution, 1927–1929

### Delhi Muslim Proposals, 20 March 1927

Jinnah and other Muslim leaders offered to **give up separate electorates** if:

1. Sindh was separated from Bombay.
2. Reforms were introduced in NWFP and Balochistan on the same footing as other provinces.
3. Muslims got one-third of the seats in the central legislature.
4. Seats in Punjab and Bengal matched population.

Congress first accepted, then drew back under Hindu Mahasabha pressure.

### Simon Commission, 1927–1930

- Seven British MPs under Sir John Simon; **no Indian member**. Appointed November 1927; arrived February 1928.
- Congress boycotted it. The League split: the **Jinnah group** boycotted; the **Shafi group** (Sir Muhammad Shafi, Lahore) cooperated.
- Report (1930) recommended ending dyarchy and full provincial self-government. It was overtaken by the Round Table Conferences.

### Nehru Report, August 1928

Lord Birkenhead, Secretary of State, had challenged Indians to produce an agreed constitution. An All-Parties Conference set up a committee under **Motilal Nehru** (secretary: Jawaharlal Nehru; Muslim members Sir Ali Imam and Shuaib Qureshi).

| Recommendation | Muslim objection |
|---|---|
| Dominion status; responsible government | — |
| **Joint electorates**; reserved seats for Muslims only where they were a minority | Ended separate electorates; no reserved seats in Punjab and Bengal, where the Muslim majority was thin and economically weak |
| Muslims to have one-quarter of central seats | They had asked for one-third |
| No weightage | Broke the Lucknow Pact |
| Strong centre; residuary powers with the centre | Muslims wanted a loose federation with strong provinces |
| Sindh to be separated only if financially viable; NWFP to get full provincial status | Conditional and uncertain |
| "Hindustani" as the official language, in Devanagari or Urdu script (many textbooks report this simply as "Hindi") | Muslims feared it would mean Hindi in practice and a threat to Urdu |

At the **All-Parties Convention, Calcutta (December 1928)** Jinnah moved amendments: one-third of central seats, residuary powers to the provinces, seats by population in Punjab and Bengal for ten years. They were rejected. He is reported to have called this "the parting of the ways".

### Jinnah's Fourteen Points, March 1929

Presented at the League Council in Delhi (28 March 1929). They reunited the League's factions and became its minimum demands for the next decade.

1. Federal constitution; residuary powers with the provinces.
2. Uniform provincial autonomy.
3. Effective minority representation in every legislature, without reducing a majority to a minority or to equality.
4. One-third Muslim representation in the central legislature.
5. Separate electorates, with the option to give them up later.
6. No territorial redistribution that affects the Muslim majority in Punjab, Bengal and NWFP.
7. Full religious liberty for all communities.
8. No bill to pass if three-fourths of a community's members oppose it as harmful to that community.
9. Separation of Sindh from Bombay.
10. Reforms in NWFP and Balochistan.
11. Adequate Muslim share in the services.
12. Safeguards for Muslim culture, education, language, religion, personal law and charitable institutions.
13. At least one-third Muslim ministers in central and provincial cabinets.
14. No change to the constitution without the agreement of the federating units.

**Easy way to remember:** federal structure (1, 2, 14); representation (3, 4, 5, 8, 13); territory (6, 9, 10); rights (7, 11, 12).

**Importance**

- Most of the points found their way into the Communal Award (1932) and the 1935 Act: separate electorates, Sindh's separation, NWFP reforms, provincial autonomy.
- Congress rejected them; Jawaharlal Nehru called them ridiculous. The gap between the two parties was now open.
- They are the last major statement of the "safeguards within India" approach. The next step, in 1930, was the idea of a separate territory.
