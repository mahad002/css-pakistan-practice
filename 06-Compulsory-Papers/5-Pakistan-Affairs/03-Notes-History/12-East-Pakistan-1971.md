# 12. The Separation of East Pakistan, 1971

Asked in the 2023 special paper (ethnic exclusion and state failure) and in 2025 (causes, effects, regional and global actors). A sensitive subject. Write it factually, give the main views, and focus on causes and lessons.

## 1. The setting

- Two wings separated by about 1,600 km of Indian territory.
- East Pakistan had about **55% of the population** on about one-seventh of the land.
- The wings shared religion and the memory of 1947. They differed in language, script, dress, diet, land system (East Bengal had abolished large estates in 1950) and political culture. The East had a politically active middle class and a significant Hindu minority.

## 2. Long-term causes

### Political

| Year | Grievance |
|---|---|
| 1948–52 | **Language:** Urdu declared the only state language; police firing on 21 February 1952 (now International Mother Language Day) |
| 1947–56 | Under-representation in the central cabinet, civil service and army; the capital and headquarters all in the West |
| 1954 | The **United Front** government, elected by a landslide on a 21-point autonomy programme, dismissed within weeks |
| 1955–56 | **Parity** and **One Unit**: a 55% majority reduced to 50% |
| 1958–69 | Military rule from a West Pakistani army; the Basic Democracies system gave the Bengali middle class no voice |
| 1965 | The war with India left the East almost undefended; the doctrine that "the defence of the East lies in the West" was discredited |
| 1968 | **Agartala Conspiracy Case** against Sheikh Mujibur Rahman made him a hero; withdrawn in February 1969 |

### Economic

- In the 1950s East Pakistan's jute earned the larger share of the country's foreign exchange, while most imports and development spending went to the West.
- The gap in income per head between the wings widened through the 1960s; the government's own planners acknowledged it. A commonly cited estimate is that the West's lead grew from about 30% in 1959–60 to about 60% in 1969–70.
- Industry, banking and insurance were owned largely by West Pakistan-based groups.
- Bengali economists (Rehman Sobhan and others) developed the "**two economies**" thesis, which became the base of the Six Points.

### Administrative and military

- Bengalis held a small share of senior civil posts and roughly 5–7% of army officer posts in the 1960s.
- Senior posts in the East were often held by West Pakistani officers who did not speak Bengali.

### Cultural

- Bengali language and literature were treated with suspicion as "Hindu-influenced"; Tagore's songs were restricted on state radio in 1967.

### The Six Points (Lahore, February 1966)

1. A federal, parliamentary constitution with direct elections, in the spirit of the Lahore Resolution.
2. The federal government to deal only with **defence and foreign affairs**.
3. Two separate, freely convertible **currencies**, or one currency with constitutional barriers against the flight of capital from East to West.
4. **Taxation** to belong to the federating units; the centre to receive a fixed share.
5. Separate **foreign exchange accounts** for each wing; units free to make trade agreements.
6. A separate **militia or paramilitary force** for East Pakistan.

West Pakistani leaders saw the points as a formula for confederation or secession. For Bengalis they were a charter of survival.

## 3. Immediate causes, 1970–71

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| 12 November 1970 | **Bhola cyclone** killed several hundred thousand people in the East. The central government's response was seen as slow and indifferent |
| **7 December 1970** | **General election.** National Assembly: 300 general seats. **Awami League 160** (all but two of the East's 162). **PPP 81** of the West's 138. Neither won a seat in the other wing |
| December 1970 – February 1971 | Mujib insisted on a constitution based on the Six Points. **Bhutto** argued that a majority in one wing could not dictate to the federation and refused to attend an Assembly session in Dhaka without a prior understanding |
| **1 March 1971** | **Yahya postponed the Assembly session** due on 3 March. Strikes and civil disobedience in the East; the Awami League ran the province in practice |
| 7 March | Mujib's speech at the Race Course: a call to struggle, stopping just short of declaring independence |
| 16–24 March | Yahya–Mujib–Bhutto talks in Dhaka failed |
| **25–26 March** | **Operation Searchlight:** the army moved against the Awami League, the university and Bengali units. Mujib was arrested. Independence was proclaimed on 26 March |
| April–November | Civil war. A government-in-exile at Calcutta; the **Mukti Bahini** trained and armed by India; millions of refugees crossed into India (India cited about 10 million) |
| **9 August 1971** | **Indo-Soviet Treaty of Peace, Friendship and Cooperation** |
| 21–22 November | Indian forces began operations inside East Pakistan's borders in support of the Mukti Bahini |
| **3 December** | Pakistan launched air strikes in the west; full-scale war |
| 4–15 December | The UN Security Council was deadlocked by **Soviet vetoes** of ceasefire resolutions. On 15 December Bhutto walked out of the Council |
| **16 December 1971** | Lt-Gen A.A.K. Niazi surrendered at Dhaka to Lt-Gen Jagjit Singh Aurora. About **93,000** prisoners (soldiers, paramilitaries and civilians) were taken to India |
| 20 December | Yahya resigned; Bhutto took over |

## 4. Who was responsible? The main views

| Factor | Argument | Associated with |
|---|---|---|
| The **military regime** | Refused to hand power to the elected majority; chose force; misjudged India and the world | Hamoodur Rahman Commission; Hasan Zaheer; most Pakistani and foreign scholars |
| **Z.A. Bhutto** | His refusal to sit in opposition and his boycott of the Dhaka session gave Yahya the excuse to postpone | G.W. Choudhury; many West and East Pakistani critics. His defenders reply that the Six Points left no federation to govern and that the decision to use force was the army's |
| **Sheikh Mujib and the Awami League** | Inflexibility on the Six Points; contacts with India | The official Pakistani view of the time |
| **Two decades of policy** | Denial of language, majority and economic rights made a breach likely whoever ruled in 1971 | Rounaq Jahan (*Pakistan: Failure in National Integration*) |
| **India** | Saw a chance to cut Pakistan in two; armed the rebels; invaded | Pakistani official view; documented by Indian officers' memoirs; Sisson and Rose (*War and Secession*) show Indian planning from April 1971 |
| **Great powers** | The USSR armed and shielded India; the USA "tilted" towards Pakistan in words but imposed an arms embargo; China gave diplomatic support only | Sisson and Rose; Gary Bass (*The Blood Telegram*) |

**The Hamoodur Rahman Commission** (appointed December 1971; main report 1972, supplement 1974; largely declassified in 2000) blamed the senior military command for political misjudgement, professional failure and moral lapses, recommended public trials of named generals, and also criticised political leaders. No trial was held.

**On casualties:** figures are contested. Bangladesh's official figure is three million dead. The Hamoodur Rahman Commission accepted about 26,000. Independent estimates mostly fall between. Violence was also committed against Urdu-speaking "Biharis". In an exam, say the numbers are disputed and that grave excesses occurred, and move on to causes and lessons.

## 5. The role of outside powers

| Actor | Role |
|---|---|
| **India** | Gave sanctuary to the exiled government; trained and equipped the Mukti Bahini; used the refugee burden to win world sympathy; secured Soviet backing; invaded with overwhelming local superiority. Indira Gandhi afterwards spoke of having avenged a thousand years of history [commonly quoted; verify wording before using] |
| **USSR** | Treaty of August 1971; arms; three vetoes in the Security Council |
| **USA** | Nixon and Kissinger valued Pakistan as their channel to China (Kissinger's secret trip, July 1971). They sent a naval task force led by **USS Enterprise** into the Bay of Bengal as a signal. No military help was given; arms supplies had been suspended |
| **China** | Verbal and diplomatic support; did not open a front, with Soviet forces on its northern border and winter in the passes |
| **United Nations** | Paralysed by the veto; the General Assembly voted 104–11 for a ceasefire on 7 December, without effect |
| **Muslim countries** | Diplomatic sympathy (Jordan, Iran, Saudi Arabia, Libya) and some material help; no decisive effect |

## 6. Consequences

**For Pakistan**

- Lost more than half its population, its eastern market and its main early export crop.
- A deep psychological shock; the army's prestige fell for a time.
- A new start: civilian government, the 1973 Constitution with a Senate and stronger provinces, the nuclear decision of 1972, a foreign policy turned towards the Muslim world and China.
- The **Simla Agreement** (1972); the prisoners returned by 1974; Pakistan recognised Bangladesh in February 1974.

**For the region**

- India became the dominant power of South Asia.
- Bangladesh was born, and in 1975 its founder was killed in a coup; its relations with India have not always been easy.

**For the ideology debate**

- Critics said the Two-Nation Theory had failed. The reply: Bangladesh did not merge with India; it remained a separate Muslim-majority state. What failed was the federation.

## 7. Lessons (the part examiners want)

1. **Accept election results.** Denying the majority its right to govern broke the country.
2. **Federalism must be real.** Provinces need a guaranteed share of power and resources (hence the Senate, CCI, NFC, and the 18th Amendment).
3. **Economic justice between regions** is a security issue, not only a development issue.
4. **Respect language and culture.** Unity cannot be imposed by uniformity.
5. **Political problems need political solutions.** Force turned a demand for autonomy into a war of independence.
6. **Inclusive institutions.** The army, civil service and business must draw from every region.
7. **External powers exploit internal divisions.** A hostile neighbour can only intervene where grievances are real.
8. **Accountability.** Burying the Hamoodur Rahman report meant the lessons were not learned in public.

**Relevance today:** Balochistan and Khyber Pakhtunkhwa. The questions of 2023 (special) and 2024 asked you to apply 1971 to present-day ethnic exclusion. Draw the parallel carefully: the lesson is about inclusion, resource shares and political dialogue.

## 8. Pakistan–Bangladesh relations since

- Recognition 1974; SAARC partners from 1985.
- Long chill under Sheikh Hasina (2009–24), especially over the war-crimes trials.
- After Hasina's fall on **5 August 2024**, a rapid thaw: direct sea trade resumed, foreign-secretary talks in Dhaka (April 2025), and a visit by Deputy Prime Minister Ishaq Dar (August 2025).
