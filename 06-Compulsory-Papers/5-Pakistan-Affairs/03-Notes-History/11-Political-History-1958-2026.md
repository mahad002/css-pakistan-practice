# 11. Political History by Era, 1958–2026

One section per government: key events, policies, and a balanced assessment. For 1947–58 see file 09. For 1971 see file 12. For the amendments see file 10. Recent events (2025–26) are checked to 23 Nov 2026 and sourced in the contemporary files.

Asked: Simla Agreement (2020), elections 2002–2013 and democracy (2017), Bhutto's nationalisation (2025), political system and culture (2018, 2020), why democracy has not taken root (2023).

## Overview

| Period | Type of rule | Head |
|---|---|---|
| 1947–58 | Parliamentary, unstable | 4 governors-general/president, 7 prime ministers |
| 1958–69 | Military, then controlled presidential | Ayub Khan |
| 1969–71 | Military | Yahya Khan |
| 1971–77 | Civilian (elected) | Z.A. Bhutto |
| 1977–88 | Military, then mixed | Zia-ul-Haq |
| 1988–99 | Civilian, four governments dismissed or removed | Benazir Bhutto, Nawaz Sharif (twice each) |
| 1999–2008 | Military, then mixed | Pervez Musharraf |
| 2008–present | Civilian, with three full parliamentary terms (2008–13, 2013–18, 2018–23) but no prime minister completing five years | PPP, PML-N, PTI, PDM/PML-N coalition |

**Four periods of direct military rule:** 1958–62 (Ayub), 1969–71 (Yahya), 1977–85 (Zia), 1999–2002 (Musharraf). About 33 of the country's first 61 years were under generals.

## 1. Ayub Khan, 1958–1969

| Area | Events |
|---|---|
| Political system | **Basic Democracies Order 1959:** 80,000 elected local councillors in four tiers, who also formed the electoral college. Confidence vote by them in February 1960. Politicians barred under **EBDO (1959)**. **1962 Constitution** (presidential). Political parties allowed again from July 1962; Ayub led the Convention Muslim League |
| Social and legal | **Muslim Family Laws Ordinance 1961** (registration of marriage, limits on polygamy, arbitration councils). Press brought under control (Press and Publications Ordinance; National Press Trust 1964) |
| Land | **Land reforms 1959:** ceiling of 500 irrigated or 1,000 unirrigated acres. Little land actually changed hands |
| Economy | "**Decade of Development**": growth near 6–7% a year in the Second Plan (1960–65); industrialisation through PIDC; Green Revolution (tube wells, new seeds, fertiliser); Mangla Dam (1967), Tarbela begun. **Indus Waters Treaty 1960**. Inequality grew: Dr Mahbub ul Haq said in 1968 that 22 families controlled most industry and banking |
| Capital | Moved from Karachi; Islamabad built in the 1960s |
| Foreign policy | Alliance with the USA; opening to **China** (boundary agreement 2 March 1963; air link); RCD with Iran and Turkey (1964) |
| **1965 election** | 2 January 1965. Ayub defeated **Fatima Jinnah**, candidate of the Combined Opposition Parties, in the Basic Democrats' college. She won Dhaka and Karachi. The result was disputed |
| **1965 war** | Rann of Kutch clash (April); **Operation Gibraltar** in Kashmir (August); India crossed the international border at Lahore on **6 September**; ceasefire **23 September**; **Tashkent Declaration 10 January 1966** restored the pre-war lines without touching Kashmir. Public disappointment; Foreign Minister **Z.A. Bhutto** left and founded the **PPP (30 November 1967)** |
| East Pakistan | **Six Points** (February 1966); **Agartala Conspiracy Case** (1968) |
| Fall | Student and labour protests from November 1968 in both wings; round-table talks failed. Ayub **resigned on 25 March 1969** and, contrary to his own constitution, handed power to the army chief |

**Assessment.** *Credit:* stability, growth, the water treaty, infrastructure, family law reform. *Debit:* a controlled political system with no room for dissent; growth concentrated in a few hands and in West Pakistan; East Pakistan's alienation; the 1965 war's cost; and the precedent of military rule.

## 2. Yahya Khan, 1969–1971

- Dissolved **One Unit** (1 July 1970); restored the four provinces; made Balochistan a full province.
- **Legal Framework Order (March 1970):** one person, one vote.
- Held the **first general election on adult franchise, 7 December 1970** — widely regarded as fair.
- Failed to transfer power; military action in East Pakistan; war with India; surrender at Dhaka **16 December 1971**. Handed over to Bhutto on **20 December 1971**.

Full account in file 12.

## 3. Zulfikar Ali Bhutto, 1971–1977

President and civilian Chief Martial Law Administrator from 20 December 1971; Prime Minister from 14 August 1973.

| Area | Events |
|---|---|
| After the war | **Simla Agreement, 2 July 1972** (below). 93,000 prisoners returned by 1974. Recognised Bangladesh at the **Islamic Summit, Lahore, 22–24 February 1974** |
| Constitution | Interim Constitution (April 1972); **1973 Constitution** by consensus |
| **Nationalisation** | January 1972: 31 large units in ten basic industries (steel, heavy engineering, chemicals, cement, automobiles, utilities). Life insurance (1972). Vegetable oil (1973). **Banks (1 January 1974)**. Cotton-ginning, rice-husking and flour mills (1976). Private schools and colleges (1972) |
| Labour and land | Labour reforms (1972): job security, profit-sharing, unions. **Land reforms 1972** (150 irrigated / 300 unirrigated acres) and 1977 |
| Other policies | Devaluation of the rupee (May 1972); passports made easy, leading to Gulf migration and remittances; Pakistan Steel Mills (with Soviet help); Port Qasim; Quaid-i-Azam University; **nuclear programme** begun (Multan meeting, January 1972) |
| Foreign policy | Left the Commonwealth (1972) and SEATO; closer to China, the Arab states and the Third World; "bilateralism" |
| Religion | **2nd Amendment (1974)** on Ahmadis; Friday holiday and prohibition (1977) |
| Provinces | Dismissed the NAP–JUI government in **Balochistan (February 1973)**; the NWFP government resigned; army operation in Balochistan 1973–77; NAP banned (1975) |
| Authoritarian side | Federal Security Force; arrests of opponents; curbs on the press; seven amendments in four years, several limiting courts |
| Fall | **Election 7 March 1977:** PPP won 155 of 200 seats; the nine-party **Pakistan National Alliance (PNA)** alleged rigging and launched a protest movement. Talks came close to agreement. **General Zia-ul-Haq took over on 5 July 1977** |

### The Simla Agreement (2020 question)

Signed at Simla on **2 July 1972** by Bhutto and Indira Gandhi.

1. Both countries would settle differences by **peaceful means through bilateral negotiations** or any other means mutually agreed.
2. Relations to be governed by the UN Charter.
3. Forces to withdraw to their side of the international border.
4. In Jammu and Kashmir the ceasefire line of 17 December 1971 became the **Line of Control**, to be respected by both sides "without prejudice to the recognised position of either side".
5. Steps to normalise relations: communications, trade, travel.
6. A further meeting to discuss prisoners, Kashmir and diplomatic ties.

**For Pakistan:** recovered about 5,000 square miles of territory; opened the way for the prisoners' return; no war-crimes trials; Kashmir kept open on paper. **For India:** bilateralism — which it reads as excluding the UN and third parties. **Did it bring lasting peace?** It prevented a full-scale war for decades, but Siachen (1984), Kargil (1999), the crises of 2001–02, 2019 and May 2025 show that it managed the conflict without settling it. In **April 2025** Pakistan said it would hold the agreement "in abeyance" after India suspended the Indus Waters Treaty [verify current status].

### Nationalisation assessed (2025 question)

| Aims | Results |
|---|---|
| Break the "22 families"; give the state the commanding heights; protect workers | Private investment fell sharply and capital left; state firms were overstaffed and loss-making; the 1976 takeover of small agro-processing units hurt the middle class that had supported the PPP; bank credit became political |
| | Some heavy industry was built (steel, fertiliser, cement); workers gained rights and a sense of dignity; the state sector became a long-term fiscal burden, which later governments tried to privatise from 1991 |

External shocks also mattered: loss of East Pakistan's market, the 1973 oil price rise, floods in 1973 and 1976. A fair judgement: socially popular, economically costly, and still felt in the losses of state-owned enterprises.

**Assessment of Bhutto.** *Credit:* rebuilt the state after defeat; the constitution; the nuclear programme; foreign policy; political awakening of the poor. *Debit:* intolerance of opposition; Balochistan; economic damage from nationalisation; the disputed 1977 election.

## 4. Zia-ul-Haq, 1977–1988

| Area | Events |
|---|---|
| Legal cover | Elections promised in 90 days, then postponed. ***Nusrat Bhutto case* (1977):** Supreme Court validated the takeover under the doctrine of necessity. **Bhutto was hanged on 4 April 1979** after a 4–3 Supreme Court verdict in a murder case. (In March 2024 the Supreme Court, answering a presidential reference, said the trial did not meet the requirements of a fair trial.) Parties banned (1979); Provisional Constitution Order (1981) |
| **Islamisation** | **Hudood Ordinances (1979)**; zakat and ushr (1980); **Federal Shariat Court (1980)**; nominated Majlis-i-Shura (1981); Law of Evidence (1984); blasphemy provisions (295-B in 1982, 295-C in 1986); interest-free banking counters; madrassa growth |
| Afghanistan | Front-line state against the Soviet occupation from December 1979; US aid packages of $3.2 billion (1981) and about $4 billion (1987); over 3 million refugees; ISI managed the resistance |
| Opposition | **Movement for the Restoration of Democracy (MRD)**, 1981; strong in Sindh in 1983 |
| Return to civilian form | **Referendum, 19 December 1984**; **non-party elections, February 1985**; **Muhammad Khan Junejo** Prime Minister; **8th Amendment**; martial law lifted 30 December 1985. Benazir Bhutto returned in April 1986 |
| End | Junejo signed the **Geneva Accords (14 April 1988)** against Zia's wishes and ordered an inquiry into the **Ojhri Camp** explosion. Zia dismissed him on **29 May 1988**. Zia died in an air crash near Bahawalpur on **17 August 1988** |

**Assessment.** *Supporters:* held the country together, checked the Soviet advance, advanced the nuclear programme, gave Islamic provisions legal form. *Critics:* longest martial law; execution of an elected prime minister; laws that disadvantaged women and minorities; sectarian and ethnic militancy; weapons and drugs from the Afghan war; non-party politics that strengthened biradari and money; loss of Siachen (1984).

## 5. The democratic decade, 1988–1999

| Government | Dates | How it ended | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Benazir Bhutto (PPP)** I | Dec 1988 – Aug 1990 | Dismissed by President Ghulam Ishaq Khan under 58(2)(b) | First woman prime minister of a Muslim country; weak majority; conflict with Punjab (Nawaz Sharif) and the President; rejoined the Commonwealth |
| **Nawaz Sharif (IJI/PML)** I | Nov 1990 – Jul 1993 | Dismissed April 1993; **restored by the Supreme Court (May 1993)**; both he and the President resigned in July under an army-brokered formula | Privatisation and deregulation; motorway begun; US sanctions under the **Pressler Amendment (1990)**; Karachi operation (1992) |
| **Benazir Bhutto (PPP)** II | Oct 1993 – Nov 1996 | Dismissed by President Farooq Leghari (her own nominee) | Independent power producers policy (1994); Taliban emerged in Afghanistan; Karachi violence; her brother Murtaza killed (September 1996) |
| **Nawaz Sharif (PML-N)** II | Feb 1997 – Oct 1999 | Removed by the army, **12 October 1999** | Two-thirds majority; **13th and 14th Amendments**; clash with the Chief Justice (Supreme Court stormed, November 1997); **nuclear tests 28 and 30 May 1998**; **Lahore Declaration (21 February 1999)**; **Kargil (May–July 1999)**; tried to replace the army chief |

**Why the decade failed:** a president armed with 58(2)(b); the army's continuing say in security and foreign policy; zero-sum rivalry between the two parties, each welcoming the other's dismissal; corruption charges used as a weapon; a weak economy under sanctions and IMF programmes; ethnic and sectarian violence. **What it left:** the lesson behind the **Charter of Democracy (London, 14 May 2006)**, in which Benazir Bhutto and Nawaz Sharif agreed not to invite military intervention against each other.

## 6. Pervez Musharraf, 1999–2008

| Area | Events |
|---|---|
| Legal cover | ***Zafar Ali Shah case* (2000):** Supreme Court validated the coup and gave three years. President from June 2001. **Referendum, 30 April 2002.** **Legal Framework Order (2002).** Elections 10 October 2002; PML-Q government; **17th Amendment** |
| Prime ministers | Zafarullah Jamali (2002–04), Chaudhry Shujaat (2004), Shaukat Aziz (2004–07) |
| Reforms | **Devolution Plan (2001):** elected district nazims; more seats for women (17% in assemblies, 33% in local bodies); joint electorates restored for minorities; **private TV channels** (PEMRA, 2002); Higher Education Commission (2002); NAB (1999); Women's Protection Act (2006) |
| Economy | Debt rescheduling and aid after 9/11; growth of 6–7% in 2004–07; banking and telecom expansion; energy investment neglected |
| Foreign policy | Joined the US war on terror (September 2001); **Agra Summit (July 2001)**; 2001–02 stand-off with India; **composite dialogue from 2004** and a back channel on Kashmir |
| Crises | **Earthquake, 8 October 2005** (about 73,000 dead); **Nawab Akbar Bugti killed (26 August 2006)** — fifth Baloch insurgency; **Chief Justice Iftikhar Chaudhry suspended (9 March 2007)** → Lawyers' Movement; **Lal Masjid operation (July 2007)** → wave of suicide bombings and the TTP (December 2007) |
| End | NRO (5 October 2007); **emergency (3 November 2007)**; left the army (28 November); **Benazir Bhutto assassinated (27 December 2007)**; elections **18 February 2008**; resigned under threat of impeachment **18 August 2008** |

**Assessment.** *Credit:* growth, media opening, local government, women's representation, peace process with India. *Debit:* another break in constitutional rule; the costs of the war on terror; Balochistan; the 2007 assault on the judiciary; an energy crisis left for his successors.

## 7. PPP government, 2008–2013

- Prime Ministers **Yusuf Raza Gilani** (2008–12; removed by the Supreme Court for contempt, June 2012) and **Raja Pervaiz Ashraf**. President **Asif Ali Zardari** (September 2008).
- Judges restored after the **Long March of March 2009**.
- **7th NFC Award (December 2009):** provincial share raised to 57.5%; multiple criteria.
- **18th Amendment (2010)**; 19th and 20th.
- Aghaz-i-Huqooq-i-Balochistan package; Gilgit-Baltistan Empowerment Order (2009); **Benazir Income Support Programme (2008)**.
- Military operations in Swat (2009) and South Waziristan.
- 2011, a year of crises with the USA: Raymond Davis (January), **Abbottabad raid (2 May)**, Memogate, **Salala attack (26 November)**.
- Floods of 2010 (about 20 million affected); severe power shortages.
- **First elected government to complete its term and hand over to another through an election (2013).**

## 8. PML-N government, 2013–2018

- Election **11 May 2013**. Prime Ministers **Nawaz Sharif** (to 28 July 2017) and **Shahid Khaqan Abbasi**.
- PTI's sit-in in Islamabad over alleged rigging (August–December 2014).
- **Operation Zarb-i-Azb (June 2014)**; **Army Public School attack (16 December 2014)**; **National Action Plan** and **21st Amendment** (military courts); Karachi operation. Terrorism fell sharply by 2017.
- **CPEC** launched (April 2015); power generation added; motorways.
- Sixth census (2017): 207.7 million.
- **Panama Papers (2016)** → Supreme Court **disqualified Nawaz Sharif on 28 July 2017** under Art. 62(1)(f).
- **25th Amendment (2018):** FATA merged.
- Second consecutive completed parliament.

## 9. PTI government, 2018–2022

- Election **25 July 2018**; **Imran Khan** Prime Minister (18 August 2018). Opposition alleged manipulation.
- Economy: IMF programme (2019); Ehsaas welfare programme; Sehat health card; COVID-19 handled with "smart lockdowns"; inflation rose in 2021–22.
- Foreign policy: Pulwama–Balakot crisis (February 2019); India's action in Kashmir (5 August 2019); Kartarpur Corridor (November 2019); facilitated the US–Taliban Doha deal (2020); Taliban returned to Kabul (August 2021).
- FATF grey list (June 2018 – October 2022).
- **Removed by a vote of no confidence on 9–10 April 2022** — the first prime minister to lose office this way — after the Supreme Court set aside the Deputy Speaker's ruling and the dissolution of the Assembly.

**The 2002, 2008 and 2013 elections and democracy (2017 question):** 2002 restored assemblies and brought in women and new parties, but under a general. 2008 ended military rule and produced a coalition that passed the 18th Amendment. 2013 gave the first peaceful transfer between elected governments. Together they showed a gradual consolidation — which the disputes of 2018 and 2024 have since put in doubt.

## 12. Recurring patterns (use in "why has democracy not taken root?")

1. Early dominance of the bureaucracy and army over weak parties.
2. Judicial validation of takeovers (1955, 1958, 1977, 2000), with the exceptions of 1972 and 2009.
3. Constitutional devices to remove governments: 58(2)(b), disqualification, contempt.
4. Parties built around families and personalities, with little internal democracy.
5. Feudal and biradari influence; money in elections.
6. Disputed elections and refusal of losers to accept results.
7. A security-driven state with India and Afghanistan as constant concerns.
8. Economic dependence on aid, which rewards strategic usefulness over reform.
9. Weak local government, restored and abolished by turns.
10. The centre–province tension that began in 1947.
