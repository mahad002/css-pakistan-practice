# 9. Early Problems and the First Decade, 1947–1958

Covers the problems at birth, the delay in constitution-making, factional politics, and the Western defence pacts. Asked: factional politics 1947–58 (2022); defence pacts and their cost (2022). The early problems themselves are a classic question and a steady source of MCQs.

---

## A. Problems at independence

Pakistan began with no capital, no central bank, no ordnance factory, few officers, two wings 1,600 km apart, and a neighbour whose leaders expected it to fail.

### 1. Refugees

- Partition caused one of the largest migrations in history: about 12–15 million people crossed the new borders.
- By the 1951 census about **7 million** refugees had settled in Pakistan; about 5 million Hindus and Sikhs had left. Estimates of the dead range from 200,000 to over a million.
- The state had to feed, house and employ them. Jinnah moved his headquarters to Lahore for a time and set up the **Quaid-i-Azam Relief Fund**.
- Long-term effects: Karachi and urban Sindh changed in character; evacuee property became a source of disputes and corruption; refugees dominated the early bureaucracy and politics.

### 2. Division of assets

| Asset | What was agreed | What happened |
|---|---|---|
| Cash balances (about Rs 4 billion) | Pakistan's share **Rs 750 million** | Rs 200 million paid at once. India held back the rest when the Kashmir fighting began. After **Gandhi's fast in January 1948**, Rs 500 million was released. **Rs 50 million was never paid** |
| Military stores | About one-third to Pakistan | Only a small part arrived, much of it unusable. All **16 ordnance factories** were in India; Pakistan was given a cash sum instead and built its own at Wah |
| Armed forces | Divided roughly 64:36 | The Supreme Commander's office under Auchinleck was closed early (November 1947), before the division was complete |

### 3. Administrative problems

- **Karachi** became the capital with offices in barracks and tents.
- Of roughly 1,100 officers of the Indian Civil Service in 1947, about one hundred were Muslims, and fewer than that came to Pakistan. British officers were kept on contract.
- Files, typewriters and furniture were lacking. The state worked largely because of the commitment of its early officials.

### 4. Economic problems

- The areas that became Pakistan were agricultural. East Bengal grew most of the world's jute but the mills were in Calcutta. West Pakistan grew cotton but had few textile mills.
- Banks and businesses run by Hindus and Sikhs closed as their owners left.
- The **State Bank of Pakistan** was opened by Jinnah on **1 July 1948**.
- In 1949 Pakistan refused to devalue its rupee along with sterling and India; India stopped trade. Pakistan found new markets and was helped by the Korean War boom (1950–51).

### 5. Canal water dispute

- The Radcliffe line left the headworks at **Madhopur (Ravi)** and **Ferozepur (Sutlej)** in India, and the canals they fed in Pakistan.
- On **1 April 1948** East Punjab cut off the water. The **Inter-Dominion Agreement of 4 May 1948** restored it in return for payment, without settling rights.
- The World Bank mediated from 1951. The result was the **Indus Waters Treaty, 1960** (see `04-Notes-Contemporary/12-Hydro-Politics-and-Water.md`).

### 6. Princely states

The 3 June Plan left some 560 states free to choose, guided by geography and their people's wishes.

| State | Position | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| **Junagadh** | Muslim ruler, Hindu majority, on the coast of Kathiawar | Acceded to Pakistan (September 1947). India sent troops (November 1947) and held a plebiscite (February 1948) that went for India |
| **Hyderabad (Deccan)** | Muslim Nizam, Hindu majority, landlocked | Sought independence. India took it by "police action" (Operation Polo), 13–17 September 1948 |
| **Jammu and Kashmir** | Hindu maharaja, Muslim majority (about 77%), bordering both | Rising in Poonch; tribal entry on 22 October 1947; the Maharaja signed accession to India (26 October); Indian troops landed at Srinagar (27 October); war; India went to the UN (1 January 1948); ceasefire 1 January 1949. Still disputed |
| Bahawalpur, Khairpur, Chitral, Dir, Swat, Amb, the Balochistan states | — | Acceded to Pakistan in 1947–48. **Kalat** acceded on 27 March 1948 |

Pakistan's argument: India applied the people's-wish principle in Junagadh and Hyderabad and the ruler's-signature principle in Kashmir, taking all three.

### 7. Defence and the first Kashmir war

- An army with few senior officers, short of stores, under a British commander (General Gracey) who at first declined to send troops to Kashmir.
- Regular troops went in from May 1948. The ceasefire line of 1949 left about one-third of the state with Pakistan (Azad Kashmir and the Northern Areas).

### 8. Constitutional and political problems

- The 1935 Act served as the interim constitution. The Constituent Assembly had to write a new one.
- **Language:** in February 1948 a motion in the Assembly to allow Bengali was rejected. In **Dhaka on 21 March 1948** Jinnah said Urdu would be the state language. Protests grew; police fired on students on **21 February 1952**. Bengali was accepted as a state language in 1954 and in the 1956 Constitution.
- **Pakhtunistan:** Afghanistan voted against Pakistan's admission to the UN (30 September 1947) and questioned the Durand Line.
- **Loss of leaders:** **Jinnah died on 11 September 1948**. **Liaquat Ali Khan was assassinated on 16 October 1951** at Rawalpindi.

### How the problems were handled

- Jinnah as Governor-General gave the state authority and direction for its first thirteen months.
- Liaquat's government produced the Objectives Resolution (1949), the Liaquat–Nehru Pact on minorities (8 April 1950), and the first budgets.
- The civil service and army kept the state running. The cost was that these two institutions grew stronger than the political parties.

## B. Why constitution-making took nine years

India adopted its constitution in January 1950. Pakistan's came into force in March 1956. The reasons:

| Issue | The dispute |
|---|---|
| **Role of Islam** | Ulema wanted an Islamic state with a role for religious scholars; modernists wanted a democratic state informed by Islamic values. The Objectives Resolution (1949) was a compromise. The 1953 anti-Ahmadi riots in Punjab (first martial law, Lahore) and the Munir Report showed how divided opinion was |
| **Representation** | East Bengal had about 55% of the population. West Pakistani leaders feared permanent Bengali majority; Bengalis rejected anything less than their numbers |
| **Federalism** | How much power for the centre; Bengal wanted maximum autonomy |
| **Language** | Urdu alone, or Urdu and Bengali |
| **Electorates** | Separate (favoured in the West) or joint (favoured in the East) |
| **Leadership** | Deaths of Jinnah and Liaquat; the League had no roots in the provinces of Pakistan |
| **Bureaucratic intervention** | Governors-General dismissed a prime minister (1953) and the Assembly itself (1954) |

### Stages

| Date | Step |
|---|---|
| **12 March 1949** | **Objectives Resolution** passed (moved by Liaquat on 7 March). Non-Muslim members objected to its religious language; Liaquat called it the most important occasion in the country's life after independence |
| September 1950 | **Basic Principles Committee, interim report:** two houses with equal powers; Urdu as the national language. Rejected in East Bengal |
| December 1952 | **Second BPC report (Nazimuddin):** parity between the wings. Rejected in Punjab |
| October 1953 | **Bogra Formula** (Muhammad Ali Bogra): an upper house of 50 with equal seats for five units; a lower house of 300 by population (East Bengal 165); the two wings equal in joint sittings. Widely accepted |
| September 1954 | The Assembly amended the 1935 Act to stop the Governor-General dismissing ministers, and repealed PRODA |
| **24 October 1954** | Governor-General **Ghulam Muhammad dissolved the Constituent Assembly** and declared an emergency |
| 1955 | ***Maulvi Tamizuddin Khan case:*** the Sindh Chief Court ruled for the Assembly's president. The **Federal Court under Chief Justice Muhammad Munir** reversed this on a technical point (laws lacked the Governor-General's assent). In the Governor-General's Reference the court relied on the **doctrine of necessity** and told him to hold elections for a new assembly |
| June–July 1955 | **Second Constituent Assembly** (80 members) elected by the provincial assemblies |
| **14 October 1955** | **One Unit:** the provinces and states of West Pakistan merged into one province, to match East Pakistan and make **parity** workable |
| 29 February 1956 | Constitution adopted (Chaudhry Muhammad Ali as prime minister) |
| **23 March 1956** | Constitution in force. Pakistan became an **Islamic Republic**. Iskander Mirza became the first President |

## C. Politics 1947–1958: instability and factions

### Heads of state and government

| Governors-General / President | Prime Ministers |
|---|---|
| M.A. Jinnah (Aug 1947 – Sep 1948) | Liaquat Ali Khan (Aug 1947 – Oct 1951) |
| Khawaja Nazimuddin (1948–51) | Khawaja Nazimuddin (Oct 1951 – Apr 1953) — dismissed |
| Ghulam Muhammad (1951–55) | Muhammad Ali Bogra (Apr 1953 – Aug 1955) |
| Iskander Mirza (GG 1955–56; President 1956–58) | Chaudhry Muhammad Ali (Aug 1955 – Sep 1956) |
| | H.S. Suhrawardy (Sep 1956 – Oct 1957) |
| | I.I. Chundrigar (Oct – Dec 1957) |
| | Feroz Khan Noon (Dec 1957 – Oct 1958) |

Seven prime ministers in eleven years; four in the last two and a half.

### Features of the period (2022 question)

1. **Decline of the Muslim League.** It had led a movement in the minority provinces of India. In Pakistan it had no programme after independence, no elections within the party, and leaders without local seats.
2. **Factions in the provinces.** Punjab: Mamdot against Daultana. Sindh: Khuhro and his rivals. NWFP: Qayyum Khan against the Pir of Manki Sharif. East Bengal: the Nazimuddin group against Suhrawardy and Fazlul Haq.
3. **New parties from splits.** Awami Muslim League (1949; Bhashani, Suhrawardy; "Muslim" dropped later); Azad Pakistan Party; Krishak Sramik Party; **Republican Party** (1956), created almost overnight around Dr Khan Sahib with Mirza's backing; National Awami Party (1957).
4. **The East Bengal election of 1954.** The **United Front** (Jugto Front) of Fazlul Haq, Suhrawardy and Bhashani, on a **21-point programme** (autonomy, Bengali), won 223 of the 237 Muslim seats. The League won about ten. The centre dismissed the new ministry within two months under Section 92A.
5. **Rise of the bureaucrats.** Ghulam Muhammad (a finance official) and Iskander Mirza (a political-service officer) dismissed governments at will. General **Ayub Khan**, army chief since January 1951, became defence minister in the 1954 "cabinet of talents".
6. **No general election.** The first national election was planned for early 1959 and never held.
7. **Central interference in the provinces.** Ministries were dismissed in Punjab, Sindh, NWFP and East Bengal. **PRODA (1949)** allowed politicians to be disqualified.
8. **The judiciary's part.** The Tamizuddin judgment validated the dissolution of 1954.
9. **Rawalpindi Conspiracy Case (1951):** Major-General Akbar Khan and others, including the poet Faiz Ahmad Faiz, were convicted of plotting a coup.

### Effects on democracy

- Parliament lost authority before it had gained any.
- The public came to see politicians as self-seeking. This prepared opinion for military rule.
- East Pakistan concluded that its majority would never be allowed to rule.
- The pattern of dismissal, validation and engineered parties returned in later decades.

### The coup of October 1958

- Disorder in the East Pakistan Assembly (the Deputy Speaker died after a brawl in September 1958), the Khan of Kalat's alleged revolt, economic difficulty, and the approaching election.
- **7 October 1958:** President Mirza abrogated the constitution, dissolved the assemblies and declared martial law, with **General Ayub Khan** as Chief Martial Law Administrator.
- **27 October 1958:** Ayub removed Mirza and became President.
- ***State v. Dosso* (1958):** the Supreme Court under Chief Justice Munir held that a successful revolution is its own legal justification (Kelsen's theory).

**Why democracy failed in the first decade — views to cite:** Ayesha Jalal (*The State of Martial Rule*) stresses the early dominance of the bureaucracy and army, driven by the defence needs of a threatened state. Khalid bin Sayeed (*Pakistan: The Formative Phase*) points to the viceregal tradition. Others stress the League's weakness, the East–West divide and the personal ambitions of Ghulam Muhammad and Mirza.

## D. Foreign policy and the Western pacts, 1947–1960

### Early choices

- Pakistan joined the UN on 30 September 1947.
- Liaquat was invited to Moscow in 1949 but visited the **United States in May 1950**.
- Relations with the Muslim world were a stated priority but brought little material help.

### The pacts

| Date | Agreement |
|---|---|
| 19 May 1954 | **Mutual Defence Assistance Agreement** with the USA |
| September 1954 | **SEATO** (Manila Pact): USA, UK, France, Australia, New Zealand, Thailand, Philippines, Pakistan |
| September 1955 | **Baghdad Pact** with Iraq, Turkey, Iran and the UK; renamed **CENTO** in 1959 after Iraq left |
| 5 March 1959 | Bilateral Agreement of Cooperation with the USA |

Pakistan was called America's "most allied ally". The USA used the **Badaber** base near Peshawar; the U-2 spy plane shot down over the USSR in May 1960 had taken off from there.

### Why Pakistan joined (2022 question)

1. **Security against India:** a larger neighbour, the Kashmir war, unfinished partition disputes.
2. **Military weakness:** it needed modern arms it could not afford.
3. **Economic need:** aid for development and food.
4. **Afghanistan's hostility** on the north-west.
5. **Hope of support on Kashmir.**
6. **The outlook of the ruling group:** Western-trained officers and officials, wary of communism.
7. **India's non-alignment** left the USA looking for a partner in South Asia.

### What it gained

- Military and economic aid worth well over a billion dollars in the first decade; modern equipment and training for the forces.
- Budget support and technical help that fed the growth of the 1960s.

### What it cost

1. **Soviet hostility:** Moscow backed India on Kashmir (vetoes in the Security Council from 1957) and Afghanistan on Pakhtunistan.
2. **Distance from the Arab and non-aligned world,** made worse by Pakistan's stance in the **Suez crisis (1956)**.
3. **No help when it mattered:** the pacts were aimed at communism, not India. The USA armed India after the 1962 war with China, embargoed arms to both sides in **1965**, and gave no military help in **1971**.
4. **Dependence:** on foreign arms and aid, and on one patron's priorities.
5. **Internal effect:** aid strengthened the army relative to civilian institutions.
6. **Sovereignty:** a foreign base on Pakistani soil exposed the country to Soviet threats.

**Afterwards:** Pakistan turned to China (boundary agreement, 2 March 1963), left SEATO in 1972–73, and left CENTO in 1979.
