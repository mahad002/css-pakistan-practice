# Model Answers — Pakistan Affairs

**How to use them**

- Each runs to roughly 1,100–1,200 words. That is slightly more than most people can hand-write in the 35 minutes available per answer (about 1,000 words). If you run short of time, cut the weakest two headings, never the introduction or conclusion. To lengthen an answer, add evidence from the matching notes file, not adjectives.
- Write your own answer first under time, then compare.
- Contemporary facts are as of 23 November 2026. Update them before the exam.
- Quotations are limited to those that are safe (see `07-Personalities-and-Quotes.md`). Scholars' views are paraphrased.

## 1. Compare Allama Iqbal's philosophical vision with Quaid-i-Azam's political interpretation of the idea of Pakistan.

**Outline**
1. Introduction
2. The common ground: Muslims as a nation
3. Iqbal's vision: the philosophy
4. Jinnah's interpretation: the politics
5. Points of comparison
6. How the two were connected
7. Critical appraisal
8. Conclusion

**1. Introduction**

The ideology of Pakistan rests on a single claim: the Muslims of the subcontinent were a nation and were entitled to a state of their own. Two men gave that claim its form. Allama Muhammad Iqbal supplied the philosophical ground and the first territorial outline. Quaid-i-Azam Muhammad Ali Jinnah turned it into a constitutional demand, a mass movement and a state. Their starting points differed: Iqbal began with Islam as a living social order; Jinnah began with the rights of a political community. Their conclusions met. This answer argues that the two were complementary: Iqbal defined what the Muslim community was and should become, and Jinnah secured the political means for it.

**2. The common ground**

Both rejected the Congress view that India was one nation. Both held that safeguards for a "minority" inside a Hindu-majority centre would fail. Both saw the Muslim-majority provinces of the north-west and east as the base of Muslim political life. And both wanted the new polity to serve ordinary Muslims, not only its elite.

**3. Iqbal's vision: the philosophy**

*(a) Islam as a social order.* For Iqbal religion in Islam was not a private matter. Belief, law and community formed one whole. To confine Islam to private life, as European nationalism did with Christianity, would dissolve the Muslim community.

*(b) Rejection of territorial nationalism.* He held that nationalism based on land and race would divide the ummah and put country in the place of faith. In 1938 he publicly disputed Maulana Husain Ahmad Madani's view that nations are made by homelands.

*(c) Khudi.* His idea of selfhood applied to communities as much as to persons: a people that loses its distinct character ceases to live.

*(d) The Allahabad Address, 29 December 1930.* As president of the Muslim League session he proposed that Punjab, the North-West Frontier Province, Sind and Baluchistan be joined into a single state, and called a consolidated north-west Indian Muslim state the final destiny of the Muslims of that region, whether within the British Empire or outside it.

*(e) A reconstructed Islamic polity.* In *The Reconstruction of Religious Thought in Islam* he argued for ijtihad exercised through an elected Muslim assembly. The state he imagined was therefore neither a theocracy of clerics nor a copy of the secular West. He called its spirit "spiritual democracy".

*(f) Social justice.* His letters to Jinnah in 1937 stressed the poverty of the Muslim masses and argued that the law of Islam, properly applied, could secure a livelihood for all, but only in a free Muslim state or states.

**4. Jinnah's interpretation: the politics**

*(a) From safeguards to nationhood.* Jinnah spent three decades seeking constitutional safeguards: the Lucknow Pact (1916), the Delhi Proposals (1927), the Fourteen Points (1929). The Congress ministries of 1937–39 convinced him that paper guarantees were worthless.

*(b) The Lahore address, 22 March 1940.* He stated the case in the language of political science: Hindus and Muslims belonged to two different religious philosophies, social customs and literatures; they neither intermarried nor interdined; the problem of India was "not of an inter-communal character but manifestly of an international one". Muslims were "a nation according to any definition of a nation" and must have their homelands, territory and state.

*(c) Self-determination and mandate.* He rested the demand on the right of a nation to self-determination and proved it at the ballot box: the League won all 30 Muslim seats at the centre in 1945 and about 428 of 492 in the provinces in 1946.

*(d) The character of the state.* On 11 August 1947 he told the Constituent Assembly that citizens were free to go to their temples and mosques and that religion had nothing to do with the business of the state. In February 1948 he said the constitution would be democratic, embodying the essential principles of Islam, and that Pakistan would not be a theocracy ruled by priests. At the State Bank in July 1948 he called for an economic system based on Islamic ideas of equality and social justice.

**5. Points of comparison**

| Aspect | Iqbal | Jinnah |
|---|---|---|
| Role | Thinker and poet | Lawyer and statesman |
| Basis of nationhood | Faith and spiritual unity | Faith, culture, history and law, argued as a claim to self-determination |
| View of nationalism | Rejected it in principle | Used it as a tool for a Muslim nation |
| Method | Lectures, poetry, an address, letters | Party, negotiation, elections |
| Territory | North-west (1930); Bengal added by 1937 | Both zones (1940); one state (1946) |
| The state | Renewal of Islamic law through an assembly | Constitutional democracy on Islamic principles; equal citizenship |
| Audience | The Muslim mind | British, Congress and Muslim voters |

**6. How the two were connected**

Iqbal urged Jinnah to return from London and lead. His letters of May and June 1937 asked why the Muslims of north-west India and Bengal should not be considered nations entitled to self-determination, and told Jinnah that the community looked to him alone for guidance. Jinnah published these letters in 1943 and acknowledged that Iqbal's views were in substance his own and had led him to the same conclusions. The Lahore Resolution came two years after Iqbal's death, and Jinnah remarked that Iqbal, had he lived, would have been happy that the League did what he wanted.

**7. Critical appraisal**

First, the difference is one of level, not of substance. Iqbal asked what makes Muslims a community; Jinnah asked how that community could hold power under a constitution.

Second, each has been read selectively. Iqbal's 1930 words about a state "within the body-politic of India" have led some historians to doubt that he sought full sovereignty then; his 1937 letters show he moved further. Jinnah's 11 August speech is cited for a secular state, his 1948 speeches for an Islamic one. Ayesha Jalal argues that Jinnah kept his demand open to win power at the centre; I.H. Qureshi and Sharif al Mujahid see a consistent drive for a state. The fair synthesis is that Jinnah wanted a modern democratic state for a Muslim nation, inspired by Islamic values and not run by clerics, and saw no contradiction in this.

Third, a tension remained unresolved after 1947: how Islamic principles and equal citizenship fit in one constitution. The Objectives Resolution (1949) was the first attempt at an answer, and the debate continues.

**8. Conclusion**

Iqbal gave the idea of Pakistan its depth; Jinnah gave it its shape. Without the philosophy the movement would have been a bargain over seats; without the politics the philosophy would have stayed a poet's hope. Their shared legacy is a standard by which the state can still be judged: a self-respecting Muslim nation, governed by its elected representatives, committed to social justice and to the equal protection of all its citizens.

## 2. Evaluate the role of Shaikh Ahmad Sirhindi (Mujaddid Alf Sani) in the revival of Islamic thought and Muslim identity in the subcontinent.

**Outline**
1. Introduction
2. The challenge: religion and state under Akbar
3. Sirhindi's life in brief
4. His reform work: six fronts
5. Impact
6. Critical evaluation
7. Conclusion

**1. Introduction**

By the end of the sixteenth century the distinct identity of Indian Islam was under strain. The emperor Akbar's policy of universal tolerance had grown into a court religion, popular Sufism had absorbed many local practices, and the ulema were divided. Shaikh Ahmad of Sirhind (1564–1624), known as Mujaddid Alf Sani, the "renewer of the second millennium", answered this situation not with the sword but with ideas, letters and a network of disciples. His immediate political influence is debated by historians, but his long-term effect on Muslim religious thought and self-definition in India is beyond doubt.

**2. The challenge**

Akbar (1556–1605) sought to rule a mixed empire through *sulh-i-kul*, peace with all. He abolished the jizya in 1564, held inter-faith debates in the Ibadat Khana from 1575, obtained through the *mahzar* of 1579 the right to decide between jurists, and in 1582 announced the Din-i-Ilahi. Orthodox opinion believed that Islamic practices were being slighted at court. At the same time the doctrine of *wahdat-ul-wujud* (unity of being), drawn from Ibn Arabi, was being read to mean that all religions were paths to the same truth. The line between Islam and its environment was blurring.

**3. Life in brief**

Born at Sirhind in 1564, educated there and at Sialkot, he spent time at Agra among Akbar's scholars. In 1599 he joined the Naqshbandi order under Khwaja Baqi Billah in Delhi. Jahangir imprisoned him at Gwalior in 1619 for about a year, then released and honoured him. He died in 1624.

**4. His reform work**

*(a) Defence of prophethood and the Shariat.* In *Isbat-un-Nubuwwat* he argued that reason alone cannot guide man and that prophetic revelation is final. He insisted that the Sufi path must stay within the law: mystic experience that contradicts the Shariat is error.

*(b) Wahdat-ul-shuhud.* Against unity of being he set "unity of witness": the mystic's sense of oneness is a state of perception, not a fact about reality. Creator and creation remain distinct. This restored a clear theological boundary between Islam and pantheistic ideas.

*(c) Purification of practice.* He attacked *bid'at* (innovation), saint-worship and customs borrowed from Hindu society.

*(d) Winning the nobility.* He wrote to leading nobles such as Shaikh Farid Bukhari, Khan-i-Azam and Abdur Rahim Khan-i-Khanan, urging them to use their influence so that the new emperor would respect Islamic law. Reform from the top was his method.

*(e) The Maktubat.* His collected letters, more than five hundred in three volumes, set out his doctrine and travelled to Central Asia and the Ottoman lands.

*(f) Personal example and organisation.* He refused the ceremonial prostration before Jahangir, accepted prison, preached to prisoners and soldiers, and sent trained deputies to towns across India. Through his sons, especially Khwaja Muhammad Masum, the Naqshbandi-Mujaddidi order became the most influential reform network in the Muslim East.

*(g) Assertion of separateness.* He held that Islam and unbelief are distinct and cannot be merged, and called for respect for Islamic symbols in the state.

**5. Impact**

*Short term.* Din-i-Ilahi did not outlive Akbar. Jahangir's later reign showed more regard for orthodox feeling.

*Medium term.* Under Shah Jahan and above all Aurangzeb (1658–1707), who had ties with the Mujaddidi family, state policy moved towards orthodoxy: the jizya returned in 1679 and Islamic law was codified in the *Fatawa-i-Alamgiri*.

*Long term.* His reformed Sufism shaped Shah Waliullah's circle in the next century and, through it, the reform movements of the nineteenth. By insisting that Indian Muslims were a community with fixed boundaries, he supplied an early source of what later became the Two-Nation Theory. Iqbal honoured him in verse as the guardian of the community's wealth of faith in India.

*His method as a model.* Three features of his approach deserve notice. He worked through persuasion, not rebellion: he never called for revolt against the emperor and sought to change the state by changing the convictions of those who ran it. He combined the scholar and the Sufi, so that neither the jurists nor the mystics could dismiss him. And he wrote: by putting his teaching into letters that could be copied and carried, he gave it a life beyond his own circle. Later reformers — Shah Waliullah with his books, Sir Syed with his journal, Iqbal with his lectures — also chose intellectual reform before political action.

*Place in the story of Muslim identity.* Under Akbar the question was whether Indian Islam would keep a distinct shape or dissolve into a composite court culture. Sirhindi's answer — that Muslims were a community defined by revelation and law, living beside others in peace but not merging with them — is the religious root of the later political claim that Muslims were a separate nation. I.H. Qureshi therefore places him at the head of the line that runs through Shah Waliullah and Syed Ahmad Shaheed to the Aligarh and Pakistan movements.

**6. Critical evaluation**

*The traditional view.* I.H. Qureshi, S.M. Ikram and Aziz Ahmad present Sirhindi as the man who saved Indian Islam from absorption into its surroundings.

*The revisionist view.* Yohanan Friedmann's study (1971) argues that in his own time Sirhindi was known mainly as a Sufi thinker, that his letters to nobles had little measurable effect on policy, and that his political image was constructed later. Din-i-Ilahi had only a handful of adherents and was never a mass threat.

*Criticism of tone.* His harsh language about Hindus and Shias sits uneasily with the needs of a plural society, and some hold that the orthodox turn he inspired narrowed the base of Mughal rule.

*A balanced judgement.* Even if his political influence at court was smaller than tradition claims, his intellectual achievement stands. He reconciled Sufism with the law, gave Indian Muslims a coherent theology of difference, and built an organisation that carried it forward for three centuries.

*What he did not do.* He offered no programme for the Muslim masses, no educational institution and no political organisation outside his order; his audience was the court and the learned. Nor could his ideas prevent the political decline that followed Aurangzeb. His contribution was to the mind of the community, and it should be judged there.

**7. Conclusion**

Sirhindi worked at a moment when Muslim political power was at its height but Muslim religious identity was uncertain. He reversed the order of concern: for him the survival of the faith came first, and power was its servant. That conviction passed to Shah Waliullah, to the reformers of the nineteenth century and, in political form, to the makers of Pakistan. He did not found a state; he helped preserve the community that later demanded one.

## 3. "The Aligarh Movement was educational in form but political in its effects." Discuss.

**Outline**
1. Introduction
2. Muslims after 1857
3. Sir Syed's diagnosis and three aims
4. The educational programme
5. Political ideas and effects
6. Social, religious and literary effects
7. Criticism
8. Conclusion

**1. Introduction**

The Aligarh Movement, led by Sir Syed Ahmad Khan (1817–98), is usually described as an educational movement, and its instruments were indeed a school, a college, a translation society and a conference. But its purpose was the survival of the Muslims of India as a community with a share in power. Education was the means; political rehabilitation was the end. Its effects on Indian politics, from separate electorates to the Muslim League, were deep and lasting.

**2. Muslims after 1857**

The British held Muslims chiefly responsible for the rising. Estates were confiscated, Delhi's Muslims expelled for a time, and government service closed to them. Persian had already been replaced by English (1837). Muslims boycotted the new schools on religious grounds, while Hindus took to them and filled the professions. W.W. Hunter's *The Indian Musalmans* (1871) described a community ruined in Bengal. From 1867 a Hindi movement attacked Urdu.

Two responses were possible. One was to withdraw and preserve the faith, the path taken at Deoband in 1866. The other was to come to terms with the new order and master its knowledge. Sir Syed, who had served the Company as a judge and had seen the rising at first hand in Bijnor, chose the second.

**3. Diagnosis and aims**

Sir Syed concluded that Muslims faced extinction as a force unless they did three things: make peace with the rulers, acquire modern knowledge, and avoid political agitation until they were strong.

**4. The educational programme**

- *Reconciliation as groundwork:* *Asbab-i-Baghawat-i-Hind* (1859) blamed British policies, not Muslim conspiracy, for the revolt; *Loyal Muhammadans of India* recorded Muslim services.
- *Scientific Society (1863–64):* translated Western works into Urdu; the *Aligarh Institute Gazette* followed (1866).
- *Visit to England (1869–70):* he studied the universities and returned with the model of a residential college.
- *Tahzib-ul-Akhlaq (1870):* a journal to reform thought and manners.
- *MAO School (24 May 1875) and College (1877),* whose foundation stone was laid by Lord Lytton: Western sciences and English alongside Islamic instruction. It became a university in 1920.
- *Muhammadan Educational Conference (1886):* carried the message to every province and inspired institutions at Karachi, Lahore and Peshawar.

*Results.* Within a generation the college was sending Muslims into the provincial and imperial services and the bar. Its residential life, debating union and sports gave students from Punjab to Bengal a common outlook and a network of friendships. It set the pattern for Sindh Madrassat-ul-Islam (1885), the Anjuman Himayat-i-Islam's college at Lahore (1892) and Islamia College Peshawar (1913), so that the provinces which later became Pakistan acquired modern Muslim institutions of their own.

**5. Political ideas and effects**

*(a) A separate community.* During the Urdu–Hindi controversy at Benares in 1867 Sir Syed told the commissioner, Mr Shakespeare, that he no longer believed the two communities could progress as one nation — a remark recorded by his biographer Hali. He began to use the word *qaum* for Muslims as a distinct people.

*(b) Opposition to majority rule.* When the Indian National Congress was founded in 1885 he advised Muslims to stay away. In speeches at Lucknow (1887) and Meerut (1888) he argued that elections on the British pattern in a country of unequal communities would give permanent power to the majority, and that competitive examinations would shut Muslims out. He formed the United Indian Patriotic Association (1888) and the Muhammadan Anglo-Oriental Defence Association (1893).

*(c) Separate representation.* In the Viceroy's Council he argued for representation by community. This was the seed of the demand for separate electorates.

*(d) A political class.* Aligarh produced the lawyers, officials and organisers who led Muslim politics. Its trustees Mohsin-ul-Mulk and Viqar-ul-Mulk arranged the **Simla Deputation (1 October 1906)**, and the **All-India Muslim League was founded on 30 December 1906** at the session of Sir Syed's own Educational Conference. Separate electorates followed in 1909.

*(e) The Pakistan Movement.* Aligarh men — the Ali brothers, Liaquat Ali Khan, Khawaja Nazimuddin and many others — led the next generation. In the 1940s its students campaigned across India for the League; Jinnah called Aligarh the arsenal of Muslim India.

*(f) The limits of his separatism.* Sir Syed did not ask for a separate state. He described Hindus and Muslims as the two eyes of the bride that was India, and worked with Hindu colleagues in the Scientific Society. His separatism was defensive: protection for a weaker community inside British India. The step from separate interests to a separate homeland was taken by a later generation in different circumstances. What he supplied was the premise: Muslims were a distinct community whose interests could not be left to a majority vote.

*(g) Aligarh and its rivals.* The movement's weight is clearer by comparison. Deoband protected religious learning away from the state and stayed hostile to British rule; most of its scholars later allied with Congress. Nadwa (1894) sought a middle course. Aligarh alone set out to place Muslims inside the new structures of power — the services, the courts, the councils. That is why the leadership of Muslim politics came from Aligarh and not from the seminaries. A section of the Deoband ulema, led by Maulana Ashraf Ali Thanvi and Maulana Shabbir Ahmad Usmani, later gave the Pakistan Movement the religious sanction that Aligarh could not supply.

**6. Other effects**

- *Economic:* a Muslim middle class in the services and professions.
- *Religious:* a rational approach to interpretation; Sir Syed's argument that the word of God and the work of God cannot conflict.
- *Literary:* simple modern Urdu prose; Hali's *Musaddas*, Shibli's histories, Nazir Ahmad's novels.
- *Social:* a new confidence and openness to the modern world.

**7. Criticism**

- Excessive loyalty to the British.
- It served the north Indian upper and middle classes and left the poor and the Muslims of Bengal largely untouched.
- Sir Syed gave little attention to women's education.
- His rationalist theology alienated the ulema; Deoband (1866) offered a rival answer.
- Nationalist historians hold that his opposition to Congress sowed separatism. The reply is that he was responding to real fears of a minority under majority rule, and that he gave Muslims the time and tools to recover.

**8. Conclusion**

Aligarh began in a classroom and ended in a parliament of the Muslim nation. It changed the Muslims of India from a defeated and inward-looking people into a community with modern education, a middle class, a political argument and an organisation. In form it was educational; in its consequences it was the first chapter of the Pakistan Movement.

## 4. Critically evaluate the Congress ministries of 1937–39. How far did they pave the way for a separate Muslim homeland?

**Outline**
1. Introduction
2. Background: the 1935 Act and the 1937 elections
3. The coalition question
4. Muslim grievances under Congress rule
5. The League's response
6. The other side of the argument
7. From grievance to the demand for Pakistan
8. Critical assessment
9. Conclusion

**1. Introduction**

The twenty-eight months of Congress rule in eight provinces, from July 1937 to late 1939, were the first experience Indians had of responsible government by a national party. For most Muslims it was an unhappy one. It did not create the idea of a separate homeland, which Iqbal had voiced in 1930, but it turned a fear into a conviction and a weak party into a mass movement. Congress rule was the catalyst of the Pakistan demand, not its origin.

**2. Background**

The Government of India Act 1935 gave the provinces autonomy from 1 April 1937. In the elections of early 1937 Congress won about 711 of 1,585 seats and went on to form ministries in eight of eleven provinces. The Muslim League won only about 109 of 482 Muslim seats and did badly in the Muslim-majority provinces, where regional parties prevailed. But Congress itself contested only 58 Muslim seats and won 26. It could not speak for Muslims either.

The League had fought the election on a programme not very different from that of Congress, and Jinnah had spoken of cooperation after the polls. The breach that followed was not foreseen.

**3. The coalition question**

In the United Provinces the League expected a share in government. Congress offered places only if the League's assembly group merged with it and its parliamentary board was wound up. The League refused. Nehru had declared that there were only two forces in India, the British and Congress; Jinnah replied that there was a third, the Muslims. Maulana Azad later called the refusal of a coalition a blunder that revived the League.

**4. Muslim grievances**

*(a) Symbols.* The song *Bande Mataram*, taken from a novel with an anti-Muslim setting, was sung in assemblies and schools. The Congress flag flew on public buildings.

*(b) Education.* The Wardha Scheme of basic education stressed crafts, excluded religious instruction and promoted reverence for Gandhi and Hindustani. The Vidya Mandir scheme in the Central Provinces used the very word "temple" for schools.

*(c) Language.* Hindi in Devanagari was advanced at the expense of Urdu.

*(d) Religion.* There were complaints of bans on cow slaughter, interference with prayers and partiality in communal riots.

*(e) Administration.* Muslims complained of exclusion from jobs and local bodies and of Congress committees acting as a parallel government.

*(f) Politics.* Nehru's Muslim Mass Contact campaign tried to detach Muslim voters from their own party.

*(g) Exclusion in daily life.* Beyond specific measures, Muslims in the minority provinces felt a change of atmosphere. District officials took their cue from Congress committees; local bodies passed into the hands of the ruling party; Muslims complained that they were pressed to join Congress to obtain ordinary favours. For a community that had shared in the administration of these provinces for generations, being ruled by a party organisation that treated their own party as illegitimate was new and alarming. The feeling was strongest in the United Provinces and Bihar, the provinces that supplied the League's leadership.

**5. The League's response**

The League documented these complaints in the **Pirpur Report (1938)**, the **Sharif Report (1939)** and A.K. Fazlul Haq's *Muslim Sufferings under Congress Rule* (1939). More important, Jinnah used the period to rebuild the party. At **Lucknow in October 1937** the premiers of Punjab, Bengal and Assam brought their followers into the League; membership was opened to the masses; by-elections were won. The Sindh Muslim League Conference of October 1938 spoke of two nations. When the ministries resigned in October–November 1939 over the war, Jinnah called for a **Day of Deliverance on 22 December 1939**.

**6. The other side**

Congress denied the charges and offered an inquiry by the Chief Justice of the Federal Court; Jinnah wanted a Royal Commission, and neither took place. British governors did not use their special powers, and some thought the complaints overstated. The reports were party documents. Several ministries did useful work in tenancy reform and education. Many of the grievances were about symbols rather than laws.

**7. From grievance to Pakistan**

Even on the most cautious reading, the political effect was decisive.

1. *Safeguards discredited.* The 1935 Act's protections for minorities had not been invoked once. Muslims concluded that no written guarantee would bind a permanent majority.
2. *The League transformed.* It emerged as the one all-India voice of Muslims.
3. *Jinnah's conversion completed.* The former ambassador of unity now spoke of two nations.
4. *British need.* With Congress out of office during the war, the Viceroy assured the League that no constitution would be imposed without Muslim consent.
5. *The Lahore Resolution.* Within four months of the resignations, on 23 March 1940, the League demanded independent states in the Muslim-majority zones.

6. *The geography of the movement shifted.* Until 1937 Muslim separatism was strongest where Muslims were a minority. After the Lucknow session the premiers of the majority provinces came in, and the League could for the first time speak of territory as well as rights. Without that shift a demand for homelands in the north-west and east would have had no base.

**8. Critical assessment**

Congress rule cannot bear the whole explanation. The idea of a Muslim territory already existed (Iqbal 1930, Rahmat Ali 1933). The structure of representative government had been pushing a minority of one-quarter towards separate politics since 1906. The war gave the League leverage it had lacked. And Jinnah's organisational skill converted discontent into votes. What the Congress ministries did was to supply proof, in the daily experience of ordinary Muslims, of an argument that had until then been theoretical. R. Coupland noted that the "Congress Raj" bore the character of one-party rule; Ayesha Jalal stresses how Jinnah used this to build an all-India standing. Both points are true.

It is also fair to note that Congress was a nationalist party facing its first test of office, anxious to prove that it represented all Indians. What it saw as national symbols and mass contact, Muslims saw as majority culture and encroachment. The tragedy of 1937–39 lay in that difference of perception as much as in any act of oppression.

**9. Conclusion**

Had Congress shared power in 1937 and governed with visible sensitivity, the Muslim League might have remained a party of safeguards. Its refusal and its style of rule convinced Muslims that in a united India they would be a permanent and powerless minority. The road from the Congress ministries to the Lahore Resolution was short and direct. They did not invent the demand for a separate homeland, but more than any other event they made it the demand of the Muslim masses.

## 5. Assess Quaid-i-Azam's leadership of the Muslims of India during the crisis-ridden decade 1937–1947.

**Outline**
1. Introduction
2. The position in 1937
3. Rebuilding the League, 1937–39
4. Defining the goal, 1940
5. Wartime strategy, 1940–45
6. Proving the mandate, 1945–46
7. Negotiating the transfer of power, 1946–47
8. Qualities of his leadership
9. Criticism
10. Conclusion

**1. Introduction**

In 1937 the Muslim League won about a quarter of the Muslim seats and no province. Ten years later it had created the largest Muslim state in the world. The distance between those two points was covered under one man's leadership. Stanley Wolpert opened his biography with the judgement that few individuals alter the course of history, fewer still modify the map of the world, and hardly anyone can be credited with creating a nation-state — and that Jinnah did all three. This answer examines how he did it and with what limits.

**2. The position in 1937**

The League was a party of notables without funds or village branches. Punjab was held by the Unionists, Bengal by Fazlul Haq's party, Sindh and the Frontier by others. Congress, with ministries in eight provinces, refused to treat the League as an equal.

**3. Rebuilding the League, 1937–39**

- At **Lucknow (October 1937)** Jinnah won over Sikandar Hayat, Fazlul Haq and Saadullah, bringing three provincial premiers under the League's banner.
- He turned the party towards the masses: a two-anna membership, a flag, provincial and district branches, a students' federation, a women's wing, and later the newspaper *Dawn*.
- He gave voice to Muslim grievances under Congress rule and observed the Day of Deliverance (22 December 1939). By then he was the *Quaid-i-Azam*.

**4. Defining the goal, 1940**

At Lahore on 22–23 March 1940 he recast the Muslim claim: not a minority seeking safeguards but a nation entitled to homelands. The Lahore Resolution gave Muslims a single, simple aim. Its deliberate breadth kept every Muslim province and every school of opinion inside the movement.

The phrase "independent states" was clarified at Delhi in April 1946 as a single state. In the meantime it let Bengal and Punjab each read their own hopes into the demand, and gave Jinnah room to negotiate.

**5. Wartime strategy, 1940–45**

- He neither obstructed nor fully joined the war effort, and extracted the August Offer's pledge (1940) that no constitution would be imposed on unwilling minorities.
- He rejected the **Cripps proposals (1942)** because they did not concede Pakistan clearly, while noting that the principle of partition had been admitted.
- He kept the League out of **Quit India (1942)**. While Congress leaders were in prison, League ministries were formed in Bengal, Sindh, Assam and the Frontier.
- In the **Gandhi–Jinnah talks (September 1944)** he refused a "maimed, mutilated and moth-eaten" Pakistan and obliged Gandhi to deal with him as the spokesman of the Muslims.
- At the **Simla Conference (1945)** he insisted that the League alone nominate Muslim members. The conference failed, but his veto over any settlement was established.

- He asserted the authority of the central League over provincial leaders. When Fazlul Haq and Sikandar Hayat joined the Viceroy's National Defence Council in 1941 without his approval, he compelled them to withdraw. In 1944 he confronted Khizr Hayat Tiwana's Unionists in Punjab, and within two years the League had replaced them as the voice of Punjab's Muslims. Pakistan could not have been won without Punjab and Bengal, and it was in these years that he won them.

**6. Proving the mandate, 1945–46**

He fought the elections on one issue. The League won all 30 Muslim seats in the central assembly and about 428 of 492 in the provinces. At the Delhi Convention (April 1946) the elected legislators pledged themselves to a single sovereign Pakistan. The claim to represent Muslim India was now a counted fact.

The campaign was a feat of mobilisation: students from Aligarh and the Islamia colleges toured the villages, pirs and ulema lent their influence, and the call for Pakistan reached voters who had never before taken part in politics.

**7. Negotiating the transfer of power, 1946–47**

- He **accepted the Cabinet Mission Plan (6 June 1946)**, which rejected a sovereign Pakistan but grouped the Muslim provinces. This shows a negotiator ready to take the substance without the name.
- When Nehru said on 10 July that Congress was free to alter the plan, Jinnah withdrew acceptance and called for **Direct Action (16 August 1946)** — the only time he left constitutional methods.
- He brought the League into the interim government (October 1946), where Liaquat Ali Khan's control of finance showed that government without the League's consent could not work.
- He accepted the **3 June Plan** and a divided Punjab and Bengal as the price of sovereignty, and saw the state founded on 14 August 1947.

- After independence his leadership met its final test. As Governor-General he set up a government from nothing in Karachi, faced the refugee emergency, the Kashmir war and the withholding of Pakistan's share of assets, laid down principles of equal citizenship (11 August 1947) and of the armed forces' obedience to civil authority (Quetta, June 1948), and held the new state together through its first and most dangerous year.

**8. Qualities of his leadership**

| Quality | Evidence |
|---|---|
| Clarity of aim | One demand, stated in terms everyone could grasp |
| Constitutional method | Argument, law and the ballot; mass action only once |
| Organisation | A notables' club turned into a mass party in under three years |
| Timing | Used the war, the Congress resignations and British weakness |
| Negotiating skill | Never conceded a point without a return; kept his final position hidden |
| Integrity | Friends and opponents agreed he could not be bought or flattered |
| Unifying power | Held together landlords, ulema, students, merchants and rival provincial leaders |

**9. Criticism**

- The demand was left undefined in territory for years, which later allowed disputes about what had been promised.
- Direct Action Day was followed by the Calcutta killings and a chain of communal massacres.
- The League had shallow roots in the provinces that became Pakistan; much rested on one man who died thirteen months after independence.
- Muslims left in India bore the cost of a state they could not join.
- Ayesha Jalal argues that he sought parity at an all-India centre and was forced to accept the truncated state he had earlier refused. Others reply that every step from 1940 pointed to sovereignty and that he took the most that was obtainable.

**10. Conclusion**

Jinnah's leadership in this decade combined a fixed purpose with flexible tactics. He read correctly that British power was ending, that Congress would not share it, and that only an organised nation with a mandate could claim a state. He built the organisation, won the mandate and secured the state by negotiation. The weaknesses of what he built — a party dependent on him, a boundary drawn in haste — became clear after his death. They do not lessen the scale of the achievement.

## 6. Analyse the causes and consequences of the separation of East Pakistan in 1971, including the role of regional and global actors. What lessons does it hold?

**Outline**
1. Introduction
2. Long-term causes: political, economic, cultural
3. Immediate causes, 1970–71
4. The role of regional and global actors
5. Consequences
6. Lessons
7. Conclusion

**1. Introduction**

On 16 December 1971 Pakistan lost its eastern wing and more than half its people. The event is often explained by Indian intervention alone. India's role was decisive in the final weeks, but it acted on a breach that had been widening for twenty-four years. The separation was the result of a long failure to share power and resources with the majority province, a refusal to honour the 1970 election, and a military solution to a political problem, which a hostile neighbour and a divided world then exploited.

**2. Long-term causes**

*(a) Political exclusion.* East Pakistan held about 55% of the population but never governed. The demand for Bengali as a state language was rejected until blood was shed on 21 February 1952. The United Front, elected by a landslide in 1954 on a programme of autonomy, was dismissed within weeks. Parity and One Unit (1955–56) cancelled the Bengali majority. From 1958 the country was ruled by an army and bureaucracy drawn overwhelmingly from the west, and Ayub Khan's indirect system gave the Bengali middle class no outlet.

*(b) Economic disparity.* In the 1950s East Pakistan's jute earned most of the country's foreign exchange, while the larger share of imports, investment and development spending went to the west. The income gap between the wings widened through the 1960s, as the government's own planners admitted. Bengali economists argued that Pakistan had "two economies" and that capital was being transferred westward.

*(c) Administrative and military imbalance.* Bengalis held few senior civil posts and a small fraction of army commissions. The capital, the headquarters of the forces and the head offices of banks and firms were all a thousand miles away.

*(d) Cultural distance.* Bengali language and culture were treated with suspicion. The 1965 war, in which the east was left virtually undefended, destroyed the argument that its security lay in the west.

*(e) A different political culture.* East Bengal had abolished its great estates in 1950 and had a vocal middle class of lawyers, teachers and students; West Pakistan's politics was led by landlords and officials. The two wings wanted different things from the state.

*(f) The Six Points.* In February 1966 Sheikh Mujibur Rahman set out a programme that left the centre only defence and foreign affairs and gave the wings separate currencies or fiscal barriers, their own taxation, their own foreign-exchange accounts and their own militia. The Agartala Conspiracy Case (1968) made him a hero.

**3. Immediate causes**

- The **Bhola cyclone** of November 1970 and the slow official response deepened alienation.
- In the **election of 7 December 1970** the Awami League won 160 of 300 general seats, all in the east; the Pakistan People's Party won 81, all in the west.
- Mujib would not dilute the Six Points. Zulfikar Ali Bhutto refused to attend the Assembly in Dhaka without prior agreement. On **1 March 1971** General Yahya Khan postponed the session; the east rose in civil disobedience.
- Talks in Dhaka failed. On **25 March** the army launched Operation Searchlight. Mujib was arrested, independence was proclaimed, and a civil war began in which grave excesses were committed; the numbers remain disputed.

**4. Regional and global actors**

*India.* It sheltered the exiled government, trained and armed the Mukti Bahini, used the refugee influx to win sympathy abroad, signed a treaty with Moscow in **August 1971**, and invaded in late November with overwhelming local superiority. It saw a historic opportunity to halve its rival.

*The Soviet Union.* It armed India and vetoed ceasefire resolutions in the Security Council three times in December.

*The United States.* President Nixon and Dr Kissinger valued Pakistan as their channel to Beijing and "tilted" towards it in words. They sent a naval task force into the Bay of Bengal as a gesture, but arms supplies had been cut off and no help came.

*China.* It gave diplomatic support but, with Soviet divisions on its border, did not intervene.

*The United Nations.* The General Assembly voted 104 to 11 for a ceasefire; the vetoed Council could do nothing. The Muslim world offered sympathy and little else.

**5. Consequences**

*For Pakistan:* military defeat and some 93,000 prisoners; the loss of a major market and export base; a crisis of confidence. It also brought renewal: civilian rule under Bhutto, the Simla Agreement (1972), the consensus Constitution of 1973 with a Senate and provincial rights, the decision to seek a nuclear deterrent, and a foreign policy turned towards China and the Muslim world.

It also left a human legacy: stranded communities in both countries, and unresolved questions of apology and assets that still surface in relations with Dhaka, which have warmed markedly since August 2024.

*For the region:* India emerged as the dominant power; Bangladesh was born.

*For ideology:* critics claimed the Two-Nation Theory had drowned in the Bay of Bengal. But Bangladesh did not rejoin India; it remained a separate Muslim-majority state. What failed was the federation, not the idea that the Muslims of the subcontinent were distinct.

**6. Who was responsible?**

The **Hamoodur Rahman Commission** blamed the military leadership for political misjudgement and professional and moral failure, and criticised politicians as well. Rounaq Jahan traces the cause to two decades of failed integration; G.W. Choudhury stresses the choices of Yahya, Bhutto and Mujib in 1971; Sisson and Rose document India's planning from April onward. The causes were cumulative: structure made a crisis likely, leaders made it fatal, and outsiders made it final.

**7. Lessons**

1. **Respect the ballot.** A majority denied power will seek it elsewhere.
2. **Make federalism real.** The Senate, the Council of Common Interests, the NFC and the 18th Amendment are the constitutional memory of 1971.
3. **Share resources fairly.** Regional inequality is a security question.
4. **Unity is not uniformity.** Languages and cultures must be accommodated.
5. **Political problems need political answers.** Force turned autonomy into independence.
6. **Make national institutions representative** of every region.
7. **Close internal fault lines** before enemies use them.
8. **Hold power accountable.** Suppressing the Commission's report meant the lessons were learned late and incompletely.

These lessons are not history only. Balochistan's grievances over resources and representation, and the suspension of an elected government in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa in 2026, are tests of whether they have been absorbed.

**8. Conclusion**

East Pakistan was not lost in the fourteen days of December 1971. It was lost in the language riots of 1952, the dismissal of 1954, the parity formula, the years of martial law and the postponement of 1 March 1971. India delivered the final blow, but the wound was self-inflicted. The surest tribute to that loss is a federation in which every province feels that its vote counts and its share is secure.

## 7. Critically evaluate the federal structure of Pakistan after the 18th Amendment. Why has the amendment come under criticism, and should devolved subjects return to the centre?

**Outline**
1. Introduction
2. Background: the centralised federation before 2010
3. What the 18th Amendment and the 7th NFC Award changed
4. Achievements
5. Criticisms
6. Health and education: a test case
7. Should subjects go back?
8. Way forward
9. Conclusion

**1. Introduction**

The Eighteenth Amendment, passed unanimously in April 2010, was the most far-reaching revision of the 1973 Constitution. Together with the Seventh National Finance Commission Award of December 2009 it moved power and money from Islamabad to the provinces on a scale not seen before. Fifteen years later it is praised as the charter of the federation and blamed for fiscal stress and poor social services. This answer argues that its design is sound and its implementation incomplete: the remedy is to finish devolution and coordinate better, not to reverse it.

**2. Background**

Pakistan's history is one of over-centralisation: the viceregal 1935 Act, One Unit, the presidential system of 1962 and long military rule. The price was paid in 1971. The 1973 Constitution promised autonomy but kept a Concurrent List of 47 subjects on which the centre prevailed, with an understanding that it would be reviewed after ten years. Military rulers then added Article 58(2)(b). By 2008 the smaller provinces' demand for autonomy was the central issue of federal politics.

**3. What changed**

- **Concurrent List abolished;** about seventeen ministries devolved, including education, health, labour, environment, culture and social welfare.
- **Council of Common Interests** made a real forum: chaired by the Prime Minister, meeting every ninety days, with its own secretariat.
- **Article 160(3A):** a province's NFC share may not fall below the previous award.
- **Article 172(3):** oil and gas owned jointly and equally by the federation and the province concerned.
- **Article 140A:** provinces to establish elected local governments.
- Article 58(2)(b) repealed; NWFP renamed Khyber Pakhtunkhwa; new rights to education (25A), information (19A) and fair trial (10A).
- **7th NFC Award:** provinces' share raised to 57.5%; the formula widened beyond population (82%) to poverty (10.3%), revenue (5%) and inverse population density (2.7%).

**4. Achievements**

1. It restored the parliamentary, federal consensus of 1973 and closed the constitutional door on dictatorship.
2. It met the main demand of the smaller provinces; mainstream nationalist parties in Sindh and Khyber Pakhtunkhwa committed themselves to the federation.
3. Provinces gained fiscal room: transfers multiplied and Balochistan's share rose to 9.09%.
4. Provinces legislated and innovated: compulsory-education laws, health-care commissions, right-to-information and right-to-services laws, women's protection laws, provincial revenue authorities, health insurance in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and Punjab.
5. During COVID-19 the National Command and Operation Centre showed that centre and provinces could act together.

6. Good practice has travelled from province to province — health cards from Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, school monitoring from Punjab, public–private health management from Sindh — which is the federal "laboratory" at work.

**5. Criticisms**

*(a) Fiscal imbalance.* After transfers the federal government's net revenue is largely absorbed by interest and defence; it borrows for the rest, yet still funds BISP, higher education and subsidies. The IMF has pressed for a "National Fiscal Pact" to rebalance.

*(b) Weak provincial revenue.* Provinces collect only about 1% of GDP. Agricultural income and urban property stayed lightly taxed until laws were passed under IMF pressure in 2025.

*(c) Capacity.* Subjects were handed over without staff, regulators or transition funds.

*(d) Loss of uniform standards* in curriculum, medicines, higher education and data; friction between federal and provincial bodies.

*(e) Devolution stopped at the provincial capital.* Local governments were delayed, dissolved or starved; Provincial Finance Commissions are dormant.

*(f) Institutions under-used.* The CCI meets irregularly; no NFC award has been agreed since 2009.

*(g) Duplication.* Federal ministries reappeared in devolved fields.

*(h) Security and emergencies.* The floods of 2022 and 2025 and the campaign against militancy exposed gaps in coordination between federal and provincial authorities. In October 2026 the centre imposed emergency and governor's rule in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa under Articles 232 and 234 — a reminder that the federal balance depends on political trust as well as on the constitutional text.

**6. Health and education: a test case**

*Gains:* provincial budgets rose many times in rupee terms; teachers were hired on merit and monitored; public–private schools and health units expanded; Lady Health Workers were regularised; health cards gave the poor hospital cover.

*Failures:* about 25 million children remain out of school; literacy is stuck near 60%; about 40% of young children are stunted; polio persists; and combined public spending on each sector has fallen to about 0.8% of GDP (Economic Survey 2025–26). Disparities between Punjab and Balochistan have widened. Vertical health programmes lost funding in the hand-over.

*Explanation:* these failures predate 2010 and come from low priority, population growth, weak management and the absence of local government. Devolution did not cause them, but neither did it cure them.

**7. Should subjects go back to the centre?**

*The case for:* national standards in schooling and health; a single population policy; fiscal sustainability; climate and disaster management.

*The case against:* the centre ran these subjects no better before 2010; reversal would breach the consensus on which the federation rests and revive the grievances of 1971 and of Balochistan; Article 144 already allows provinces to hand a matter to Parliament by resolution, as they did for drug regulation; standards can be agreed in the CCI; the fiscal gap is better closed by taxing more and sharing expenditure.

*Judgement:* no wholesale return. A few functions that need one national voice — regulation of medicines, minimum learning and health standards, statistics, international commitments — should be exercised jointly through the CCI. The attempts in 2025 to reopen the NFC guarantee and recentralise education and population, and the talk in 2026 of a 28th Amendment, will strengthen the federation only if they proceed by consent.

India offers a useful comparison. Education there is a concurrent subject, yet most gains have come from states that invested in their own systems, with the centre financing national programmes through conditional grants. Money and standards can be shared without taking powers back.

**8. Way forward**

1. A new NFC award that matches money to duties, rewards provincial tax effort and human development, and shares national burdens.
2. Conditional grants for out-of-school children, nutrition and family planning.
3. Constitutional protection for local government: tenure, functions, finance.
4. A regular, working CCI with implementation tracking.
5. Provincial capacity: planning, data, regulation.
6. Equalisation for Balochistan and the merged districts.
7. Cooperative, not coercive, federalism.

**9. Conclusion**

The 18th Amendment gave Pakistan the federal design its diversity requires. Its weaknesses lie in what was left undone: money without matching responsibility, provinces without local government, autonomy without coordination. To roll it back would be to forget why it was needed. To complete it is the unfinished task.

## 8. Why has democracy failed to take root in Pakistan? Discuss with reference to civil–military relations and suggest a way forward.

**Outline**
1. Introduction
2. The record
3. Causes: historical and structural
4. Causes: civil–military imbalance
5. Causes: the political class and society
6. Causes: institutions and external factors
7. Signs of resilience
8. The present position
9. Way forward
10. Conclusion

**1. Introduction**

Pakistan was created by a constitutional movement and a vote, yet in nearly eight decades no prime minister has completed a five-year term. The country has known four spells of military rule, three constitutions, and elections whose results were repeatedly disputed. Democracy has not been absent; it has been interrupted, managed and distrusted. The causes lie in a weak start, an early imbalance between elected and unelected institutions, the failures of the political class itself, and a security environment that favoured exceptional rule. Recovery depends on civilians earning authority through performance and on all institutions keeping to their constitutional limits.

**2. The record**

- 1947–58: seven prime ministers; no general election; the Constituent Assembly dissolved (1954).
- 1958–71: Ayub and Yahya; the first direct election came only in 1970 and its result was not honoured.
- 1971–77: elected government; ended by martial law after a disputed election.
- 1977–88: General Zia. 1988–99: four elected governments removed. 1999–2008: General Musharraf.
- 2008–present: three completed parliaments (2013, 2018, 2023), the first peaceful transfers of power, but also prime ministers removed by courts (2012, 2017) and by a no-confidence vote amid controversy (2022), a disputed election (2024) and far-reaching constitutional changes (2024–25).

**3. Historical and structural causes**

*(a) The inheritance.* Pakistan inherited the viceregal system of the 1935 Act and, as Hamza Alavi put it, an "overdeveloped" bureaucracy and army relative to its political institutions. The Muslim League had led a movement in provinces that stayed in India; it had shallow roots in the new country.

*(b) Early loss of leadership.* Jinnah died in 1948, Liaquat in 1951.

*(c) Delay.* Nine years to make a constitution; twenty-three to hold a national election.

*(d) Insecurity.* War in Kashmir within weeks of independence, a larger hostile neighbour, and Afghan irredentism made defence the first charge on the state. Ayesha Jalal argues that this, and the alliance with the United States, tilted the internal balance towards the army and bureaucracy.

*(e) The loss of the majority.* The refusal to accept the result of the 1970 election cost the country its eastern wing, and with it the province that had the strongest tradition of popular politics.

**4. Civil–military imbalance**

- The army became the most cohesive institution in a divided polity (Hasan-Askari Rizvi) and came to see itself as the guardian of the state (Aqil Shah).
- Direct rule for some thirty-three years; decisive influence over security and foreign policy in the civilian intervals.
- Constitutional devices kept civilians insecure: Article 58(2)(b) removed four governments between 1988 and 1996.
- Economic interests grew alongside (Ayesha Siddiqa's "Milbus").
- Recent developments — the SIFC (2023), longer service tenures (2024), the 27th Amendment's Chief of Defence Forces and lifetime rank and immunity (2025) — have formalised a larger military role. Supporters call this coordination that delivered stability and success in the May 2025 conflict; critics call it a hybrid system.

**5. The political class and society**

*(a) Weak parties.* Parties are built around families and personalities, hold no real internal elections and depend on "electables".

*(b) Dynastic and feudal influence.* Land, tribe and biradari still deliver votes in much of rural Sindh, south Punjab and Balochistan — though urbanisation has reduced this, and business and urban elites are now as dominant.

*(c) Zero-sum politics.* Governments persecute opponents; oppositions invite intervention. Each side welcomed the other's removal in the 1990s and since.

*(d) Poor governance.* Corruption, patronage and neglect of services cost elected governments their legitimacy.

*(e) No local government.* The nursery of democratic leadership has been kept closed by provincial governments themselves.

*(f) Social conditions.* Low literacy, poverty and inequality make citizens dependent on patrons.

*(g) Accountability as a weapon.* From PRODA (1949) and EBDO (1959) to the National Accountability Bureau, anti-corruption laws have been applied selectively against those out of favour. The effect has been to discredit both politicians and accountability itself.

**6. Institutions and external factors**

- **The judiciary** validated takeovers in 1955, 1958, 1977 and 2000 under the doctrine of necessity. Later it swung to activism, unseating prime ministers.
- **Election management** has rarely been trusted by losers.
- **Foreign patrons** found military rulers convenient partners during the Cold War and the war on terror.

**7. Signs of resilience**

Every military regime ended amid public demand for democracy. The 1973 Constitution has survived two suspensions. The 18th Amendment was passed by consensus. The Lawyers' Movement defended judicial independence. The media, civil society and a young, connected electorate make politics harder to manage: the 2024 election showed voters could not simply be directed. Democracy in Pakistan is contested, not dead.

Comparative experience is encouraging. Indonesia after 1998, South Korea, Chile and, for a period, Turkey show that the balance between soldiers and civilians is not fixed. It shifts when civilian governments deliver growth and when political forces agree among themselves on the rules.

**8. The present position (November 2026)**

A coalition governs with a two-thirds majority obtained after a disputed election and a court review of reserved seats. The main opposition leader has been in prison since 2023. The 26th and 27th Amendments have reshaped the superior courts. In October 2026 emergency and governor's rule were imposed in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa. The government points to economic stabilisation and diplomatic gains; the opposition speaks of a managed democracy. Stability without broad consent remains fragile.

**9. Way forward**

1. **Deliver.** Authority follows performance: growth, jobs, services, clean administration.
2. **A new charter of democracy** among all parties: accept results, end political victimisation, settle disputes in Parliament.
3. **Credible elections:** an Election Commission appointed by genuine consensus, transparent result systems.
4. **Party reform:** internal elections, open finances.
5. **Local government** with constitutional protection.
6. **Institutional civil–military forums** under the cabinet and Parliament; gradual, negotiated rebalancing, as in Indonesia.
7. **Judicial independence and restraint;** civilian courts for civilians.
8. **Political reconciliation:** dialogue with the opposition and restoration of elected government in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa.
9. **Education and civic awareness.**
10. **Peace in the neighbourhood,** which would lower the premium on security.

**10. Conclusion**

Democracy has struggled in Pakistan because it was never allowed to run long enough to correct itself, and because those who ran it too often failed those who voted for it. The military's role grew in the space that civilian weakness left, and then helped keep civilians weak. Breaking that circle requires patience on one side and performance on the other. The constitution already provides the framework; what is needed is the habit of living within it.

## 9. Discuss the crisis of national integration in Pakistan since 1947. How can a diverse, multi-ethnic country strengthen its unity?

**Outline**
1. Introduction
2. The nature of Pakistan's diversity
3. How the crisis developed
4. Causes
5. Present fault lines
6. Forces of unity and what has worked
7. Way forward
8. Conclusion

**1. Introduction**

National integration is the process by which people of different regions, languages and groups come to feel that they belong to one political community. Pakistan was founded on a shared religious identity, but its people speak many languages and live in provinces of very unequal size. For most of its history the state tried to secure unity through centralisation and uniformity. The result was the opposite: the loss of East Pakistan in 1971 and recurring unrest in the smaller provinces. Experience shows that in Pakistan unity grows from accommodation — a fair share of power, resources and respect — and weakens under compulsion.

**2. The nature of the diversity**

By mother tongue (2023 census) Punjabi-speakers are about 37%, Pashto 18%, Sindhi 14%, Saraiki 12%, Urdu 9% and Balochi about 3%, with many smaller languages. Punjab has just over half the population; Balochistan has 44% of the area and about 6% of the people. One unit therefore dominates the legislature, the army and the services by numbers alone. Sectarian, tribal, rural–urban and class divisions cut across these.

Diversity itself is not the problem. Many languages coexisted for centuries in the Indus valley under shared religious and cultural traditions, and the Sufi poetry of Bulleh Shah, Shah Abdul Latif, Rahman Baba and Khwaja Ghulam Farid is a common heritage. The problem arises when identity becomes the channel for grievances about power and resources.

**3. How the crisis developed**

- **1947–71:** Urdu alone as national language; One Unit; parity; martial law. The Bengali majority was excluded and left.
- The same years saw the first Baloch and Pashtun grievances: the manner of Kalat's accession in 1948 and the dismissal of Dr Khan Sahib's ministry in the Frontier left memories that later movements drew upon.
- **1973–77:** a federal constitution, but the dismissal of the Balochistan government and an insurgency.
- **1980s:** the Sindh uprising of 1983; the rise of the MQM; the Kalabagh Dam controversy; sectarian militancy.
- **2000s:** the killing of Nawab Akbar Bugti (2006) and the fifth Baloch insurgency; military operations in the tribal areas.
- **2010s:** the 18th Amendment and 7th NFC Award eased tensions; FATA was merged (2018); but the Pashtun Tahaffuz Movement and Baloch protests showed new grievances.
- **2020s:** the worst violence in Balochistan in decades, a dispute between Sindh and Punjab over new canals (2025), and governor's rule in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (2026).

**4. Causes**

1. **Over-centralisation** and long denial of provincial autonomy.
2. **Military rule,** in which smaller provinces had little voice.
3. **Unequal development and resource disputes:** water, gas, minerals, the NFC.
4. **Language and cultural policy** that treated diversity as a threat.
5. **Force used for political problems.**
6. **Weak national parties** and engineered provincial politics.
7. **No local government** to give communities control of their affairs.
8. **Economic stagnation,** which turns every question into a fight over shares.
9. **External interference** exploiting real grievances.
10. **A curriculum and media** that did little to build a shared civic identity.

**5. Present fault lines**

*Balochistan.* Complaints of exploitation of gas and minerals, enforced disappearances, demographic fears around Gwadar and manipulated politics. Militants hijacked the Jaffar Express in March 2025 and mounted coordinated attacks across the province on 31 January 2026; mining and CPEC projects are targets. The state stresses foreign sponsorship; the grievances are also local.

*Khyber Pakhtunkhwa.* War fatigue, unmet promises to the merged districts, disputes over hydel profits, and a confrontation between the provincial government and the centre that ended in emergency rule in October 2026.

*Sindh.* Water and the delta; Karachi's governance; rejection of any division of the province in the new-provinces debate.

*Southern Punjab, Gilgit-Baltistan, Azad Kashmir.* Demands for a province, for constitutional status and for relief from prices.

*The new-provinces debate.* Since mid-2026 ministers have spoken of a constitutional amendment to create several new provinces. Supporters argue that smaller units would be easier to govern and would dilute Punjab's weight. Sindh's ruling party rejects any division of that province, and Article 239(4) requires the consent of two-thirds of the provincial assembly concerned. Handled by consensus, new provinces could meet old demands such as that of the Saraiki belt. Imposed from the centre, they would become one more grievance.

**6. Forces of unity and what has worked**

Islam and a shared history; Urdu as a link language; an integrated economy and labour market; national parties; motorways and media; sport; common response to floods and to the May 2025 conflict. Constitutional devices have worked where they were honoured: the Senate, the Council of Common Interests, and above all the consensus of 2009–10. The 7th NFC Award introduced poverty, revenue and density into the formula; the 18th Amendment abolished the Concurrent List and gave provinces joint ownership of oil and gas. These steps drew nationalist parties into the mainstream in Sindh and Khyber Pakhtunkhwa. Where devolution failed to reach, as in Balochistan's districts, alienation persisted.

Comparative experience points the same way. India eased its language conflicts by reorganising states on linguistic lines in 1956; Switzerland and Canada hold together through deep autonomy. Centralised multi-ethnic states — Yugoslavia, Sudan, and Pakistan itself before 1971 — broke apart. Katharine Adeney's comparison of India and Pakistan concludes that the design of the federation, more than the fact of diversity, explains the difference in outcomes.

**7. Way forward**

*Political*
1. Implement the 18th Amendment in letter and spirit; a CCI that meets on time.
2. Elected, empowered local governments.
3. Credible elections in every province; accept regional parties as partners.
4. In Balochistan: dialogue with nationalists who accept the federation, an end to enforced disappearances through law, and a local stake in Reko Diq and Gwadar.
5. In Khyber Pakhtunkhwa: restore elected government; honour the merger commitments.
6. New provinces, if any, only by consensus and through Article 239.

*Economic*
7. A new NFC award with more weight for poverty, area and human development; Provincial Finance Commissions; an equalisation fund for backward districts.
8. A fixed share of royalties and jobs for producing districts.
9. Fair water sharing with trusted measurement.

*Cultural and social*
10. Respect and teach regional languages; a curriculum that presents diversity as heritage.
11. Broader recruitment to national institutions.
12. Inter-provincial exchanges, media and sport.

**8. Conclusion**

Pakistan's diversity is a fact, not a flaw. The countries that hold together across such differences are those that share power generously. Pakistan learned in 1971 what the alternative costs, and showed in 2010 that it can choose accommodation. The task now is to carry that accommodation to Balochistan, the merged districts and every neglected region, so that citizens there see the federation as the guarantor of their rights.

## 10. Identify Pakistan's main economic challenges. How do IMF conditions affect stabilisation and long-term sustainability?

**Outline**
1. Introduction
2. The economy in 2026
3. Structural challenges
4. The IMF programme and its conditions
5. Effects on stabilisation
6. Effects on long-term sustainability
7. Way forward
8. Conclusion

**1. Introduction**

Pakistan's economy moves in a familiar cycle: a spurt of consumption-led growth, a balance-of-payments crisis, an IMF programme, a painful stabilisation, and a relaxation that starts the cycle again. The country has turned to the Fund more than twenty times. The present Extended Fund Facility, approved in September 2024, has again brought stability. But stability is not growth, and the Fund treats symptoms. Sustainability depends on whether Pakistan itself reforms taxation, exports, energy and human capital.

**2. The economy in 2026**

According to the Economic Survey 2025–26, GDP grew by 3.7% (target 4.2%), the fastest in four years; the economy reached about $452 billion and income per head $1,901. Inflation averaged about 6%, down from a peak of 38% in May 2023. Reserves were about $17 billion; remittances were heading for a record $41 billion. But goods exports fell, poverty was estimated at 28.9% and unemployment at 7.1%, and public spending on education and on health was about 0.8% of GDP each. The 2026–27 budget of Rs 18.77 trillion gives about Rs 8 trillion to interest and Rs 3 trillion to defence, against a tax target of Rs 15.26 trillion to be shared with the provinces.

**3. Structural challenges**

*(a) Low revenue.* Tax-to-GDP is about 10–11%. Salaried people and organised industry carry the load; agriculture, retail and real estate largely escape.

*(b) Debt.* Public debt is near 70% of GDP; interest is the largest item of spending; external repayments exceed $20 billion a year and depend on rollovers from friendly states.

*(c) Weak exports.* About $30 billion, a tenth of GDP, concentrated in textiles. Every growth spurt sucks in imports and ends in a dollar shortage.

*(d) Low savings and investment:* about 13–14% of GDP, less than half the regional norm.

*(e) Energy:* surplus capacity bought on take-or-pay terms, circular debt of about Rs 1.7–1.8 trillion, and tariffs that make industry uncompetitive.

*(f) Loss-making state enterprises.*

*(g) Human capital and population:* 2.55% growth a year; 25 million children out of school; widespread stunting; few women in paid work.

*(h) Elite capture.* The UNDP (2021) estimated that privileges enjoyed by powerful groups cost about 6% of GDP.

*(i) Political instability and insecurity,* which deter investment.

*(j) Climate shocks:* floods in 2022 and 2025; and in 2026 the oil and shipping shock of the Gulf war.

*(k) Dependence on external financing.* Over the last decade grants shrank while commercial loans, project debt and central-bank deposits from friendly states grew; external debt roughly doubled between 2013 and 2023. Borrowing built power plants and roads, but repayments in dollars were not matched by exports.

**4. The IMF programme**

After a nine-month Stand-By Arrangement in 2023 averted default, the Fund approved a 37-month **Extended Fund Facility of about $7 billion on 25 September 2024**, and a climate-linked **Resilience and Sustainability Facility of about $1.4 billion in May 2025**. The third review was completed on 8 May 2026, taking disbursements to about $4.8 billion.

Main conditions: a primary budget surplus; higher tax collection, including on agricultural income, retail and property; cost-recovery energy tariffs and a cap on circular debt; a market-based exchange rate and tight money; reform or sale of state enterprises; a fiscal pact shifting spending to provinces; no new amnesties or exemptions; governance and anti-corruption measures.

**5. Effects on stabilisation**

*Positive.* Default was avoided. Reserves rose from under $4 billion in early 2023 to about $17 billion. Inflation fell to single digits and interest rates were halved. The budget moved to a primary surplus. Ratings agencies upgraded Pakistan in 2025. The Fund's seal reopened lending by the World Bank, the ADB and Gulf partners. Provinces legislated to tax agricultural income — a reform avoided for decades.

The Resilience and Sustainability Facility also ties part of the money to climate measures, a useful push in a flood-prone country.

*Negative.* Stabilisation came through compression. Higher power and gas prices and indirect taxes raised living costs and closed factories. Development spending was cut to meet targets. Growth stayed near population growth, and poverty and unemployment rose. Revenue targets were met largely by squeezing existing taxpayers.

**6. Effects on long-term sustainability**

*How conditions can help.* They address the true weaknesses: a narrow tax base, energy losses, state-enterprise deficits and fiscal indiscipline. They give reformers cover against vested interests.

*Why they may not.* First, the Fund's horizon is the balance of payments, not development; it does not build exports or schools. Second, austerity can harm the base of future growth by cutting investment in people and infrastructure. Third, ownership is weak: past programmes were abandoned once the crisis passed, and reforms were reversed before elections. Fourth, uniform prescriptions — high tariffs to cover inefficiency rather than removing the inefficiency — can deepen the problem, as in the power sector's shrinking demand. Fifth, repeated programmes erode policy sovereignty and public trust.

*A comparative note.* Other countries have used a Fund programme once and not returned. India's programme of 1991 was followed by lasting trade and industrial reform; Indonesia and South Korea after 1998 rebuilt their banks and export sectors. In each case the programme was a platform for a national reform agenda with political backing. Pakistan's programmes have too often been treated as the agenda itself, to be endured until the money arrives.

*Judgement.* IMF conditions are a necessary discipline and an insufficient strategy. They can stabilise; only domestic reform can sustain.

**7. Way forward**

1. Tax all incomes alike; digitise collection; reduce rates for the compliant.
2. An export strategy: competitive energy, lower input tariffs, diversification into IT, engineering, food and minerals.
3. Restructure the power sector: renegotiate contracts, privatise distribution, cut losses.
4. Reform or sell state enterprises.
5. A new NFC bargain that shares burdens and rewards provincial effort.
6. Protect and raise spending on education, health, nutrition and family planning.
7. End elite subsidies and exemptions.
8. A charter of economy among parties to keep policy steady across elections.
9. Climate resilience in every project.
10. Make this the last programme by building reserves and export capacity before it ends in 2027.

**8. Conclusion**

Pakistan's economic problem is not a shortage of diagnoses. The same list of reforms has appeared in every programme document for thirty years. The IMF can lend time and impose discipline; it cannot supply the political will to tax the powerful, compete in world markets and invest in the young. The present stability is an opportunity. Whether it becomes the start of sustained growth or only a pause before the next crisis is a choice that will be made in Islamabad, not Washington.

## 11. Analyse the causes and effects of Pakistan's energy crisis. Is it a failure of strategy and political will? Suggest remedies.

**Outline**
1. Introduction
2. How the crisis changed shape
3. The present picture
4. Causes
5. Effects
6. A failure of strategy and will?
7. Remedies
8. Conclusion

**1. Introduction**

For a decade Pakistan's energy crisis meant darkness: power cuts of ten hours and more. Today the lights are on, the country has almost twice the generating capacity it uses, and yet the crisis is deeper. Electricity is among the most expensive in the region, industry is leaving the grid, and the sector's unpaid bills threaten public finances. A crisis of shortage has become a crisis of cost and of governance. Its roots lie less in a lack of resources than in decades of short-term planning and avoided decisions.

**2. How the crisis changed shape**

- *1994:* a power policy invited independent producers on imported fuel oil with dollar-indexed, guaranteed returns.
- *2006–2017:* demand outran supply; long outages cost the economy an estimated 2% of GDP a year.
- *2015–2022:* some 12,000 MW or more were added, largely under CPEC and on imported coal and LNG, again on "take-or-pay" contracts.
- *2022 onwards:* high prices and a slow economy cut demand, while households, farms and factories turned to cheap Chinese solar panels.

**3. The present picture**

The Economic Survey 2025–26 puts installed capacity at 49,651 MW, including 7,319 MW of net-metered rooftop solar, against a summer peak of about 30,000 MW and a winter demand of a third of that. Power-sector circular debt stood at about Rs 1.7–1.8 trillion in mid-2026, above the IMF's ceiling; the gas sector carries a further large stock. Thirteen older private plants have been closed or had contracts ended. The 2026 Gulf war and the near-closure of the Strait of Hormuz exposed the risk of importing fuel.

**4. Causes**

*(a) Capacity payments.* Under take-or-pay contracts the state pays fixed charges of about Rs 2 trillion a year whether plants run or not. As grid demand falls, the same bill is spread over fewer units and tariffs rise further — a spiral.

*(b) Circular debt.* Distribution companies lose 16–18% of power to theft and technical faults and fail to recover all bills; governments set tariffs below cost and pay subsidies late. Arrears pass up the chain to generators and fuel suppliers.

*(c) An imported, expensive fuel mix.* Furnace oil, LNG and imported coal displaced hydropower and domestic gas. Long-term LNG contracts now oblige Pakistan to take cargoes it does not need.

*(d) Neglect of hydropower and local resources.* Kalabagh was blocked by provincial distrust; Diamer-Bhasha and Dasu were delayed for years; Thar coal waited decades.

*(e) A weak grid.* Cheap southern power cannot reach northern load centres; a national blackout in January 2023 showed its fragility.

*(f) Depleting gas* priced far below cost for households and fertiliser.

*(g) Fragmented governance.* Many ministries, companies and regulators; no integrated plan; decisions made in emergencies.

*(h) The solar shift.* A benefit for those who can afford panels, but it leaves fixed costs to be paid by those who cannot.

*(i) External shocks:* oil prices and a falling rupee.

**5. Effects**

- *Industry and exports:* tariffs above those of competitors; closures and lost orders in textiles; self-generation.
- *Public finances:* subsidies and bail-outs above a trillion rupees a year; the chief risk to the IMF programme.
- *Households:* bills that consume a large part of income; protests in several regions.
- *External account:* fuel imports of some $15 billion or more a year.
- *Employment and growth:* lost in the shortage years and again in the high-price years.
- *Federation:* disputes over hydel profits, gas priority and loss-making distribution companies.
- *Foreign relations:* arrears to Chinese producers strain CPEC; the Iran–Pakistan pipeline is stalled by sanctions and carries a penalty risk.
- *Environment:* a coal legacy, partly offset by rising hydro, nuclear and solar.

**6. A failure of strategy and political will?**

Largely, yes.

*Strategy.* The shortage was forecast in the early 2000s and not addressed. When it came, each government bought the quickest capacity on the most generous terms, twice. Demand was over-estimated; transmission and distribution were ignored; fuel choice ignored the balance of payments.

*Political will.* Theft and non-payment were tolerated in politically sensitive areas. Tariffs were held down before elections. Distribution companies were kept public as sources of patronage. Hydropower was sacrificed to inter-provincial mistrust. Contracts were not published.

*The limits of the charge.* Oil shocks, the rupee's fall and security conditions were outside any government's control, and the plants built after 2013 did end the blackouts. But the form the crisis takes today — paying for idle capacity — is the product of choices.

**7. Remedies**

*Immediate*
1. Renegotiate contracts fairly and openly: longer debt tenors, lower returns, "take-and-pay" where possible; convert imported-coal plants to Thar coal.
2. Stop adding guaranteed capacity; procure only by competitive bidding.
3. Raise grid demand with marginal-cost tariffs for industry, electric vehicles and winter use.

*Structural*
4. Reform distribution: private management or sale, smart meters, action against theft, provincial ownership.
5. Invest in transmission, storage and a competitive wholesale market under an independent operator.
6. Redesign tariffs: fixed charges for grid access; direct support for poor households through BISP in place of general subsidies.
7. Rationalise gas pricing and cut losses.

*Long term*
8. Local and clean sources: complete Dasu, Mohmand and Diamer-Bhasha; expand wind and solar with storage; nuclear power; domestic exploration.
9. Regional energy trade when conditions allow (CASA-1000, TAPI).
10. One integrated energy plan and an empowered regulator, protected from political cycles.
11. Conservation and efficiency standards.

*A note on alternative sources.* Pakistan's hydropower potential is about 60,000 MW, of which about a sixth is in use. The Gharo–Jhimpir corridor has wind potential of tens of thousands of megawatts. Sunshine is among the best in the world. Thar holds about 175 billion tonnes of lignite. The official target is 30% of capacity from wind, solar and similar sources by 2030, in addition to hydropower. The obstacle is not resources but room: contracted thermal capacity and a weak grid leave little space for new clean power until the old contracts are restructured.

**8. Conclusion**

Pakistan has sun, wind, rivers and coal enough for its needs. Its energy crisis has been made by contracts, losses and delay. The shift to solar that ordinary citizens have carried out on their own shows how quickly change comes when incentives are right. The state's task is to match that speed in the areas only it can fix: the grid, the distribution companies and the terms on which it buys power. That requires what has been missing for thirty years — a plan that outlasts governments and the will to enforce it.

## 12. "Kashmir is the unfinished agenda of Partition." Evaluate Pakistan's Kashmir policy and discuss whether the dispute exposes the structural weakness of the UN Security Council.

**Outline**
1. Introduction
2. Why Kashmir is "unfinished business"
3. The dispute at the United Nations
4. Pakistan's Kashmir policy: phases and instruments
5. Evaluation of the policy
6. Does Kashmir expose the Security Council's weakness?
7. The position after 5 August 2019 and May 2025
8. Way forward
9. Conclusion

**1. Introduction**

Seventy-nine years after independence, Jammu and Kashmir remains divided by a military line, claimed by two nuclear powers, and denied the plebiscite the United Nations promised. For Pakistan the dispute is the unfinished business of Partition: a Muslim-majority state contiguous to Pakistan whose future was to be settled by its people. Pakistan has pursued that aim by war, diplomacy, support for resistance and dialogue. None has altered the territorial position. The failure is shared: by India's refusal, by the limits of Pakistan's own methods, and by a Security Council whose structure lets a determined state ignore its resolutions.

**2. Why Kashmir is unfinished business**

Under the 3 June Plan, princely states were to accede with regard to geography and the wishes of their people. Kashmir had a Muslim majority of about 77%, and its rivers, roads and trade ran to West Punjab. Its Hindu ruler hesitated; a revolt broke out in Poonch; tribesmen entered on 22 October 1947; the Maharaja signed an accession to India on 26 October and Indian troops landed the next day. India's Governor-General accepted the accession subject to a reference to the people. The Radcliffe award of Gurdaspur had given India its only road in. India applied the principle of the people's will in Junagadh and Hyderabad and the ruler's signature in Kashmir.

**3. At the United Nations**

India took the matter to the Security Council on 1 January 1948. **Resolution 47 (21 April 1948)** and the UNCIP resolutions of 13 August 1948 and 5 January 1949 provided for a ceasefire, demilitarisation and a free and impartial plebiscite. The ceasefire took effect on 1 January 1949. Mediators — Dixon, Graham, Jarring — failed to secure agreed demilitarisation. From 1957 the Soviet veto shielded India. After the **Simla Agreement (1972)** India has treated the issue as bilateral.

**4. Pakistan's policy: phases and instruments**

| Phase | Instrument |
|---|---|
| 1947–49 | War and appeal to the UN |
| 1950s | Diplomacy through the UN and Western allies |
| 1962–65 | Talks (Bhutto–Swaran Singh), then Operation Gibraltar and the 1965 war; Tashkent restored the status quo |
| 1972–88 | Simla: the Line of Control; the issue kept alive on paper |
| 1989–2001 | Political, moral and, as India and others charged, material support to the uprising; Kargil (1999) |
| 2004–07 | Composite dialogue and a back channel; Musharraf's four points — soft borders, self-governance, demilitarisation, joint mechanism |
| 2008–19 | Stalled talks after Mumbai; internationalising human rights abuses |
| Since 2019 | Rejection of India's revocation of Article 370; diplomatic and legal campaign; downgraded ties |

The constants: support for the right of self-determination under UN resolutions, and refusal to accept the Line of Control as a border.

**5. Evaluation**

*Achievements.* Pakistan kept the dispute on the international agenda for nearly eight decades; one-third of the state is under its administration; the UN resolutions remain the legal basis of its claim; world attention to human rights in the valley owes much to its advocacy.

*Shortcomings.*
1. **War did not succeed** in 1947–48, 1965 or 1999, and each attempt carried heavy costs.
2. **Association with militancy** in the 1990s allowed India, after 9/11, to present a freedom struggle as terrorism, and cost Pakistan diplomatic support and a place on the FATF grey list.
3. **Inconsistency:** policy swung between the UN, bilateralism, and out-of-the-box formulas with each government.
4. **Kashmiris were often spoken for, not with.**
5. **Economic weakness** reduced Pakistan's weight against a large and growing Indian market.
6. **The response to August 2019** — downgrading ties and stopping trade — imposed little cost on India.
7. **Azad Kashmir and Gilgit-Baltistan** were not made models of self-rule and development that would strengthen the case.

**6. Does Kashmir expose the Council's structural weakness?**

*Yes.*
1. The resolutions were passed under **Chapter VI**: they recommend; they do not compel.
2. The **veto** allowed one permanent member to block follow-up for decades, and today none of the five is willing to confront India.
3. **Enforcement follows interest:** the Council acted in Korea, Kuwait and East Timor; it has not on Kashmir or Palestine.
4. The Council has **no means to make a party demilitarise** or hold a plebiscite without its consent.
5. **Reform is blocked** by the same veto that causes the paralysis.

*But not only structure.* The Council did stop the first war, keeps observers on the line (UNMOGIP), and met on Kashmir in 2019 and 2025. Its inaction also reflects the parties' own acts: the bilateral commitment at Simla, and the shift in world opinion after militancy. Structure makes inaction possible; politics makes it actual.

**7. After August 2019 and May 2025**

On **5 August 2019** India revoked Articles 370 and 35A, divided the state into two Union Territories and imposed a prolonged lockdown; its Supreme Court upheld the change in December 2023. New domicile and land rules raise fears of demographic change. After the **Pahalgam attack of 22 April 2025** India suspended the Indus Waters Treaty and struck Pakistan; four days of fighting ended in a ceasefire announced by the US President on 10 May. The offer of American mediation briefly re-internationalised Kashmir, but India rejected it. As of November 2026 there is no dialogue, the treaty remains suspended, and the two states plan for a faster next round.

**8. Way forward**

1. **Stabilise first:** restore the ceasefire understanding, military hotlines and a political back channel.
2. **Keep the case legal and political:** UN resolutions, human rights mechanisms, the OIC, parliaments and courts abroad.
3. **Deny India the terrorism narrative:** no space for armed groups; a standing offer of joint or neutral investigation.
4. **Centre the Kashmiris:** include their representatives; press for release of prisoners, restored statehood and civil liberties.
5. **Revive practical measures:** cross-LoC travel and trade; the four-point ideas as a basis for an interim arrangement.
6. **Strengthen Azad Kashmir and Gilgit-Baltistan** politically and economically.
7. **Build economic strength,** the real source of diplomatic weight.
8. **Support Security Council reform** that widens elected membership and restrains the veto.

**9. Conclusion**

Kashmir is unfinished because the principle on which the subcontinent was divided was not applied there, and because the body that promised a remedy lacked the means and the will to deliver it. Pakistan's case in law and justice is strong; its instruments have often weakened it. A policy that is consistent, peaceful, Kashmiri-centred and backed by a stronger Pakistan offers the best hope that the agenda of 1947 will one day be completed by the people it concerns.

## 13. How does instability in Afghanistan affect Pakistan's internal security, regional diplomacy and counter-terrorism? Suggest policy measures.

**Outline**
1. Introduction
2. Background: four decades of war
3. From ally to adversary since 2021
4. Impact on internal security
5. Impact on counter-terrorism
6. Impact on regional diplomacy
7. Economic and humanitarian effects
8. Analysis
9. Policy measures
10. Conclusion

**1. Introduction**

No external factor has shaped Pakistan's security over the past forty-five years as much as Afghanistan. Pakistan was the base of the war against the Soviet occupation, a party to the civil war that followed, and an uneasy partner in America's twenty-year campaign. Each phase sent refugees, weapons and militants across the Durand Line. When the Taliban returned to Kabul in August 2021 many in Pakistan expected a friendly neighbour at last. Instead the two states were in open armed conflict by 2026. Afghan instability now presses on Pakistan's internal security, complicates its diplomacy and stretches its counter-terrorism effort. Force alone cannot resolve it; a regional and political approach is needed.

**2. Background**

- **1979–89:** Soviet invasion; Pakistan hosted over three million refugees and channelled Western and Arab aid to the mujahideen. Legacy: the "Kalashnikov and heroin culture", militant networks, sectarian groups.
- **1989–2001:** civil war; the Taliban, backed by Pakistan, took Kabul in 1996.
- **2001–21:** the US-led war; militants displaced into the tribal areas; the Tehrik-i-Taliban Pakistan formed in 2007; more than 80,000 Pakistanis died in the violence that followed.
- **2020–21:** the Doha Agreement, facilitated by Pakistan, and the US withdrawal.

**3. From ally to adversary**

The Afghan Taliban declined to act against the TTP, with whom they share ideology and battlefield history, and rejected the Durand Line and the border fence. Talks they hosted in 2021–22 let TTP fighters return; the group ended its ceasefire in November 2022. Pakistan responded with air strikes, the expulsion of undocumented Afghans from October 2023, and border closures. In **October 2025** a week of fighting ended in a ceasefire brokered by Qatar and Turkey. It broke down. On **27 February 2026** Pakistan struck Kabul, Kandahar and Paktia and its defence minister spoke of "open war". An Eid truce and week-long talks hosted by China at **Urumqi (1–7 April 2026)** produced a pledge to avoid escalation but no agreement on the TTP. Clashes resumed in late October 2026.

**4. Impact on internal security**

1. **Resurgent terrorism.** Think tanks recorded over a thousand militant attacks in 2025, the deadliest year in a decade, and the Global Terrorism Index 2026 ranked Pakistan first. Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and Balochistan bear over 90% of the violence.
2. **Better-armed militants,** using American equipment abandoned in 2021: night-vision devices, sniper rifles, drones.
3. **Erosion of the state's writ** in the merged districts: extortion, targeted killings of police and elders, displacement.
4. **Links with Baloch insurgents,** who also find space across the border.
5. **Political strain.** Disagreement between the federal government and the PTI-led provincial government over operations and talks was a stated ground for the emergency in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa in October 2026.
6. **Sectarian and ethnic tension,** as in Kurram, and resentment among Pashtuns who feel collectively suspected.

**5. Impact on counter-terrorism**

- The gains of Zarb-i-Azb (2014) and the National Action Plan have been partly reversed.
- Militants enjoy sanctuary beyond the reach of ground forces; cross-border strikes carry diplomatic and civilian costs.
- The army and police are stretched across two provinces while the eastern border also demands attention after May 2025.
- IS-Khorasan, hostile to both governments, exploits the confusion.
- Civilian capacity — police, prosecution, NACTA — remains weak; the non-military points of the Action Plan lag.
- Operations without local consent feed resentment and recruitment.

**6. Impact on regional diplomacy**

1. **Loss of leverage.** Pakistan can no longer deliver the Taliban, and the Taliban no longer need Pakistan.
2. **Dependence on mediators:** China (the Urumqi process), Qatar, Turkey and Saudi Arabia.
3. **India's return to Kabul:** New Delhi upgraded its mission in 2025 and received the Taliban foreign minister, reviving Pakistan's fear of encirclement.
4. **Blocked connectivity:** the Uzbekistan–Afghanistan–Pakistan railway, CASA-1000, TAPI and the extension of CPEC all wait on peace.
5. **Friction with the UN and rights bodies** over deportations and civilian casualties.
6. **Opportunity:** every neighbour — China, Iran, Russia, the Central Asian states — shares Pakistan's fear of militancy from Afghanistan. Pakistan's SCO chairmanship in 2026–27 gives it a platform.

**7. Economic and humanitarian effects**

Closed crossings halt trade once worth billions of dollars a year and ruin border communities. Smuggling distorts markets. Over a million Afghans have been returned since late 2023 to a country in economic collapse, and millions remain. Insecurity deters investment in mining and CPEC.

**8. Analysis**

First, the present danger is in part blowback: the policy of "strategic depth" nurtured forces that outgrew their sponsor. Second, the Taliban are not a proxy; they are a nationalist-religious movement with their own interests, now recognised by Russia and engaged by China and the Gulf. Third, air power can punish but cannot uproot a movement embedded in tribal society; it also hardens Afghan opinion. Fourth, the problem is half internal: the TTP feeds on the gaps left by the incomplete merger of the tribal areas. Fifth, Pakistan's best cards are economic and diplomatic: transit, trade, recognition, and the weight of regional consensus.

**9. Policy measures**

*Towards Afghanistan*
1. Pursue a **verifiable arrangement on the TTP** through the Urumqi process: relocation from the border, disarmament, and monitoring by guarantor states.
2. Keep diplomatic channels open in all circumstances.
3. Offer **incentives** — trade facilitation, transit to ports, energy projects — tied to action, and separate humanitarian trade from the dispute.
4. Build a **regional compact** through the SCO and the neighbours' forum linking recognition and aid to counter-terrorism commitments.
5. Seek joint management of the border without demanding formal recognition as a precondition.

*At home*
6. **Police-led, intelligence-based counter-terrorism;** fund CTDs, forensics and prosecution; complete the National Action Plan.
7. **Honour the merger:** courts, policing, schools, the promised development funds.
8. **Political consensus:** restore elected government in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa; involve its representatives and elders in security decisions.
9. **A lawful, phased refugee policy** with documentation.
10. **Modern border management** that blocks militants and keeps lawful trade moving.
11. **A declared Afghan policy debated in Parliament.**

**10. Conclusion**

Pakistan cannot choose its neighbours or wall itself off from them. Afghanistan's instability will keep reaching across the border until Kabul sees more to gain from cooperation than from sheltering Pakistan's enemies, and until Pakistan's own border regions are governed well enough to deny militants a welcome. The measures required — patient diplomacy with regional backing, and political and administrative repair at home — are slower than air strikes. They are also the only ones that have a chance of lasting.

## 14. Examine how the Indus Waters Treaty of 1960 shaped water sharing and relations between Pakistan and India. What are the implications of India holding it "in abeyance", and what should Pakistan do?

**Outline**
1. Introduction
2. Background to the treaty
3. Main provisions
4. How it shaped water sharing
5. How it shaped bilateral relations
6. Disputes under the treaty
7. The crisis since April 2025
8. Implications for Pakistan
9. Pakistan's own water management
10. What Pakistan should do
11. Conclusion

**1. Introduction**

Pakistan is a river-made country: the Indus system irrigates the land that feeds 240 million people and supplies over nine-tenths of its water to agriculture. More than three-quarters of that water rises outside its borders. The Indus Waters Treaty, signed at Karachi on 19 September 1960, gave this dependence a legal foundation that survived three wars. India's decision on 23 April 2025 to hold the treaty "in abeyance" has removed that certainty. The treaty divided the rivers successfully but never built trust; its suspension is a political act that requires both a legal defence abroad and long-delayed reform at home.

**2. Background**

The Radcliffe line placed the headworks at Madhopur and Ferozepur in India and the canals they fed in Pakistan. On 1 April 1948 East Punjab cut the supply; an interim agreement of 4 May 1948 restored it for payment. From 1951 the World Bank mediated. After nine years, President Ayub Khan, Prime Minister Nehru and the Bank's W.A.B. Iliff signed the treaty.

**3. Main provisions**

- **Eastern rivers** (Ravi, Beas, Sutlej) for India's unrestricted use.
- **Western rivers** (Indus, Jhelum, Chenab) for Pakistan; India may use them for domestic purposes, limited irrigation and run-of-river hydropower within design limits.
- A ten-year **transition** and an internationally financed fund for replacement works in Pakistan — Mangla and Tarbela dams and link canals.
- A **Permanent Indus Commission** for data exchange and inspection.
- A three-step **dispute procedure**: Commission, Neutral Expert, Court of Arbitration.
- **No expiry and no provision for unilateral suspension;** change only by a new agreed treaty.

**4. Effect on water sharing**

Pakistan obtained about four-fifths of the system's flow by volume and the security to build the world's largest contiguous irrigation network. It also gave up three rivers entirely, and the southern Punjab lands they watered became dependent on link canals. As the lower riparian on the western rivers it relies on India's observance of design limits, since several run-of-river projects together can affect the timing of flows in the sowing season.

**5. Effect on relations**

The treaty is the one major India–Pakistan agreement that functioned through the wars of 1965, 1971 and 1999. The Commission met even when ambassadors were withdrawn. But it was a divorce settlement, not a partnership: it divided rivers and required no joint development. Each side came to resent it — Pakistan for the loss of the eastern rivers and India's upstream projects, India for receiving a fifth of the water and for limits on development in Kashmir.

**6. Disputes**

- **Baglihar** (Chenab): a Neutral Expert in 2007 largely upheld India's design.
- **Kishanganga:** the Court of Arbitration in 2013 allowed diversion but required a minimum flow and barred drawdown flushing.
- **Kishanganga and Ratle designs:** from 2016 Pakistan sought a court and India a neutral expert; the Bank launched both in 2022 and India boycotted the court.
- India served notices in 2023 and 2024 seeking renegotiation.

**7. The crisis since April 2025**

A day after the Pahalgam attack India declared the treaty in abeyance until Pakistan ended support for cross-border terrorism, stopped sharing data, flushed reservoirs on the Chenab without notice and announced accelerated projects. Pakistan said that any diversion of its water would be treated as an act of war and that the treaty contains no suspension clause. The Court of Arbitration held in 2025 that India's step did not affect its competence and, on interpretation, supported Pakistan's reading of India's obligations; India rejects the court. In November 2026 India's foreign ministry repeated that its position was unchanged, and no talks are scheduled.

**8. Implications for Pakistan**

*Short term.* India lacks the storage to stop the western rivers. It can alter timing — holding and releasing water — and withhold flood data, which threatens sowing and flood warning.

*Long term.* If India builds storage and diversion works over the next decade, a real ability to reduce flows would follow.

*Legal.* A precedent that a treaty with no exit clause can be shelved damages every lower riparian, and India itself is downstream of China.

*Strategic.* Water has joined the instruments of coercion between two nuclear states. Pakistan's declared red line raises the stakes of any future crisis.

*Domestic.* The shock has exposed how little storage and efficiency Pakistan has.

**9. Pakistan's own management**

Per-person availability has fallen from about 5,000 cubic metres in 1951 to under 1,000. Storage covers about thirty days. About half of canal water is lost before reaching crops. Dams have been delayed by provincial mistrust, and the 1991 Water Apportionment Accord is disputed in years of shortage; in April 2025 the CCI had to shelve a canals plan after protests in Sindh. External vulnerability is multiplied by internal waste and distrust.

**10. What Pakistan should do**

*Externally*
1. Hold to the treaty and its procedures; pursue the Court of Arbitration and engage the World Bank as signatory.
2. Raise the precedent at the UN and with states that share rivers.
3. Seek restoration of data-sharing as a first confidence step, through third parties if needed.
4. Remain open to discussing climate change and environmental flows inside the treaty framework.
5. Lower the temperature: keep water out of war talk and tie it to a broader crisis-management track.
6. Begin a basin understanding with Afghanistan on the Kabul River when relations permit.

*Internally*
7. Complete Diamer-Bhasha, Dasu and Mohmand; add small dams and groundwater recharge.
8. Cut losses: canal lining, laser levelling, drip irrigation, realistic water pricing, less thirsty crops.
9. Trusted telemetry on every barrage and updated operating rules under the 1991 Accord through the CCI.
10. Regulate groundwater; treat and reuse wastewater.
11. Implement the National Water Policy (2018).

**11. Conclusion**

For sixty-five years the Indus Waters Treaty was proof that even bitter rivals could keep an agreement where survival was at stake. Its suspension shows that law without trust is fragile. Pakistan's legal case is strong and should be pressed in every forum. But the lasting answer to upstream leverage is a country that stores, saves and shares its own water wisely — and a relationship with India in which rivers are once again kept apart from conflict.

## 15. Discuss the changing trends in Pakistan–US relations since 2001. What are the prospects for a stable relationship?

**Outline**
1. Introduction
2. The pattern before 2001
3. Phases since 2001
4. What Pakistan gained and lost
5. Sources of mistrust
6. The reset of 2025–26
7. Prospects and conditions for stability
8. Conclusion

**1. Introduction**

Pakistan's relationship with the United States has been described as a marriage of convenience: intense when Washington needs Pakistan for a purpose, cold when the purpose is served. The years since 2001 repeated the cycle in full — alliance, suspicion, rupture, neglect and, since 2025, a renewed warmth. The relationship has been transactional because it rested each time on a single issue. It will become stable only if it is rebuilt on trade, investment and a frank acceptance of where interests differ.

**2. The pattern before 2001**

- 1954–65: alliance through SEATO and CENTO; aid; then an arms embargo in the 1965 war.
- 1971: a "tilt" without help.
- 1979–89: partnership against the Soviets in Afghanistan.
- 1990–2001: Pressler sanctions over the nuclear programme; further sanctions after the 1998 tests and the 1999 coup.

**3. Phases since 2001**

*(a) Alliance, 2001–07.* After 9/11 General Musharraf joined the US-led campaign, providing airspace, bases, supply routes and intelligence, and arresting al-Qaeda figures. Sanctions were lifted, debt rescheduled, and Pakistan named a major non-NATO ally in 2004.

*(b) "Do more", 2008–10.* As the Afghan insurgency grew, Washington accused Pakistan of sheltering the Afghan Taliban. Drone strikes multiplied. The Kerry–Lugar–Berman Act (2009) offered $7.5 billion of civilian aid with conditions that the military resented.

*(c) Breakdown, 2011.* The Raymond Davis affair, the raid at Abbottabad that killed Osama bin Laden on 2 May, and the Salala attack of 26 November, which killed 24 Pakistani soldiers, brought relations to their lowest point. Pakistan closed NATO's supply routes for seven months.

*(d) Drift, 2012–17.* Routes reopened; aid declined; the United States deepened its partnership with India.

In these years Washington signed defence and technology agreements with New Delhi, backed India's entry into export-control regimes, and treated Pakistan mainly as a problem of Afghan policy.

*(e) Rupture and transaction, 2018–21.* President Trump suspended security assistance in January 2018, then sought Pakistan's help to bring the Taliban to the table. The Doha Agreement (February 2020) and the withdrawal of August 2021 followed.

*(f) Neglect, 2021–24.* With no war to supply, Washington lost interest. Pakistan was blamed for the Taliban's victory; in Pakistan the 2022 "cipher" controversy fed the belief in American interference.

*(g) Reset, 2025–26.* Cooperation against IS-Khorasan, Washington's role in the May 2025 ceasefire with India, a trade understanding with a 19% tariff, a memorandum on critical minerals (September 2025), US financing for Reko Diq, and Pakistan's mediation in the 2026 US–Iran war have produced the closest ties in over a decade.

**4. Gains and losses**

*Gains:* about $33 billion in assistance and reimbursements between 2002 and 2018; debt relief; military equipment; access to the largest market for Pakistani exports; periods of diplomatic support.

*Losses:* more than 80,000 lives and economic damage the government estimates above $150 billion; the rise of the TTP; drone strikes and raids that breached sovereignty; radicalisation; dependence that postponed reform; and a public opinion deeply distrustful of the United States.

*Was joining the war on terror worth it?* In the short term it rescued a sanctioned economy and an isolated government. In the long term the costs were far greater than the aid, and they fell on ordinary citizens and soldiers. The deeper failure was of management: the terms of cooperation were never debated in Parliament or written down, permissions were given in secret, and no plan existed for the tribal areas into which the war spilled.

**5. Sources of mistrust**

| American concerns | Pakistani concerns |
|---|---|
| Sanctuary for the Afghan Taliban and Haqqani network during the war | Sacrifices unrecognised; blamed for America's failure |
| The A.Q. Khan network; the missile programme (sanctions of December 2024) | Discriminatory treatment: a nuclear deal for India, sanctions for Pakistan |
| Closeness to China; CPEC | The US built India up as a regional power and ignored Kashmir |
| Militant groups focused on India | Abandoned after 1989 and after 2021 |
| Democracy and human rights | Selective and intrusive conditionality |

Beneath these lies a mismatch of aims: the United States has seen Pakistan through other files — the Soviet Union, al-Qaeda, Afghanistan, China — while Pakistan has sought a counterweight to India that Washington was never willing to be.

**6. The reset assessed**

The crisis of May 2025 illustrates the change. Washington, which in 2019 had largely backed India's position, intervened this time as a crisis manager acceptable to Islamabad, and the US President's public offers to mediate on Kashmir — rejected by India — were welcomed in Pakistan.

*Strengths:* it is not tied to Afghanistan; it includes economic content — minerals, energy, technology; Pakistan has shown value as a diplomatic channel to Tehran; the strain in US–India ties over tariffs in 2025 widened Pakistan's room.

*Weaknesses:* it depends heavily on personal relations at the top and on the military channel; no trade agreement has been concluded; the mineral promise is unproven and located in insecure regions; sanctions on missile entities remain; Congress and the strategic community remain sceptical; and it must coexist with Pakistan's partnership with China. A shift in US politics or a deal between Washington and New Delhi could cool it quickly.

**7. Prospects and conditions for stability**

1. **Broaden the base:** trade and investment first — textiles, IT, agriculture, energy, minerals — with a concluded trade agreement.
2. **De-hyphenate:** a relationship valued for itself, not as a function of Afghanistan, India or China.
3. **Balance, not choice:** Pakistan should state plainly that it will keep its partnership with China and work with the United States where interests meet.
4. **Counter-terrorism cooperation** on shared threats, with transparency.
5. **Institutionalise:** regular strategic, economic and defence dialogues; parliamentary and people-to-people links; use of the diaspora.
6. **Manage differences openly:** nuclear and missile issues through dialogue, not sanctions and silence.
7. **Parliamentary ownership** in Pakistan, so that commitments survive changes of government.
8. **Economic strength at home,** so that Pakistan is a partner and not a petitioner.

9. **Use multilateral settings** — the UN Security Council, where Pakistan sits in 2025–26, and regional diplomacy on Iran and Gaza — to show that cooperation with Pakistan serves wider stability.

**8. Conclusion**

Since 2001 Pakistan and the United States have moved from alliance to estrangement and back, without ever settling into trust. The lesson of seven decades is that a relationship built on one crisis ends with that crisis. The present opening gives both sides a chance to build something more durable — modest in expectation, wide in content and honest about limits. For Pakistan, the surest way to keep Washington's regard is the same as the way to keep Beijing's: a stable, solvent country that others need for what it is, not only for where it lies.
