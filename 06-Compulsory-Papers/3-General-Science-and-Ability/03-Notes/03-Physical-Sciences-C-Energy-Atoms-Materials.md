## §1 Sources of energy: classification

**Energy** is the capacity to do work. Unit: joule (J). Power is the rate of using energy. Unit: watt (W). Electricity is sold in kilowatt-hours (1 kWh = 3.6 million J = one "unit").

| Point | Renewable | Non-renewable |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Replaced by nature as fast as it is used | Formed over millions of years; finite |
| Examples | Solar, wind, hydro, geothermal, biomass, tidal, wave | Coal, oil, natural gas, nuclear (uranium) |
| Supply | Practically unlimited | Will run out |
| Pollution | Low; little or no CO₂ in use | High; CO₂, SO₂, NOx, ash (nuclear: radioactive waste) |
| Cost | High to build, low to run | Lower to build, fuel cost continues |
| Reliability | Many depend on weather and site | Available on demand |
| Energy density | Low; needs large area | High |

**Advantages of renewables:** clean, no fuel import bill, low running cost, suited to remote areas, create local jobs, cut greenhouse gases.
**Limits:** intermittent (need storage or backup), site-specific, large land area, high first cost, grid upgrades needed.

**Pakistan.** Installed capacity is roughly 45,000 MW. Thermal plants (gas, imported LNG, coal, furnace oil) are over half of it; hydropower about a quarter; nuclear under a tenth; wind, solar and bagasse the rest. Rooftop solar has grown very fast since 2022. Problems: circular debt, costly imported fuel, line losses of about 16–17%, capacity payments. Prospects (asked 2021): Gharo–Jhimpir wind corridor in Sindh (potential about 50,000 MW), high solar irradiance across Balochistan, Sindh and south Punjab, large untapped hydropower on the Indus (about 60,000 MW potential), biogas from livestock dung, bagasse from sugar mills, Thar coal.

## §2 Renewable sources

### 2.1 Solar energy

Two routes: **photovoltaic (PV)** makes electricity directly; **solar thermal** uses heat (water heaters, cookers, concentrated solar power with mirrors and a steam turbine).

**How a PV cell works (asked 2026)**
1. A cell is a slice of silicon with two layers: n-type (extra electrons) on top and p-type (holes) below. Where they meet is the **p-n junction**, which has a built-in electric field.
2. A photon with enough energy (at least the band gap, 1.1 eV for silicon) knocks an electron free. This creates an electron–hole pair. This is the **photovoltaic effect**.
3. The junction field pushes electrons to the n-side and holes to the p-side.
4. Connect the two sides through a wire and electrons flow: **direct current (DC)**.
5. An **inverter** changes DC to AC for the grid. Cells are joined into modules (panels) and panels into arrays.

```
        sunlight (photons)
     \    \    \    \
  =========================  glass + anti-reflective coat
  ---- metal contact grid ----        (-)
  |      n-type silicon     |  ---> electrons out
  |-------p-n junction------|             [load]
  |      p-type silicon     |  <--- electrons return
  ------ back contact -------        (+)
```

Commercial panel efficiency is about 18–23%.

**Factors affecting output**

| Factor | Effect |
|---|---|
| Irradiance (sunlight intensity) | Current and power rise almost in proportion. Panels are rated at 1,000 W/m². Cloud, haze and dust cut output. |
| Orientation and tilt | Maximum when rays hit at right angles. In Pakistan face panels south and tilt them at about the latitude (roughly 25°–33°). Sun trackers add 20–35%. |
| Temperature | Hot cells give lower voltage. Output drops about 0.3–0.5% for each °C above 25 °C. Cool, bright days are best. |
| Others | Shading of even one cell, dust, age, wiring and inverter losses. |

Pros: silent, no fuel, modular, low maintenance, falling cost. Cons: works only by day, needs batteries or grid, land area, panel waste at end of life.
Pakistan: Quaid-e-Azam Solar Park, Bahawalpur (first 100 MW in 2015); net metering for rooftop systems.

### 2.2 Wind energy

- Moving air has kinetic energy. It turns the blades (by aerodynamic lift), the blades turn a shaft, a gearbox speeds it up, and a generator makes electricity. (MCQ 2026: wind turbines convert **kinetic** energy.)
- Power available: P = ½ ρ A v³. Power depends on the **cube of wind speed**: double the speed gives eight times the power. A is the area swept by the blades.
- No turbine can capture more than 59.3% of the wind's energy (Betz limit).
- Turbines start at about 3–4 m/s, reach rated power near 12–15 m/s and shut down at about 25 m/s for safety.
- Parts: tower, blades, hub, nacelle (gearbox, generator), yaw system, anemometer.
- Pros: clean, cheap to run, land below can be farmed, offshore sites have strong steady wind. Cons: variable, noise, visual impact, bird and bat deaths, far from cities.
- Pakistan: wind farms at Jhimpir and Gharo, Sindh.

### 2.3 Hydroelectric power (asked 2020, 2026)

**Principle.** Water stored at a height has gravitational potential energy. As it falls through a pipe (penstock) this becomes kinetic energy, which spins a turbine. The turbine drives a generator that converts mechanical energy to electricity.

Power: P = η ρ g Q h (efficiency × water density × g × flow rate × head). More head and more flow give more power.

```
 Reservoir ~~~~~~|
 (potential E)   | DAM
                 |\__ penstock __
                 |               \--> [TURBINE]--shaft--[GENERATOR] ---> transformer ---> grid
                                         |
                                     tailrace ---> river
```

Types: storage dam, run-of-river (little storage), pumped storage (pumps water up at night, releases at peak time; acts as a battery).

**Advantages:** renewable, no fuel, very cheap per unit once built, quick to start and stop, long life, dams also give irrigation and flood control.

**Constraints**

| Environmental | Geographical and other |
|---|---|
| Floods valleys, forests and farmland | Needs a steady large flow and a good head (mountains, narrow valleys) |
| Displaces people | Sites are far from cities; long transmission lines |
| Blocks fish migration; changes river ecology | Flow is seasonal (low in winter in Pakistan) |
| Traps silt; delta downstream starves and erodes | Siltation cuts reservoir life (Tarbela) |
| Rotting plants in reservoirs release methane | Earthquake risk in mountain zones |
| Changes downstream flow and water quality | Very high first cost; 8–10 years to build; disputes between provinces and countries |

Pakistan: Tarbela (Indus; largest earth-filled dam; about 4,900 MW), Mangla (Jhelum), Ghazi Barotha (1,450 MW), Neelum–Jhelum (969 MW). Under construction: Diamer-Bhasha, Dasu, Mohmand.

### 2.4 Geothermal energy (asked 2020)

- Heat from inside the Earth (from radioactive decay and heat left from its formation). Temperature rises about 25–30 °C per km of depth.
- Wells bring up hot water or steam. Steam spins a turbine. Cooled water is pumped back.
- Plant types: dry steam, flash steam, binary cycle (a second fluid with a low boiling point).
- Also used directly for heating homes and greenhouses.
- Pros: available day and night, small land use, low emissions. Cons: only near plate boundaries and hot spots, high drilling cost, may release H₂S, can cause small tremors.
- Leaders: USA, Indonesia, Philippines, Türkiye, New Zealand, Kenya, Iceland.

### 2.5 Other renewables

| Source | How it works | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Biomass and biogas | Burning or fermenting plant and animal matter | See Biological Sciences §24 |
| Tidal | Rise and fall of tides drives turbines in a barrage | La Rance, France |
| Wave | Motion of surface waves | Experimental |
| Hydrogen | Burned or used in fuel cells; only water is produced | "Green hydrogen" is made by electrolysis using renewable power |

## §3 LED lighting (the syllabus says "LED Energy")

- **LED = light-emitting diode.** A semiconductor p-n junction that gives light when current flows in the forward direction.
- **How:** electrons from the n-side cross the junction and recombine with holes on the p-side. Each recombination releases energy as a photon. This is **electroluminescence**. (MCQ 2026: light is produced by electron–hole recombination.)
- The colour depends on the band gap of the material. White light: a blue LED (gallium nitride) coated with a yellow phosphor. The blue LED won the 2014 Nobel Prize in Physics.

| Lamp | Light per watt (approx.) | Life (hours) | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Incandescent bulb | 10–17 lumens | 1,000 | About 90% of energy lost as heat |
| CFL (energy saver) | 50–70 lumens | 8,000 | Contains mercury |
| LED | 100–150+ lumens | 25,000–50,000 | No mercury, instant light, cool |

- An LED uses about 75–85% less electricity than an incandescent bulb for the same light.
- Uses: lighting, screens, traffic signals, car lamps, remote controls (infrared LEDs), fibre-optic transmitters.
- A solar cell and an LED are opposites: one turns light into current, the other turns current into light.

## §4 Non-renewable sources

**Fossil fuels** formed from the remains of plants and tiny sea organisms buried and heated under pressure for millions of years.

| Fuel | Notes | Pakistan |
|---|---|---|
| Coal | Solid; mostly carbon. Ranks from low to high: peat, lignite, bituminous, anthracite. Dirtiest fuel: most CO₂ per unit of energy, plus SO₂, ash, mercury. | Thar (Sindh) has about 175 billion tonnes of lignite |
| Oil (petroleum) | Liquid mix of hydrocarbons. Separated by fractional distillation into LPG, petrol, kerosene, diesel, furnace oil, bitumen. | Small fields in Potwar and Sindh; most oil is imported |
| Natural gas | Mainly methane (CH₄). Cleanest fossil fuel. CNG and LNG are compressed and liquefied forms. | Sui, Balochistan (found 1952); reserves falling; LNG imported |
| Nuclear | Fission of uranium-235. Not a fossil fuel but finite. No CO₂ in operation. | Karachi (K-2, K-3, about 1,100 MW each) and Chashma (four units) |

**Nuclear fission.** A slow neutron hits a U-235 nucleus. It splits into two smaller nuclei, 2–3 neutrons and a large amount of energy. The new neutrons split more nuclei: a **chain reaction**.
Reactor parts: fuel rods (enriched uranium), moderator (water or graphite; slows neutrons), control rods (boron or cadmium; absorb neutrons), coolant, steam generator, turbine, thick concrete containment.
Problems: radioactive waste, accident risk (Chernobyl 1986, Fukushima 2011), high cost, weapons proliferation.

**Problems of fossil fuels:** finite; CO₂ and global warming; SO₂ and NOx cause acid rain and smog; oil spills; mining damage; import bills and price shocks.

## §5 Energy conservation and sustainable use

- **Energy conservation:** using less energy by cutting waste (switching off, sharing transport).
- **Energy efficiency:** getting the same service from less energy (LED instead of a bulb).
- **Sustainable use:** meeting today's needs without harming the ability of future generations to meet theirs. UN Sustainable Development Goal 7: affordable and clean energy for all.

| Sector | Measures |
|---|---|
| Homes and offices | LED lights, inverter air-conditioners and fans, insulation, shading, solar water heaters, set AC to 26 °C, switch off standby loads |
| Transport | Public transport, car pooling, tuned engines and correct tyre pressure, electric and hybrid vehicles, cycling |
| Industry | Efficient motors and boilers, waste-heat recovery, cogeneration, energy audits |
| Power sector | Cut transmission and distribution losses and theft, smart meters, time-of-use tariffs, replace old plants |
| Agriculture | Efficient and solar tube-well pumps, drip irrigation |
| Policy | Appliance efficiency labels, building energy codes, awareness campaigns, pricing that rewards saving |

Pakistan: the National Energy Efficiency and Conservation Authority (NEECA) works under the 2016 Act. A unit saved is cheaper than a unit generated.

## §6 Atomic structure

**Atom:** the smallest particle of an element that keeps its chemical properties. Size about 10⁻¹⁰ m. The nucleus is about 100,000 times smaller but holds almost all the mass.

| Particle | Charge | Relative mass | Place | Discovered by |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Proton | +1 | 1 | Nucleus | Rutherford (1919) |
| Neutron | 0 | 1 | Nucleus | Chadwick (1932) |
| Electron | −1 | 1/1836 | Shells around nucleus | J. J. Thomson (1897) |

- **Atomic number (Z)** = number of protons. It fixes the element.
- **Mass number (A)** = protons + neutrons. Neutrons = A − Z.
- A neutral atom has equal protons and electrons. An **ion** has gained or lost electrons.
- **Isotopes:** atoms of the same element (same Z) with different numbers of neutrons. Examples: hydrogen-1, deuterium (H-2), tritium (H-3); carbon-12 and carbon-14; uranium-235 and uranium-238.

**Atomic models**

| Model | Year | Idea | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dalton | 1803 | Atom is a solid, indivisible sphere | Atoms have parts |
| Thomson | 1904 | "Plum pudding": electrons set in a positive sphere | No nucleus |
| Rutherford | 1911 | Gold-foil experiment: tiny, dense, positive nucleus; electrons orbit; mostly empty space | Orbiting electrons should lose energy and spiral in |
| Bohr | 1913 | Electrons move in fixed energy levels (shells); light is emitted or absorbed when they jump | Works only for hydrogen |
| Quantum mechanical (Schrödinger, Heisenberg) | 1926 | Electrons are described by orbitals: regions of high probability | Current model |

**Electron arrangement**
- Shells K, L, M, N hold up to 2n² electrons: 2, 8, 18, 32.
- Sub-shells: s (2), p (6), d (10), f (14).
- Example: sodium (Z = 11) is 2, 8, 1 or 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s¹.
- **Valence electrons** (outer shell) decide chemical behaviour and the group in the periodic table.

**Diagram:** small circle "nucleus (p⁺, n)"; three rings labelled K, L, M with 2, 8, 1 dots for sodium.

### Radioactivity (asked 2020, 2022, 2024)

**Definition.** The spontaneous emission of radiation from an unstable nucleus. Found by Henri Becquerel (1896); studied by Marie and Pierre Curie.

| Radiation | Nature | Charge | Penetration | Ionising power |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Alpha (α) | Helium nucleus (2p + 2n) | +2 | Stopped by paper or skin | Highest |
| Beta (β) | Fast electron | −1 | Stopped by a few mm of aluminium | Medium |
| Gamma (γ) | Electromagnetic wave | 0 | Reduced by thick lead or concrete | Lowest |

**Laws of radioactivity**
1. **Decay is spontaneous and random.** Heat, pressure or chemical state do not change it.
2. **Decay law.** The rate of decay is proportional to the number of undecayed nuclei: N = N₀ e^(−λt).
3. **Half-life** is the time for half the nuclei to decay: T½ = 0.693/λ. After n half-lives the fraction left is (½)ⁿ.
4. **Displacement law (Soddy–Fajans).** Alpha emission lowers Z by 2 and A by 4. Beta emission raises Z by 1 and leaves A the same. Gamma emission changes neither.

Example: uranium-238 → thorium-234 + alpha.

| Point | Natural radioactivity | Artificial (induced) radioactivity |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Unstable nuclei found in nature decay by themselves | A stable nucleus is made radioactive by bombarding it with neutrons, alpha particles or protons |
| Elements | Mostly heavy (Z above 82): uranium, thorium, radium, radon; also potassium-40, carbon-14 | Any element; made in reactors and accelerators |
| Discovery | Becquerel, 1896 | Irène and Frédéric Joliot-Curie, 1934 |
| Control | Cannot be controlled | Can be produced and controlled |
| Examples | U-238, Ra-226, Th-232 | Cobalt-60, iodine-131, technetium-99m, phosphorus-32 |

Units: becquerel (1 decay per second); sievert for dose.
Uses: cancer treatment (Co-60), thyroid treatment (I-131), medical scans (Tc-99m), carbon dating (C-14, half-life 5,730 years), nuclear power, tracers, sterilising equipment and food, smoke detectors.
Hazards: burns, radiation sickness, cancer, genetic damage. Protect by time, distance and shielding.

**Fission vs fusion:** fission splits a heavy nucleus (reactors, atomic bomb). Fusion joins light nuclei (Sun, hydrogen bomb) and needs millions of degrees.

## §7 Chemical bonding

**Why atoms bond (asked 2019).** Atoms are most stable with a full outer shell (8 electrons, the octet rule; 2 for hydrogen and helium). Noble gases already have this and are unreactive. Other atoms lose, gain or share electrons to reach it. Bonded atoms have lower energy than separate atoms.

| Point | Ionic bond | Covalent bond | Metallic bond |
|---|---|---|---|
| Formed by | Transfer of electrons | Sharing of electron pairs | Positive ions in a "sea" of free electrons |
| Between | Metal + non-metal | Non-metal + non-metal | Metal atoms |
| Force | Attraction between opposite ions | Attraction of both nuclei for shared electrons | Attraction between ions and free electrons |
| Examples | NaCl, MgO, CaCl₂, KBr | H₂, Cl₂, H₂O, CH₄, CO₂, NH₃ | Cu, Fe, Al |
| Melting point | High | Usually low (but diamond, a giant structure, is very high) | Usually high |
| Solubility | In water | In organic solvents | Insoluble |
| Conducts electricity | When molten or dissolved | Usually no | Yes |
| Other | Hard, brittle crystals | Gases, liquids or soft solids | Malleable, ductile, shiny |

**Ionic example.** Na (2,8,1) gives one electron to Cl (2,8,7). Na⁺ (2,8) and Cl⁻ (2,8,8) attract.
**Covalent example.** In H₂O, oxygen shares one pair with each hydrogen. Single bond: H–H. Double: O=O, O=C=O. Triple: N≡N.
- **Polar covalent:** electrons shared unequally (H₂O, HCl). **Non-polar:** shared equally (H₂, CH₄).
- **Coordinate (dative) bond:** both shared electrons come from one atom (NH₄⁺).
- **Hydrogen bond:** weak attraction between a hydrogen attached to N, O or F and a lone pair on a nearby N, O or F. It explains water's high boiling point, why ice floats, and the pairing of DNA strands.

**Shape of the water molecule (asked 2016)**
- Oxygen has 6 valence electrons. Two form bonds with hydrogen; four remain as two lone pairs.
- Oxygen's orbitals mix into four sp³ hybrids pointing to the corners of a tetrahedron. Two overlap with hydrogen 1s orbitals (bond pairs). Two hold lone pairs.
- Lone pairs repel more strongly than bond pairs. They squeeze the H–O–H angle from 109.5° to **104.5°**.
- Result: a **bent (V-shaped)** molecule. Oxygen carries a slight negative charge and the hydrogens slight positive charges, so water is polar.

```
        ..   ..        (two lone pairs on O)
          \ /
           O   (δ−)
          / \
   (δ+) H     H (δ+)      angle H–O–H = 104.5°
```

## §8 Electromagnetic radiation

**Definition.** Energy that travels as waves of oscillating electric and magnetic fields. The two fields are at right angles to each other and to the direction of travel (transverse wave).

Properties:
- Needs no medium. Travels through a vacuum.
- Speed in a vacuum: c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s for all types.
- **c = f λ** (speed = frequency × wavelength). Higher frequency means shorter wavelength.
- Energy of one photon: **E = h f** (h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s). Higher frequency means more energy.
- Shows reflection, refraction, diffraction, interference, polarisation.
- Behaves as both wave and particle (photon).

### The electromagnetic spectrum (low to high frequency)

| Type | Wavelength | Source | Uses | Hazard |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Radio waves | Over 1 m | Oscillating circuits, antennas | Radio and TV broadcast, communication, MRI, radio astronomy | None known at normal levels |
| Microwaves | 1 mm – 1 m | Magnetron, klystron | Microwave ovens, mobile phones, Wi-Fi, radar, satellite links | Heating of tissue |
| Infrared | 700 nm – 1 mm | Warm bodies | Remote controls, thermal cameras and night vision, heaters, fibre-optic signals, weather satellites | Burns |
| Visible light | 400–700 nm | Sun, lamps, LEDs | Sight, photosynthesis, photography, lasers | Eye damage from intense light |
| Ultraviolet | 10–400 nm | Sun, mercury lamps | Sterilising water and tools, vitamin D formation in skin, detecting fake notes, curing inks | Sunburn, skin cancer, cataract |
| X-rays | 0.01–10 nm | X-ray tubes | Imaging bones and teeth, CT scans, airport scanners, crystal structure | Cancer, cell damage |
| Gamma rays | Under 0.01 nm | Radioactive nuclei, nuclear reactions | Cancer radiotherapy, sterilising medical supplies and food, checking welds, PET scans | Most dangerous; needs lead shielding |

Order (long to short wavelength): **R**adio, **M**icrowave, **I**nfrared, **V**isible, **U**ltraviolet, **X**-ray, **G**amma. Memory line: "Rich Men In Vegas Use eXpensive Gadgets."
Visible colours, long to short: red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo, violet.

**Gamma rays (asked 2016).** Highest frequency and energy, no charge, no mass. Emitted when a nucleus drops from an excited state. Very penetrating. Uses as in the table. Discovered by Paul Villard (1900).

### Waves: longitudinal and transverse (asked 2024)

| Point | Longitudinal | Transverse |
|---|---|---|
| Particle motion | Parallel to the direction of travel | At right angles to the direction of travel |
| Parts | Compressions and rarefactions | Crests and troughs |
| Medium | Needed | Not needed for EM waves |
| Examples | Sound, earthquake P waves, a pushed spring | Light and all EM waves, ripples, earthquake S waves, a shaken rope |

### Photoelectric effect and the particle nature of light (asked 2026)

**Definition.** Emission of electrons from a metal surface when light of high enough frequency shines on it.

| Observation | What classical wave theory expected | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| No electrons below a **threshold frequency**, however bright the light | Any frequency should work if bright enough | Fails |
| Maximum energy of electrons depends on **frequency**, not brightness | Brighter light should give more energetic electrons | Fails |
| Brighter light gives **more** electrons, not faster ones | — | Unexplained |
| Emission starts **instantly**, even in dim light | Dim light should need time to build up energy | Fails |

**Einstein's explanation (1905; Nobel Prize 1921).** Light comes in packets called **photons**, each with energy E = hf. One photon gives all its energy to one electron.

**hf = φ + KE(max)**

φ is the work function: the minimum energy needed to free an electron. If hf is less than φ, no electron leaves. Threshold frequency f₀ = φ/h. Brightness means more photons, so more electrons, each with the same energy.

**Road to quantum mechanics:** Planck's quanta (1900) → Einstein's photon (1905) → Bohr's atom (1913) → de Broglie's matter waves (1924) → Schrödinger and Heisenberg (1925–26). Light and matter both show wave–particle duality.

Uses: photocells, automatic doors, light meters, solar cells, camera sensors.

**Diagram:** metal plate; wavy arrows "light (hf)" hitting it; small arrows leaving "photoelectrons"; a collector plate, ammeter and battery in a loop.

## §9 Ceramics (asked 2023)

**Definition.** Inorganic, non-metallic solids made by shaping a powder or clay and firing it at high temperature.

| Class | Examples |
|---|---|
| Traditional (clay-based) | Pottery, bricks, tiles, porcelain, sanitary ware, cement |
| Advanced (engineering) | Alumina (Al₂O₃), zirconia (ZrO₂), silicon carbide (SiC), silicon nitride (Si₃N₄), boron carbide |

**Properties:** hard, wear-resistant, brittle, very high melting point, resist heat and corrosion, electrical and thermal insulators, strong in compression, weak in tension.

**Uses**
1. Building: bricks, tiles, cement, sanitary ware.
2. Electrical: insulators on power lines, spark plugs, capacitors.
3. Engineering: cutting tools, abrasives, bearings, engine and turbine parts.
4. Aerospace: heat-shield tiles on spacecraft.
5. Defence: body armour (boron carbide).
6. Medical: hip joints, dental crowns and implants.
7. Electronics: piezoelectric sensors, high-temperature superconductors.
8. Household: crockery, cookware.

**Nano-ceramics.** Ceramics whose grains or particles are smaller than 100 nanometres.

Why they perform better than ordinary ceramics:
- Finer grains mean more grain boundaries, which block cracks: higher hardness and strength.
- Less brittle and tougher; can deform a little before breaking.
- Sinter (fuse) at lower temperatures, saving energy.
- Very large surface area: better catalysts, sensors and filters.
- Bio-active: nano-hydroxyapatite bonds with bone.
- Can be made transparent (armour windows, laser parts).

## §10 Plastics (asked 2024, 2026)

**Definition.** Synthetic polymers that can be moulded when soft and then set. A polymer is a long chain of repeating small units (monomers). Most plastics are made from petrochemicals.

- **Addition polymerisation:** monomers add without losing atoms. Ethene → polyethylene.
- **Condensation polymerisation:** monomers join and release a small molecule such as water. Nylon, PET.

| Type | Structure | Behaviour on heating | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Thermoplastic | Separate long chains | Softens; can be remoulded and recycled | Polyethylene, polypropylene, PVC, polystyrene, PET, nylon |
| Thermosetting | Heavily cross-linked network | Does not soften; chars | Bakelite, melamine, epoxy |
| Elastomer ("elastic") | Coiled chains with a few cross-links | Stretches and returns | Natural rubber, neoprene, silicone |

**Plastics vs elastics (asked 2024)**

| Point | Plastic | Elastic (elastomer) |
|---|---|---|
| Response to force | Deforms and stays deformed | Stretches and returns to shape |
| Chain structure | Linear or branched, or rigid network | Coiled, lightly cross-linked |
| Stretch | Small | Up to several times its length |
| Forces between chains | Stronger | Weak |
| Examples | PVC pipe, PET bottle | Rubber band, tyre |

**Advantages:** light, cheap, strong, waterproof, corrosion-resistant, insulating, easy to shape.

**Environmental and chemical problems of degradation**
1. The carbon–carbon backbone is very stable. Microbes have no enzymes for it, so plastics persist for hundreds of years.
2. Sunlight (UV), heat and oxygen make plastics brittle. They break into **microplastics** (under 5 mm) but do not disappear. Microplastics are now found in oceans, soil, food, drinking water and human blood.
3. Additives (plasticisers such as phthalates, bisphenol A, flame retardants) leak out and disturb hormones.
4. Burning releases CO₂, carbon monoxide, hydrogen chloride and **dioxins** (from PVC).
5. Litter chokes drains (urban flooding), kills marine animals by tangling and swallowing, and fills landfills.
6. Only about 9% of all plastic ever made has been recycled.

**Biodegradable plastics: how effective?**

| For | Against |
|---|---|
| Made from renewable matter (PLA from maize starch; PHA made by bacteria) | Most need industrial composting (about 60 °C); they barely break down in sea water, soil or landfill |
| Break down to CO₂, water and biomass under the right conditions | "Oxo-degradable" types only fragment into microplastics |
| Lower carbon footprint | Contaminate normal plastic recycling |
| Useful for food-waste bags, farm mulch film, medical stitches | Cost more; compete with food crops for land; about 1% of production |

Judgement: a useful niche, not a full answer. The main answers are reduce, reuse, recycle, bans on single-use bags, producer responsibility and proper waste collection.

## §11 Semiconductors (asked 2021)

**Definition.** Materials whose electrical conductivity lies between that of conductors and insulators. Examples: silicon, germanium, gallium arsenide.

- Conductivity **rises** with temperature (the opposite of metals) and with light.
- Conductivity can be controlled precisely by **doping** (adding a tiny amount of impurity).

| Type | Dopant | Charge carriers |
|---|---|---|
| Intrinsic | None (pure) | Few electrons and holes |
| n-type | Group 5 element (phosphorus, arsenic): one extra electron | Electrons |
| p-type | Group 3 element (boron, gallium): one missing electron | Holes |

**Devices**
- **p-n junction diode:** lets current flow one way. Used as a rectifier (AC to DC), LED, solar cell, photodiode.
- **Transistor** (invented 1947 at Bell Labs): three layers (npn or pnp). Works as a switch or amplifier.
- **Integrated circuit (chip):** millions to billions of transistors on one piece of silicon. Microprocessors and memory.

**Why semiconductors are the "brain" of modern electronics**
1. A transistor is an electrically controlled switch: on = 1, off = 0. This is the basis of all digital logic.
2. Billions of them on a chip make processors that calculate and memory that stores.
3. They are tiny, fast, cheap, reliable and use little power. Transistor counts have doubled about every two years (Moore's law).
4. They are in every device: computers, phones, cars, medical scanners, satellites, solar panels, LEDs, sensors.

**Diagram:** two blocks joined: "p-type (holes)" and "n-type (electrons)" with a thin "depletion layer" between them; battery connected for forward bias; arrow for current.

## §12 Antibiotics (asked 2019)

**Definition.** Drugs that kill bacteria or stop them multiplying. They do **not** work against viruses (cold, flu, dengue, hepatitis).

- First antibiotic: **penicillin**, found by Alexander Fleming in 1928 from the mould *Penicillium*.
- **Bactericidal** drugs kill bacteria. **Bacteriostatic** drugs stop growth.
- **Broad-spectrum** drugs act on many kinds of bacteria. **Narrow-spectrum** drugs act on a few.

| How they act | Examples |
|---|---|
| Block cell-wall building | Penicillins (amoxicillin), cephalosporins |
| Block protein making at ribosomes | Tetracyclines, streptomycin, erythromycin, azithromycin |
| Block DNA copying | Ciprofloxacin |
| Block folic acid making | Sulpha drugs |

They harm bacteria but not human cells because they target structures human cells lack (cell wall, 70S ribosomes).

**Why casual use is dangerous**
1. **Antibiotic resistance.** Some bacteria survive by mutation. Misuse kills the weak and lets resistant ones multiply. Examples: MRSA, multidrug-resistant TB, extensively drug-resistant typhoid (Sindh, since 2016).
2. Kills helpful gut bacteria: diarrhoea, fungal infections.
3. Allergic reactions, sometimes fatal (penicillin).
4. Damage to kidney, liver or hearing with some drugs.
5. Useless for viral illness, so cost and risk without benefit.
6. Stopping early leaves the toughest bacteria alive.

Safe use: only on prescription, right dose, full course, no sharing or leftover use, no routine use in animal feed.

## §13 Vaccines (asked 2016, 2019, 2022)

**Definition.** A biological preparation that trains the immune system to recognise a germ and gives protection (active immunity) without causing the disease.

- Edward Jenner made the first vaccine (smallpox, 1796). Louis Pasteur developed rabies and anthrax vaccines.
- **How it works:** the vaccine carries an antigen. White blood cells (B and T lymphocytes) respond and make antibodies. **Memory cells** remain. On real infection the response is fast and strong.

| Type | What it contains | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Live attenuated | Weakened live germ | Oral polio (OPV), measles, BCG (TB), rotavirus |
| Inactivated (killed) | Dead germ | Injectable polio (IPV), rabies, hepatitis A |
| Subunit, recombinant, conjugate | Only a part (protein or sugar) | Hepatitis B, HPV, pneumococcal |
| Toxoid | Inactivated toxin | Tetanus, diphtheria |
| Viral vector | Harmless virus carrying a gene of the germ | Oxford–AstraZeneca COVID-19, Ebola |
| mRNA | Genetic message; our cells make the antigen | Pfizer–BioNTech, Moderna COVID-19 |
| DNA | Plasmid DNA carrying an antigen gene | ZyCoV-D (India, 2021), first DNA vaccine approved for humans |

**DNA vaccines (asked 2016).** A ring of DNA (plasmid) with the gene for one antigen is injected. Body cells take it up, make the antigen and trigger both antibody and cell-mediated immunity. Advantages: no live germ so no risk of infection, stable without strict cold storage, cheap and quick to design. Limits: weak response in humans; needs special delivery methods.

**Side effects:** common and mild (sore arm, mild fever, tiredness). Serious reactions such as severe allergy are very rare. Oral polio vaccine very rarely causes vaccine-derived polio.

**Effectiveness:** smallpox eradicated (1980); polio cases down by over 99% since 1988; measles, tetanus and diphtheria deaths cut sharply. When most people are immune, the germ cannot spread: **herd immunity**.

**Pakistan:** the Expanded Programme on Immunization (EPI, 1978) gives free vaccines against 12 diseases: childhood TB, polio, diphtheria, whooping cough, tetanus, hepatitis B, Hib, pneumonia, rotavirus diarrhoea, measles, rubella and typhoid.

**Vaccines vs antibiotics (asked 2019)**

| Point | Vaccine | Antibiotic |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Prevent disease | Treat infection |
| When given | Before infection | After infection |
| Works against | Viruses and bacteria | Bacteria only |
| How | Stimulates the body's own immunity | Directly kills or stops bacteria |
| Protection | Long-lasting | Only while taken |
| Nature | Weakened or killed germ, or a part of it | Chemical from microbes or synthetic |
| Main risk | Rare allergic reaction | Resistance |

## §14 Fertilizers (asked 2019, 2024, 2025, 2026)

**Definition.** Natural or manufactured substances added to soil to supply nutrients that plants need.

**Plant nutrient elements (asked 2024).** Plants need 17 essential elements.

| Group | Elements |
|---|---|
| From air and water | Carbon, hydrogen, oxygen |
| Primary macronutrients | **Nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), potassium (K)** |
| Secondary macronutrients | Calcium, magnesium, sulphur |
| Micronutrients | Iron, manganese, zinc, copper, boron, molybdenum, chlorine, nickel |

| Nutrient | Role | Deficiency sign |
|---|---|---|
| N | Leaf growth, chlorophyll, proteins | Yellow leaves, stunted growth |
| P | Roots, flowers, seeds, energy transfer (ATP) | Purple leaves, poor roots |
| K | Water balance, disease resistance, fruit quality | Scorched leaf edges |

**Types**
- **Organic:** farmyard manure, compost, green manure, bone meal. Slow, improve soil structure.
- **Inorganic (chemical):** nitrogen (urea, 46% N; ammonium nitrate; CAN), phosphate (DAP, superphosphate), potash (potassium chloride or sulphate), mixed NPK.
- **Biofertilizers:** living microbes such as *Rhizobium* in legume root nodules.
- Ammonia for nitrogen fertilizer comes from the Haber process: N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃.

**Advantages of nitrogen fertilizers:** fast growth, much higher yields, cheap per unit of nutrient, easy to store and apply, dissolve quickly.

**Effects of excess use**

| On soil chemistry | On groundwater and water bodies |
|---|---|
| Acidification: ammonium turns to nitrate and releases acid | **Nitrate leaching:** nitrate is very soluble and washes down to groundwater |
| Salt build-up; hard, compacted soil | Nitrate in drinking water above the WHO limit (50 mg/L) causes **blue baby syndrome** in infants |
| Loss of humus, earthworms and useful microbes | **Eutrophication:** run-off feeds algal blooms; decay uses up oxygen; fish die |
| Nutrient imbalance (zinc and other micronutrients locked up) | Phosphate and heavy-metal impurities (cadmium) accumulate |
| Release of nitrous oxide (N₂O), a strong greenhouse gas | |

Also: soft growth attracts pests; cost to farmers; much energy used in manufacture.

**Remedies:** soil testing, balanced NPK, split doses at the right time, slow-release and coated urea, mixing in organic manure, crop rotation with legumes, precision placement.

## §15 Pesticides (asked 2020, 2025)

**Definition.** Chemicals used to kill, repel or control pests that damage crops, stored food or health.

| By target | Kills |
|---|---|
| Insecticide | Insects |
| Herbicide | Weeds |
| Fungicide | Fungi |
| Rodenticide | Rats and mice |
| Nematicide | Nematode worms |

| By chemistry | Examples | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Organochlorines | DDT, aldrin, dieldrin, endosulfan | Very persistent; mostly banned |
| Organophosphates | Malathion, parathion, chlorpyrifos | Attack the nervous system; break down faster; very toxic |
| Carbamates | Carbaryl | Similar action, short-lived |
| Pyrethroids | Permethrin, cypermethrin | Based on a chrysanthemum compound; low toxicity to mammals |
| Biopesticides | Neem extract, *Bacillus thuringiensis* (Bt) | Safer |

**Advantages:** protect yields (pests destroy a large share of world crops), protect stored grain, control disease carriers (mosquitoes), lower food prices, save labour.

**Disadvantages:** kill bees, natural predators and fish; pests become resistant; residues in food; contaminate soil and water; poison farm workers; pest numbers bounce back when predators are gone.

**Why persistent pesticides are more lethal (asked 2020)**
1. They resist breakdown and stay in soil and water for years (DDT: up to 15 years).
2. They dissolve in fat, not water, so they collect in body fat: **bioaccumulation**.
3. Their concentration multiplies at each step of the food chain: **biomagnification**. Top consumers, including humans, get the highest dose.
4. Long-term effects: cancer, nerve damage, hormone disruption, birth defects; thin eggshells in birds.
5. They travel far by wind and water and are found even in polar animals.
- Rachel Carson's *Silent Spring* (1962) raised the alarm. The **Stockholm Convention (2001)** bans or restricts persistent organic pollutants.

```
Water -> plankton -> small fish -> large fish -> fish-eating bird
 DDT concentration rises at every step (biomagnification)
```

**Safer practice:** integrated pest management (IPM): use resistant varieties, crop rotation, biological control and traps first; spray chemicals only when needed, at the right dose, with protective gear.
