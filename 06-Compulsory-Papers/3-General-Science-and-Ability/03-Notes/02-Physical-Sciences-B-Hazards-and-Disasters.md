# Physical Sciences B: Natural Hazards and Disasters

Syllabus line: Earth Quake, Volcanic Eruption, Tsunami, Floods, Avalanche, Travelling Cyclone (Tropical Cyclone, Middle Latitude Cyclone and Tornadoes), Drought, Wildfire, Urban Fire. Disaster Risk Management.

**Hazard vs disaster.** A hazard is a natural or human-made event that could cause harm. It becomes a disaster when it hits people who are exposed and vulnerable and the damage is more than they can cope with.

Standard frame for any hazard answer: **definition → causes → types → effects → management → one example.**

---

## §1 Earthquake

**Definition.** Sudden shaking of the ground caused by the release of stored energy in the Earth's crust as seismic waves.

- **Focus (hypocentre):** the point underground where the rock breaks.
- **Epicentre:** the point on the surface directly above the focus.
- **Fault:** the fracture along which rock moves.

**Causes**
1. **Plate tectonics (main cause).** Plates collide, pull apart or slide past each other. Stress builds along faults. When it exceeds the rock's strength, the rock snaps and rebounds (elastic rebound theory).
2. Volcanic activity (moving magma).
3. Collapse of mines or caves.
4. Human activity: filling large reservoirs, mining, underground nuclear tests, fluid injection.

**Seismic waves (asked 2024)**

| Wave | Type | Motion | Speed | Travels through | Damage |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| P (primary) | Body, longitudinal | Push-pull along the direction of travel | Fastest (about 6–8 km/s in the crust) | Solids, liquids, gases | Least |
| S (secondary) | Body, transverse | Up-down or side-to-side, at right angles to travel | Slower (about 3.5–4.5 km/s) | Solids only | More |
| Love | Surface | Horizontal side-to-side shear | Slower than S | Surface layers | High |
| Rayleigh | Surface | Rolling, like ocean waves | Slowest | Surface layers | Highest |

- S waves cannot pass through liquid. Their absence on the far side of the Earth (the S-wave shadow zone) shows the outer core is liquid.
- The time gap between P and S arrivals gives the distance to the epicentre. Three stations locate it.

**Measurement**

| Scale | Measures | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Richter (1935) | Magnitude (energy released) from wave amplitude | Logarithmic. Each whole number = 10 times the amplitude and about 32 times the energy. No upper limit. |
| Moment magnitude (Mw) | Magnitude from fault area, slip and rock strength | Used today for large earthquakes. |
| Modified Mercalli | Intensity (observed effects and damage) | I to XII. Varies from place to place. |

Instrument: **seismograph** (the record is a seismogram).

**By depth of focus (asked 2020)**

| Type | Depth | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Shallow-focus | 0–70 km | Most common and most destructive, because energy reaches the surface with little loss. Occur at all plate boundaries. |
| Intermediate | 70–300 km | |
| Deep-focus | 300–700 km | Only in subduction zones (Wadati–Benioff zone). Felt over a wide area but weaker at the surface. |

**Distribution:** Circum-Pacific belt ("Ring of Fire", about 80% of large earthquakes), Alpine–Himalayan belt, mid-ocean ridges.

**Effects:** ground shaking and rupture, building collapse, landslides, liquefaction, tsunamis, fires, dam failure, deaths, disease, economic loss.

**Pakistan.** The country sits where the Indian plate pushes into the Eurasian plate (about 4–5 cm a year).

| Event | Magnitude | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Quetta, 31 May 1935 | about 7.7 | About 30,000–60,000 deaths |
| Kashmir, 8 October 2005 | 7.6 | Epicentre near Muzaffarabad; over 73,000 deaths in Pakistan; 3.5 million homeless |
| Awaran, 24 September 2013 | 7.7 | Balochistan; over 800 deaths |
| Hindu Kush, 26 October 2015 | 7.5 | Epicentre in Badakhshan, north-east Afghanistan; focus about 210 km deep; about 400 deaths, most of them in Pakistan |

**Management:** seismic zoning and building codes, earthquake-resistant design (flexible frames, base isolation), retrofitting schools and hospitals, land-use control near faults, public drills ("drop, cover, hold on"), emergency stocks, early warning systems that detect P waves seconds before S waves arrive. Earthquakes cannot yet be predicted.

**Diagram:** cross-section of crust with a fault line; star at depth labelled "focus"; point above on surface labelled "epicentre"; curved lines spreading from the focus labelled "seismic waves".

## §2 Volcanic eruption

**Definition.** A volcano is an opening in the crust through which molten rock (magma), ash and gases reach the surface. Magma that reaches the surface is lava.

**Causes (where and why magma forms)**
1. **Subduction zones (convergent boundaries).** A sinking plate releases water, the mantle above melts, and magma rises. Explosive volcanoes. Example: the Pacific Ring of Fire.
2. **Divergent boundaries.** Plates separate, pressure drops, the mantle melts. Example: Mid-Atlantic Ridge, Iceland.
3. **Hotspots.** A plume of hot mantle under the middle of a plate. Example: Hawaii.
- An eruption happens when gas pressure in the magma chamber exceeds the strength of the rock above. Thick, gas-rich magma erupts explosively; thin magma flows quietly.

**Types**

| Basis | Types |
|---|---|
| Shape | **Shield** (broad, gentle slopes, runny basalt lava; Mauna Loa). **Composite or stratovolcano** (steep, layers of lava and ash, explosive; Fuji, Vesuvius). **Cinder cone** (small, loose fragments). **Caldera** (large collapse crater). |
| Activity | Active, dormant, extinct |

**Products:** lava flows, ash, pyroclastic flows (fast hot clouds of gas and rock), volcanic bombs, gases (water vapour, CO₂, SO₂), lahars (mudflows).

**Effects**

| Harmful | Useful |
|---|---|
| Deaths and destruction of settlements | Fertile soils from weathered ash |
| Ash damages crops, lungs, aircraft engines (Iceland 2010 closed European airspace) | Geothermal energy (Iceland, New Zealand) |
| Lahars, landslides, tsunamis (Krakatoa 1883) | Minerals: sulphur, copper, gold, diamonds |
| SO₂ aerosols cool the climate for 1–3 years (Pinatubo 1991 cooled the globe by about 0.5 °C; Tambora 1815 caused the "year without a summer") | New land, tourism, hot springs |
| Acid rain | Gases built the early atmosphere and oceans |

**Pakistan:** no active magma volcano. Mud volcanoes exist on the Makran coast of Balochistan. Koh-i-Sultan in Chagai is an extinct volcano.

**Management:** monitoring (seismometers, gas sensors, ground tilt, satellite thermal images), hazard maps, exclusion zones, evacuation plans, ash advisories for aviation.

**Diagram:** cone in cross-section; magma chamber below; central vent (pipe); crater at top; side vent; alternating layers of ash and lava; ash cloud above.

## §3 Tsunami

**Definition.** A series of very long ocean waves caused by a sudden displacement of a large volume of water. The word is Japanese for "harbour wave".

**Causes**
1. Undersea earthquakes at subduction zones (the cause of most tsunamis): the sea floor jerks up or down and lifts the water column.
2. Submarine or coastal landslides.
3. Volcanic eruptions and caldera collapse (Krakatoa 1883; Tonga 2022).
4. Meteorite impact (rare).

**Characteristics**

| In deep ocean | Near the coast |
|---|---|
| Wavelength 100–200 km or more | Wavelength shortens |
| Speed 700–800 km/h (like a jet) | Slows to 30–50 km/h |
| Height under 1 m; ships do not notice | Height grows to 10–30 m (shoaling) |

- Speed depends on depth: v = √(g × d). At 4,000 m depth, v ≈ 200 m/s ≈ 710 km/h.
- The sea often pulls back first (drawback). This is a natural warning.
- Several waves arrive minutes to an hour apart. The first is often not the largest.

**Tsunami vs tidal wave (asked 2018)**

| Point | Tsunami | Tide ("tidal wave") |
|---|---|---|
| Cause | Sudden displacement of water by earthquake, landslide, eruption | Gravity of the Moon and Sun |
| Timing | Sudden, irregular | Regular, twice daily, predictable |
| Wavelength | Hundreds of km | Half the Earth's circumference |
| Effect | Destructive flooding | Normal rise and fall of sea level |

**Major events**

| Event | Notes |
|---|---|
| Indian Ocean, 26 December 2004 | Magnitude 9.1 earthquake off Sumatra. About 230,000 deaths in 14 countries. Deadliest on record. |
| Tōhoku, Japan, 11 March 2011 | Magnitude 9.0–9.1. Over 18,000 dead or missing. Fukushima nuclear accident. |
| Makran, 28 November 1945 | Magnitude about 8.1 off Balochistan. About 4,000 deaths along the Makran coast. Pakistan's main tsunami risk. |

**Management:** seismic networks and deep-ocean pressure sensors (DART buoys), warning centres (Pacific Tsunami Warning Center; Indian Ocean system set up after 2004), sirens and phone alerts, marked evacuation routes to high ground, mangrove belts and sea walls, land-use rules, public education ("if the sea withdraws or the ground shakes for long, run uphill").

**Diagram:** sea-floor fault with one side thrust up; arrow lifting the water; low long waves in deep water; tall steep wave at the shore.

## §4 Floods

**Definition.** An overflow of water onto land that is normally dry.

**Types**

| Type | Cause |
|---|---|
| Riverine (fluvial) | River overtops its banks after long rain or snowmelt |
| Flash flood | Intense rain or cloudburst in hills; rises within hours |
| Urban (pluvial) | Drains cannot carry the rain; paved surfaces stop soaking |
| Coastal | Storm surge from cyclones; tsunamis |
| Glacial lake outburst flood (GLOF) | A glacial lake dam breaks; a rising risk in Gilgit-Baltistan and Chitral |

**Causes:** heavy monsoon rain, rapid snow and glacier melt, cloudbursts, dam or embankment failure, deforestation in catchments, building on flood plains, silted rivers and blocked drains, climate change (a warmer atmosphere holds about 7% more moisture per degree).

**Effects:** deaths, displacement, loss of crops, livestock, houses, roads and bridges, water-borne disease (cholera, diarrhoea, typhoid, malaria, dengue), food shortage. Benefits: fertile silt, groundwater recharge.

**Pakistan**

| Flood | Impact |
|---|---|
| 2010 | About 20 million people affected, about 2,000 deaths, about one-fifth of the country flooded |
| 2022 | About 33 million affected, over 1,700 deaths, Sindh and Balochistan worst hit, damage and losses about US$30 billion |
| 2025 | Monsoon cloudbursts in KP and northern areas, and river floods in Punjab; around 1,000 deaths |

**Management**
- Structural: dams and reservoirs, embankments (bunds), barrages, flood diversion channels, drainage, check dams, river dredging.
- Non-structural: flood forecasting and early warning (Flood Forecasting Division of the Pakistan Meteorological Department, Lahore), flood-plain zoning, watershed afforestation, removal of encroachments, evacuation plans, insurance, community training.
- Institutions: Federal Flood Commission, NDMA and provincial PDMAs.

**Diagram:** river cross-section showing channel, natural levees, flood plain, embankment, and the normal and flood water levels.

## §5 Avalanche

**Definition.** A rapid flow of snow, often with ice and rock, down a mountain slope.

**Types (asked 2020)**

| Type | Description |
|---|---|
| Loose snow (sluff) | Starts at a point in loose surface snow and fans out. Usually small. |
| **Slab** | A cohesive plate of snow breaks away along a weak buried layer and slides as a block. **Most dangerous**: large, fast (over 100 km/h), responsible for most deaths. |
| Powder snow | A slab or loose avalanche that turns into a turbulent cloud of airborne snow. Can exceed 250 km/h. |
| Wet snow | Snow soaked by melt or rain. Slow but dense and heavy. |

Others: ice avalanche (falling glacier ice), cornice fall.

**Physical factors that trigger avalanches (asked 2026)**
1. **Slope angle:** most start on slopes of 30°–45°.
2. **Snowpack structure:** a weak layer under a heavy slab.
3. **Heavy new snowfall** (about 30 cm or more in a day) or rain on snow.
4. **Wind:** moves snow to the lee side and builds unstable slabs and cornices.
5. **Temperature:** rapid warming weakens bonds.
6. **Terrain and aspect:** convex slopes, gullies, smooth ground, lack of trees.
7. **Triggers:** earthquakes, falling cornices, skiers, snowmobiles, explosives.

**Prediction and monitoring technology**
- Automatic weather stations and snow-depth sensors.
- Snow-pit tests and snowpack computer models.
- Satellite remote sensing, LiDAR and drones to map snow depth.
- Doppler radar, infrasound and seismic sensors that detect avalanches as they start.
- GIS hazard maps and public avalanche forecasts (danger scale 1–5).
- Rescue aids: avalanche transceivers (beacons), airbag packs, probes.

**Control:** controlled blasting to release small slides, snow fences and nets, deflecting walls, snow sheds over roads, forests on slopes, closing routes.

**Pakistan:** Gayari, Siachen (7 April 2012): an avalanche buried an army camp; 140 died. Neelum Valley, January 2020: over 70 died.

**Diagram:** a slope with three zones labelled starting zone, track, run-out zone; a slab breaking at the "crown" over a "weak layer".

## §6 Travelling cyclones

A cyclone is a low-pressure system with winds spiralling inward: anticlockwise in the northern hemisphere, clockwise in the southern.

### 6.1 Tropical cyclone

**Definition.** An intense rotating storm that forms over warm tropical oceans, with very low central pressure, strong winds and heavy rain.

| Region | Name |
|---|---|
| Atlantic, north-east Pacific | Hurricane |
| North-west Pacific | Typhoon |
| Indian Ocean, south Pacific | Cyclone |

**Conditions for formation**
1. Sea surface temperature of at least 26.5 °C to a depth of about 50 m.
2. At least 5° away from the equator, so the Coriolis force can start rotation.
3. Moist air in the middle troposphere.
4. Low vertical wind shear.
5. An existing weather disturbance.

**How it forms**
1. Warm, moist air rises from the sea. Surface pressure falls.
2. Vapour condenses and releases latent heat. This warms the air and makes it rise faster.
3. More moist air flows in and spirals because of the Coriolis force.
4. The system grows: tropical depression → tropical storm (named at 63 km/h) → hurricane (119 km/h or more).
5. It weakens over land or cold water because its supply of warm moist air is cut.

**Structure**

| Part | Features |
|---|---|
| Eye | Centre, 30–65 km wide. Calm, clear, sinking air, lowest pressure. |
| **Eyewall** | Ring of towering clouds round the eye. Strongest winds and heaviest rain. **The most dangerous part** (asked 2019). |
| Rain bands | Spiral bands of thunderstorms extending hundreds of km. |

**Scale:** Saffir–Simpson, categories 1–5 (category 5: winds of 252 km/h or more).

**Damage:** storm surge (the main killer), destructive winds, flooding rain, landslides, tornadoes, loss of crops, power and water, disease afterwards.

**Examples:** Bhola cyclone, East Pakistan, 1970 (300,000–500,000 deaths; the deadliest on record). Hurricane Katrina, USA, 2005. Sindh cyclone 1999. Cyclone Biparjoy, June 2023 (large evacuations in Sindh; landfall in Gujarat).

**Diagram:** side view: central clear column "eye"; tall cloud walls "eyewall"; arrows rising in the wall, sinking in the eye, flowing out at the top; outer "rain bands".

### 6.2 Middle-latitude cyclone

**Definition.** A large low-pressure system that forms between 30° and 60° latitude along the polar front, where cold polar air meets warm tropical air. Also called an extratropical or temperate cyclone, or a depression.

- Energy comes from the **temperature contrast** between air masses, not from warm seas.
- It has **fronts**: a warm front (steady rain) and a cold front (heavy showers, thunder).
- **Life cycle (polar front theory):** stationary front → wave → mature stage with a warm sector → occlusion (cold front overtakes warm front) → decay. Lasts 3–10 days.
- Moves west to east with the westerlies.
- Brings cloud, wind and widespread rain or snow. Western disturbances that bring Pakistan's winter rain are of this family.

### 6.3 Tornado

**Definition.** A violently rotating, narrow column of air that reaches from a thunderstorm cloud (cumulonimbus) to the ground. It is seen as a funnel cloud.

**Formation**
1. Warm moist air near the ground meets cold dry air above. The air is very unstable.
2. Wind speed and direction change with height (wind shear). This makes a horizontal rolling tube of air.
3. A strong updraft in a supercell thunderstorm tilts the tube upright. A rotating column, the mesocyclone, forms.
4. A narrow funnel drops from the cloud base and touches the ground.

**Features:** width from tens of metres to 2 km; life from a few minutes to about an hour; winds up to about 480 km/h, the fastest on Earth; path a few km long. Measured on the **Enhanced Fujita scale** (EF0–EF5). Most common in the USA ("Tornado Alley") and also in Bangladesh. A tornado over water is a waterspout.

**Effects:** total destruction along a narrow track, flying debris, deaths and injuries, uprooted trees, wrecked vehicles and power lines.

### 6.4 Comparison (asked 2020 and 2024)

| Point | Tropical cyclone | Mid-latitude cyclone | Tornado |
|---|---|---|---|
| Where | Over warm oceans, 5°–30° | 30°–60°, land or sea | Over land, under thunderstorms |
| Size | 200–1,000 km | 1,000–2,500 km | Under 2 km |
| Life | Days to 2 weeks | 3–10 days | Minutes to an hour |
| Energy | Latent heat from warm sea | Temperature contrast of air masses | Instability and wind shear in a storm |
| Fronts | None | Warm and cold fronts | None |
| Wind | 119–300 km/h | 30–120 km/h | Up to about 480 km/h |
| Centre | Calm eye | No eye | Funnel |
| Movement | East to west, then curves poleward | West to east | Irregular, short path |
| Scale | Saffir–Simpson | None in common use | Enhanced Fujita |
| Warning time | Days | Days | Minutes |

## §7 Drought

**Definition.** A long period of abnormally low rainfall that causes a serious shortage of water. It is a slow-onset disaster.

**Types**

| Type | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Meteorological | Rainfall well below normal |
| Agricultural | Soil moisture too low for crops |
| Hydrological | Low rivers, lakes, reservoirs and groundwater |
| Socio-economic | Water shortage harms people's food, income and health |

**Causes**
- Natural: failure of the monsoon, persistent high pressure, El Niño, high temperatures and evaporation.
- Human: deforestation, overgrazing, over-pumping of groundwater, wasteful irrigation, poor storage, climate change.

**Effects:** crop failure, loss of livestock, famine and malnutrition, drying wells, migration, desertification and dust storms, wildfires, less hydropower, conflict over water.

**Pakistan:** drought of 1998–2002 (worst in decades; Balochistan and Sindh). Tharparkar suffers repeated droughts (for example 2014, 2018). Pakistan is a water-stressed country: water available per person has fallen below 1,000 cubic metres a year.

**Management:** storage dams and small check dams, rainwater harvesting, lining canals, drip and sprinkler irrigation, drought-resistant crops, groundwater regulation, afforestation, drought monitoring and early warning (Pakistan Meteorological Department), fodder and grain reserves, crop insurance.

## §8 Wildfire

**Definition.** An unplanned, uncontrolled fire in forest, grassland or bush.

**Fire triangle:** heat + fuel + oxygen. Remove one and the fire stops.

**Types (asked 2023)**

| Type | Burns | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Ground fire | Roots, peat and buried organic matter | Slow, smouldering, hard to detect |
| Surface fire | Leaf litter, grass, low shrubs | Most common |
| Crown fire | Tops of trees | Fastest and most intense; driven by wind |

**Causes**
- Natural: lightning, volcanic eruption, sparks from rockfall, spontaneous heating in very dry heat.
- Human (most fires): campfires, cigarettes, burning crop waste, arson, power lines, machinery.

**Spread depends on:**
1. Fuel: amount, dryness, type (pine resin burns fast).
2. Weather: high temperature, low humidity, strong wind.
3. Topography: fire moves faster uphill.
4. Spotting: wind carries embers ahead and starts new fires.

**Effects:** deaths, loss of homes, forests, wildlife and habitat, smoke pollution (PM2.5, CO), large CO₂ release, soil erosion and floods afterwards. Ecological role: clears dead matter, returns nutrients, helps some seeds germinate.

**Prevention and control:** fire bans in the dry season, public awareness, firebreaks (cleared strips), controlled (prescribed) burning to cut fuel, thinning, watch towers, satellite detection (MODIS, VIIRS) and drones, water-bombing aircraft, trained crews, fire-safe building near forests.

**Examples:** Australia's "Black Summer" 2019–20 (over 18 million hectares). Los Angeles, January 2025 (about 30 deaths, over 16,000 structures destroyed). Pakistan: Koh-e-Sulaiman pine-nut forests, Balochistan, May 2022; Margalla Hills fires most summers.

## §9 Urban fire

**Definition.** A fire in a built-up area: houses, high-rises, markets, factories, warehouses.

**Causes**
1. Electrical faults: short circuits, overloaded or illegal wiring (the leading cause in Pakistani cities).
2. Gas leaks and cylinder explosions.
3. Cooking and heating accidents.
4. Careless smoking, candles, fireworks.
5. Flammable goods stored in homes and markets (chemicals, cloth, fuel).
6. Industrial accidents. Arson.

**Why urban fires become disasters:** dense buildings, narrow lanes that block fire engines, no fire exits or locked exits, no alarms or sprinklers, flammable cladding, weak enforcement of building codes, few hydrants, slow response.

**Classes of fire and extinguishers**

| Class | Fuel | Extinguisher |
|---|---|---|
| A | Wood, paper, cloth | Water, foam |
| B | Flammable liquids (petrol, oil) | Foam, CO₂, dry powder |
| C | Flammable gases | Dry powder; shut off the gas |
| D | Metals | Special dry powder |
| Electrical | Live equipment | CO₂, dry powder (never water) |
| F / K | Cooking oils | Wet chemical |

Using an extinguisher: **PASS** = Pull the pin, Aim at the base, Squeeze, Sweep.

**Prevention and management:** enforce building and fire codes (Pakistan added fire-safety provisions to its building code in 2016), fire exits and stairs, smoke detectors, alarms, sprinklers, hydrants, safe wiring and regular inspection, zoning of hazardous industry, fire drills, well-equipped fire brigades and Rescue 1122, public training.

**Examples:** Baldia Town garment factory, Karachi, 11 September 2012 (about 260 deaths; locked exits and barred windows). Grenfell Tower, London, 2017 (72 deaths; flammable cladding).

## §10 Disaster risk management (DRM)

**Key terms**

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Hazard | A dangerous event or condition |
| Exposure | People and assets in the hazard's path |
| Vulnerability | Weakness that makes harm likely (poverty, poor housing) |
| Capacity | Strengths and resources to cope |
| Risk | Chance of loss. Risk = Hazard × Exposure × Vulnerability ÷ Capacity |
| Disaster | Serious disruption that exceeds a community's ability to cope |

**Definition of DRM.** The planned use of policies, organisation and skills to reduce disaster risk and to manage what remains. The modern approach is proactive (reduce risk before) rather than reactive (relief after).

**The four phases (asked 2026)**

| Phase | When | Aim | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Mitigation (prevention) | Before | Reduce or remove the risk | Building codes, embankments, land-use zoning, afforestation, insurance |
| 2. Preparedness | Before | Get ready to respond | Early warning, evacuation plans, drills, stockpiles, training |
| 3. Response | During and just after | Save lives and meet basic needs | Search and rescue, first aid, shelter, food, water |
| 4. Recovery | After | Restore and improve | Rehabilitation, rebuilding houses and roads, livelihoods; "build back better" |

```
        MITIGATION  --->  PREPAREDNESS
            ^                  |
            |    (DISASTER)    v
         RECOVERY   <---    RESPONSE
```
Draw it as a circle with four arrows. It is a cycle: recovery feeds into mitigation.

**Global frameworks**
- Hyogo Framework for Action, 2005–2015.
- **Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction, 2015–2030.** Four priorities: (1) understand disaster risk; (2) strengthen risk governance; (3) invest in risk reduction for resilience; (4) improve preparedness for response and "build back better". It has seven global targets.
- International Day for Disaster Risk Reduction: 13 October.

**Pakistan's system**
- The 2005 earthquake exposed a relief-only system. ERRA was created for reconstruction.
- National Disaster Management Ordinance 2006, then the **National Disaster Management Act 2010**.
- **National Disaster Management Commission** (chaired by the Prime Minister) makes policy.
- **NDMA** (federal, set up 2007), **PDMAs** (provincial), **DDMAs** (district).
- National Disaster Risk Reduction Policy 2013; National Disaster Management Plan.
- Support bodies: Pakistan Meteorological Department, Federal Flood Commission, SUPARCO, Rescue 1122, armed forces, Pakistan Red Crescent.

**Weaknesses to mention in a "discuss" answer:** reactive mindset, weak district level, poor enforcement of building and land-use rules, limited funds, weak early warning to the last mile, little community training.
