## §1 Universe

**Definition.** The universe is all of space, time, matter and energy. It contains galaxies, stars, planets, gas, dust, radiation, dark matter and dark energy.

### Origin: the Big Bang theory

- About **13.8 billion years ago** the universe began from an extremely hot, dense state and has been expanding and cooling ever since.
- It was not an explosion into empty space. Space itself expanded.
- Timeline:

| Time after start | Event |
|---|---|
| Fraction of a second | Rapid expansion (inflation). Quarks, electrons form. |
| First 3 minutes | Protons and neutrons fuse into hydrogen and helium nuclei. |
| About 380,000 years | Atoms form. Light travels freely. This light is the cosmic microwave background (CMB). |
| A few hundred million years | First stars and galaxies. |
| 9.2 billion years | Solar system forms (4.6 billion years ago). |

**Evidence (learn these three):**
1. **Redshift of galaxies.** Hubble (1929) found that distant galaxies move away, and the farther they are, the faster they go. Hubble's law: v = H₀ × d.
2. **Cosmic microwave background.** Faint radiation from all directions at 2.7 K. Found by Penzias and Wilson in 1965.
3. **Abundance of light elements.** About 75% hydrogen and 25% helium by mass, as the theory predicts.

**Other theories (one line each):** Steady State theory (universe has no beginning; new matter appears as it expands; rejected after the CMB was found). Oscillating universe (repeated expansions and collapses).

### Composition

| Component | Share | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Dark energy | about 68% | Unknown form of energy. Makes the expansion speed up. Found in 1998 from distant supernovae. |
| Dark matter | about 27% | Does not emit or absorb light. Known only through its gravity (galaxy rotation speeds, gravitational lensing). |
| Ordinary matter | about 5% | Stars, planets, gas, us. |

### How the age of the universe is estimated

1. **Expansion rate.** Measure the Hubble constant and run the expansion backwards. Age is roughly 1/H₀.
2. **CMB measurements.** Satellites WMAP and Planck mapped the CMB. Planck result: 13.8 billion years.
3. **Oldest stars.** Globular clusters hold stars about 12–13 billion years old. The universe must be older.
4. **Cooling of white dwarfs** and **radioactive dating** of elements in old stars give lower limits.

### Stars

- A star is a huge ball of hot gas (plasma) that makes its own light by nuclear fusion.
- **Life cycle:** nebula → protostar → main sequence star → red giant (or supergiant) → then:
  - Small or medium star (like the Sun): planetary nebula → white dwarf → black dwarf.
  - Massive star: supernova → neutron star or black hole.

```
Nebula -> Protostar -> Main sequence --(Sun-like)--> Red giant -> Planetary nebula -> White dwarf
                                     \--(massive)--> Supergiant -> Supernova -> Neutron star / Black hole
```

- **Magnitude** = brightness of a star.
  - Apparent magnitude: how bright it looks from Earth.
  - Absolute magnitude: how bright it would look from a standard distance of 10 parsecs.
  - Smaller number means brighter. A difference of 5 magnitudes is a factor of 100 in brightness.
- **Colour and temperature.** Hotter stars are bluer; cooler stars are redder.

| Colour | Surface temperature | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Blue to blue-white | 10,000 K to over 30,000 K | Rigel |
| White | about 7,500–10,000 K | Sirius |
| Yellow | about 5,800 K | Sun |
| Orange | about 4,000–5,000 K | Arcturus |
| Red | about 3,000–3,500 K | Betelgeuse |

**Star vs planet**

| Point | Star | Planet |
|---|---|---|
| Light | Makes its own by fusion | Reflects the star's light |
| Temperature | Very high | Much lower |
| Size and mass | Very large | Small |
| Appearance | Twinkles (point source) | Steady light (small disc) |
| Motion | Fixed pattern in the sky | Moves against the star background |
| Example | Sun, Sirius | Earth, Mars |

### Black holes

- **Definition.** A region where gravity is so strong that nothing, not even light, can escape.
- **Parts:** singularity (centre, where known physics breaks down), event horizon (boundary of no return), accretion disc (hot gas spiralling in).
- The radius of the event horizon is the Schwarzschild radius: r = 2GM/c².
- **Formation.** When a star above roughly 20 solar masses runs out of fuel, its core collapses in a supernova. If the remaining core is above about 3 solar masses, it becomes a black hole.
- **Types:** stellar (a few to tens of solar masses), intermediate, supermassive (millions to billions of solar masses, at galaxy centres; Sagittarius A* in the Milky Way is about 4 million solar masses).
- **How they are found** (they emit no light):
  1. X-rays from the hot accretion disc (Cygnus X-1).
  2. Fast orbits of stars around an unseen mass (Sagittarius A*; Nobel Prize 2020).
  3. Gravitational waves from merging black holes (LIGO, first detected 2015).
  4. Direct image of the shadow by the Event Horizon Telescope (M87* in 2019, Sagittarius A* in 2022).
  5. Gravitational lensing.
- **Inside:** matter is crushed towards the singularity. Time slows near the horizon. Tidal forces stretch objects ("spaghettification"). Hawking predicted that black holes slowly lose energy as Hawking radiation.

**Diagram to draw:** a dark circle labelled "event horizon", a dot at the centre "singularity", a flattened ring around it "accretion disc", and two jets along the axis.

## §2 Galaxy

- **Definition.** A galaxy is a huge system of stars, gas, dust and dark matter held together by gravity.
- **Types (Hubble classification):**

| Type | Shape | Stars | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spiral | Flat disc with arms and a central bulge | Young and old; much gas | Andromeda (M31) |
| Barred spiral | Spiral with a bar across the centre | Same | Milky Way |
| Elliptical | Oval, no arms | Mostly old; little gas | M87 |
| Irregular | No fixed shape | Many young stars | Magellanic Clouds |

- **Milky Way** (our galaxy):
  - Barred spiral. Diameter about 100,000 light years.
  - 100–400 billion stars.
  - The Sun lies in the Orion arm, about 26,000 light years from the centre.
  - The Sun takes about 225–250 million years to orbit the centre once (a "cosmic year").
  - A supermassive black hole, Sagittarius A*, sits at the centre.
- Nearest large galaxy: **Andromeda**, about 2.5 million light years away.
- The Milky Way belongs to the **Local Group** (over 50 galaxies). The observable universe holds hundreds of billions of galaxies (some estimates reach 2 trillion).

**Diagram:** top view of a spiral with a central bar, four curved arms, and a dot two-thirds of the way out labelled "Sun".

## §3 Light year and the astronomical system of units

| Unit | Definition | Value |
|---|---|---|
| Astronomical unit (AU) | Mean Earth–Sun distance | 149.6 million km (1.496 × 10⁸ km) |
| Light year (ly) | Distance light travels in a vacuum in one year | 9.46 × 10¹² km (about 63,240 AU) |
| Parsec (pc) | Distance at which 1 AU subtends an angle of 1 arcsecond | 3.26 ly = 3.09 × 10¹³ km |
| Solar mass | Mass of the Sun | 1.99 × 10³⁰ kg |

- A light year is a unit of **distance**, not time.
- Speed of light c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s (299,792 km/s).
- Sunlight reaches Earth in about **8 minutes 20 seconds**. Moonlight takes about 1.3 seconds.
- Nearest star after the Sun: Proxima Centauri, 4.24 light years.
- The **IAU astronomical system of units** uses: the astronomical unit for length, the solar mass for mass, and the day (86,400 s) for time.
- Use AU inside the solar system, light years and parsecs for stars, megaparsecs for galaxies.

Check of the light year: 3 × 10⁵ km/s × 3.156 × 10⁷ s ≈ 9.46 × 10¹² km.

## §4 Solar system

**Definition.** The Sun and everything bound to it by gravity: 8 planets, their moons, dwarf planets, asteroids, comets and dust. Age: about 4.6 billion years. It formed from a collapsing cloud of gas and dust (nebular hypothesis). The Sun holds 99.8% of the total mass.

### Structure (inside to outside)

```
SUN | Mercury Venus Earth Mars | Asteroid belt | Jupiter Saturn | Uranus Neptune | Kuiper belt | Oort cloud
      (terrestrial, rocky)       (Ceres)         (gas giants)     (ice giants)     (Pluto, Eris)  (comets)
```

| Group | Members | Features |
|---|---|---|
| Terrestrial (inner) planets | Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars | Small, rocky, dense, solid surface, few or no moons, no rings |
| Asteroid belt | Between Mars and Jupiter | Rocky bodies; Ceres is the largest |
| Gas giants | Jupiter, Saturn | Very large, mostly hydrogen and helium, no solid surface, many moons, rings |
| Ice giants | Uranus, Neptune | Water, ammonia, methane ices; blue colour from methane |
| Dwarf planets | Ceres, Pluto, Haumea, Makemake, Eris | Round, orbit the Sun, but have not cleared their orbits |
| Small bodies | Comets, asteroids, meteoroids | Comets come from the Kuiper belt and Oort cloud |

**IAU definition of a planet (2006):** it (1) orbits the Sun, (2) has enough mass to be nearly round, (3) has cleared the neighbourhood of its orbit. Pluto fails the third test, so it is a dwarf planet.

### Planet facts

| Planet | Key facts |
|---|---|
| Mercury | Smallest. Closest to Sun. Year = 88 days. No moon. No real atmosphere. |
| Venus | Hottest (about 465 °C) due to a thick CO₂ atmosphere. Rotates backwards (east to west). Its day is longer than its year. Brightest planet; "morning/evening star". No moon. |
| Earth | Only known planet with life. 1 moon. |
| Mars | "Red planet" (iron oxide). Olympus Mons, the largest volcano in the solar system. 2 moons: Phobos, Deimos. |
| Jupiter | Largest. Great Red Spot (a storm). Fastest rotation (about 10 hours). About 95 known moons; Ganymede is the largest moon in the solar system. |
| Saturn | Bright rings of ice and rock. Least dense planet (less than water). Most known moons (over 270 by 2025); Titan has a thick atmosphere. |
| Uranus | Tilted about 98°, so it rolls on its side. Rotates backwards. Coldest atmosphere. |
| Neptune | Farthest. Strongest winds. Year = 165 Earth years. Moon Triton orbits backwards. |

Moon counts change as new moons are confirmed. Give them as "about".

Memory line for order: **M**y **V**ery **E**ducated **M**other **J**ust **S**erved **U**s **N**oodles.

### Kepler's laws of planetary motion (asked 2024)

1. **Law of orbits.** Each planet moves in an ellipse with the Sun at one focus.
2. **Law of areas.** The line from the Sun to a planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times. So a planet moves fastest at perihelion (nearest) and slowest at aphelion (farthest).
3. **Law of periods.** The square of the orbital period is proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis: T² ∝ a³. With T in years and a in AU, T² = a³.

Example: a planet at a = 4 AU has T² = 64, so T = 8 years.

**Diagram:** an ellipse; Sun at the left focus; mark perihelion and aphelion; shade two equal-area sectors, a short fat one near the Sun and a long thin one far from it.

## §5 Sun

| Property | Value |
|---|---|
| Type | Yellow dwarf, main sequence star (class G2) |
| Age | About 4.6 billion years; about half-way through its life |
| Diameter | About 1.39 million km (109 times Earth) |
| Mass | 2 × 10³⁰ kg (333,000 Earths) |
| Composition | About 73% hydrogen, 25% helium, 2% heavier elements (by mass) |
| Surface temperature | About 5,500 °C (5,800 K) |
| Core temperature | About 15 million °C |
| Distance from Earth | 149.6 million km (1 AU) |
| Rotation | About 25 days at the equator, about 35 near the poles |

**Energy source: nuclear fusion.** In the core, four hydrogen nuclei fuse into one helium nucleus. A little mass is lost and becomes energy (E = mc²). The Sun turns about 4 million tonnes of matter into energy every second.

**Layers (centre outward):** core → radiative zone → convective zone → photosphere (visible surface) → chromosphere → corona (outer atmosphere, 1–2 million °C, seen in a total eclipse).

**Features:** sunspots (cooler dark patches; number peaks every 11 years), solar flares, prominences, solar wind (stream of charged particles that causes auroras and can disturb satellites and power grids).

**Why does a ball of gas have strong gravity? (asked 2019)** Gravity depends on mass, not on whether matter is solid or gas. The Sun has 99.8% of the solar system's mass, so its pull is huge. Its own gravity squeezes the gas inward; pressure from fusion pushes outward. The balance (hydrostatic equilibrium) keeps it stable.

**Future:** in about 5 billion years it will swell into a red giant, shed its outer layers and end as a white dwarf. It is too small to become a supernova or black hole.

**Diagram:** concentric circles labelled core, radiative zone, convective zone, photosphere; outer wisps labelled corona; a dark dot labelled sunspot.

## §6 Earth

| Property | Value |
|---|---|
| Position | Third planet from the Sun |
| Age | About 4.54 billion years |
| Shape | Oblate spheroid (geoid): flattened at poles, bulged at equator |
| Equatorial / polar radius | 6,378 km / 6,357 km (mean 6,371 km) |
| Circumference | About 40,075 km at the equator |
| Tilt of axis | 23.5° from the vertical to its orbital plane |
| Rotation | 23 h 56 min 4 s (taken as 24 h), west to east |
| Revolution | 365 days 5 h 48 min 46 s (about 365¼ days) |
| Orbital speed | About 29.8 km/s |
| Surface | 71% water, 29% land |
| Moon | One; mean distance 384,400 km |

**Internal structure**

| Layer | Depth / thickness | State and material |
|---|---|---|
| Crust | 5–10 km (oceanic), 30–70 km (continental) | Solid rock. Continental crust is granitic; oceanic crust is basaltic. |
| Mantle | To 2,900 km | Solid but slowly flowing silicate rock. Upper part includes the soft asthenosphere. |
| Outer core | 2,900–5,150 km | Liquid iron and nickel. Its motion makes Earth's magnetic field. |
| Inner core | 5,150–6,371 km | Solid iron and nickel, about 5,000–6,000 °C. |

Why Earth supports life: right distance from the Sun (liquid water), protective atmosphere with oxygen and an ozone layer, magnetic field that deflects solar wind, moderate temperature range.

## §7 Solar and lunar eclipses

**Eclipse:** one body passes into the shadow of another. Shadow parts: **umbra** (full shadow) and **penumbra** (partial shadow).

| Point | Solar eclipse | Lunar eclipse |
|---|---|---|
| Arrangement | Sun – **Moon** – Earth | Sun – **Earth** – Moon |
| Moon phase | New moon | Full moon |
| What is hidden | The Sun (Moon's shadow falls on Earth) | The Moon (Earth's shadow falls on it) |
| Types | Total, partial, annular (ring), hybrid | Total, partial, penumbral |
| Seen from | A narrow track on Earth | The whole night side of Earth |
| Duration | Totality at most about 7.5 minutes | Totality up to about 1 hour 45 minutes |
| Safety | Never look directly without certified filters | Safe to watch |

- **Annular eclipse:** the Moon is near its farthest point, looks smaller than the Sun, and leaves a bright ring.
- **Blood moon:** in a total lunar eclipse the Moon looks red. Earth's atmosphere bends sunlight into the shadow and scatters out the blue.
- **Why not every month?** The Moon's orbit is tilted about 5° to Earth's orbit. Eclipses happen only when a new or full moon falls near the crossing points (nodes). There are 4–7 eclipses a year in total.

**Diagrams (draw both, one above the other):**

```
Solar:   SUN  ------>  (Moon)  ---umbra--->  EARTH      [observer in umbra sees total eclipse]
Lunar:   SUN  ------>  EARTH  ---umbra--->  (Moon)      [Moon inside Earth's umbra]
```
Draw the umbra as a dark cone and the penumbra as a lighter, widening cone on each side.

## §8 Rotation and revolution

| Point | Rotation | Revolution |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Earth spinning on its own axis | Earth moving around the Sun in its orbit |
| Direction | West to east | Anticlockwise seen from above the north pole |
| Time | About 24 hours | About 365¼ days |
| Speed | About 1,670 km/h at the equator, zero at the poles | About 107,000 km/h |

### Effects of rotation
1. **Day and night.**
2. **Apparent movement** of the Sun, Moon and stars from east to west.
3. **Time differences.** 360° in 24 hours = 15° of longitude per hour (1° = 4 minutes). Pakistan Standard Time is UTC+5.
4. **Coriolis effect.** Winds and ocean currents are deflected to the right in the northern hemisphere and to the left in the southern.
5. **Shape.** Bulge at the equator, flattening at the poles.
6. **Tides** come twice a day because the Earth turns under the two tidal bulges raised by the Moon and Sun.

### Effects of revolution (with the 23.5° tilt)
1. **Seasons.** The hemisphere tilted towards the Sun has summer.
2. **Changing length of day and night** through the year.
3. **Solstices and equinoxes.**

| Date | Event | Sun overhead at | Northern hemisphere |
|---|---|---|---|
| 21 March | Spring equinox | Equator | Day = night |
| 21 June | Summer solstice | Tropic of Cancer (23.5° N) | Longest day |
| 23 September | Autumn equinox | Equator | Day = night |
| 22 December | Winter solstice | Tropic of Capricorn (23.5° S) | Shortest day |

4. **Changing height of the noon Sun** and shift of wind and pressure belts.
5. **The year and leap year.** The extra quarter day adds up to one day every four years.
6. **Perihelion** (nearest, about 3 January, 147 million km) and **aphelion** (farthest, about 4 July, 152 million km). Seasons are caused by tilt, not by distance.

**Diagram:** the Sun in the centre; four Earths on an ellipse at the four dates; each Earth drawn with its axis tilted the same way; label seasons for the northern hemisphere.

## §9 Weather variables

**Weather** is the state of the atmosphere at a place over a short time. Its elements are temperature, pressure, wind, humidity, precipitation and cloud.

### Global temperature
- Measured by thermometer in °C, °F or K. Global mean surface temperature is about 15 °C.
- Source of heat: solar radiation (insolation). The ground heats the air above it.
- **Factors:** latitude (angle of the Sun), altitude (temperature falls about 6.5 °C per km), distance from the sea (land heats and cools faster), ocean currents, prevailing winds, cloud cover, slope and aspect.
- Heat moves by radiation, conduction, convection and advection (horizontal).

### Pressure
- Weight of the air column on a unit area. Sea-level average: **1013.25 millibars** (760 mm of mercury).
- Measured by barometer. Lines of equal pressure on a map are isobars.
- Pressure falls with height (roughly halves every 5.5 km).
- Warm air rises: low pressure, cloud and rain. Cold air sinks: high pressure, clear sky.
- **Pressure belts:**

| Belt | Latitude | Name / weather |
|---|---|---|
| Equatorial low | 0°–5° | Doldrums; rising air, heavy rain |
| Subtropical high | about 30° N and S | Horse latitudes; sinking air, deserts |
| Subpolar low | about 60° N and S | Rising air, storms |
| Polar high | 90° N and S | Cold sinking air |

### Circulation (winds)
- Wind blows from high to low pressure and is turned by the Coriolis effect.
- **Three cells in each hemisphere:** Hadley cell (0°–30°), Ferrel cell (30°–60°), Polar cell (60°–90°).
- **Planetary winds:** trade winds (30° towards the equator, from the north-east and south-east), westerlies (30°–60°), polar easterlies (60°–90°).
- **Upper air:** less friction, so winds are faster and flow almost parallel to isobars (geostrophic wind). **Jet streams** are narrow fast westerly currents near the tropopause (9–12 km) at speeds above 100 km/h.
- **Seasonal winds:** monsoon (reversal of wind between summer and winter; brings Pakistan's July–September rain).
- **Local winds:** sea breeze (day), land breeze (night), mountain and valley winds.

```
90N  Polar HIGH      | polar easterlies
60N  Subpolar LOW    | westerlies
30N  Subtropical HIGH| NE trade winds
 0   Equatorial LOW  | (doldrums)
30S  Subtropical HIGH| SE trade winds
60S  Subpolar LOW    | westerlies
90S  Polar HIGH      | polar easterlies
```

### Precipitation
- Water falling from clouds: rain, drizzle, snow, sleet, hail.
- Forms when moist air rises, cools to its dew point and condenses on tiny particles.
- **Three types of rainfall:**
  1. **Convectional:** ground heating makes air rise (equatorial afternoons, summer thunderstorms).
  2. **Orographic (relief):** air is forced up a mountain; the far (leeward) side stays dry, a "rain shadow". Example: Murree gets heavy rain.
  3. **Frontal (cyclonic):** warm air rises over cold air at a front.
- Measured by rain gauge in mm.

### Humidity
- Amount of water vapour in the air.
- **Absolute humidity:** grams of vapour per cubic metre of air.
- **Relative humidity:** vapour present ÷ maximum vapour the air can hold at that temperature × 100%.
- Warm air holds more vapour. Cooling raises relative humidity. At 100% the air is saturated; that temperature is the **dew point**.
- Measured by hygrometer (wet and dry bulb thermometers).

| Element | Instrument | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| Temperature | Thermometer | °C |
| Pressure | Barometer | millibar (hPa) |
| Wind speed | Anemometer | km/h, knots |
| Wind direction | Wind vane | compass point |
| Humidity | Hygrometer | % |
| Rainfall | Rain gauge | mm |

## §10 Weather variations

**Weather vs climate**

| Point | Weather | Climate |
|---|---|---|
| Time scale | Hours to days | Average of at least 30 years |
| Area | A place | A region |
| Change | Fast | Slow |
| Studied by | Meteorology | Climatology |

**Why weather varies:**
- **Daily:** heating by day, cooling by night.
- **Short term (days):** moving pressure systems. Lows (depressions) bring cloud, wind and rain. Highs (anticyclones) bring settled weather. Air masses meet at fronts. (MCQ 2026: rapid weather change is driven by pressure systems.)
- **Seasonal:** tilt and revolution; shifting pressure belts; monsoon.
- **Year to year:** El Niño (warm eastern Pacific; tends to weaken the South Asian monsoon) and La Niña (the reverse; linked to Pakistan's 2010 and 2022 floods).
- **Long term:** climate change.

**Factors that control the climate of a place (asked 2022):**
1. Latitude.
2. Altitude.
3. Distance from the sea (continentality). Karachi is mild; Jacobabad is extreme.
4. Ocean currents.
5. Prevailing winds and air masses.
6. Mountains (relief): barrier and rain shadow.
7. Vegetation and land use (forests cool and moisten; cities form heat islands).
8. Human activity (greenhouse gases).

**Pakistan's weather systems:** summer monsoon (July–September, from the Bay of Bengal and Arabian Sea), western disturbances (winter rain and snow from the Mediterranean, December–March), heatwaves in May–June, and occasional Arabian Sea cyclones.
