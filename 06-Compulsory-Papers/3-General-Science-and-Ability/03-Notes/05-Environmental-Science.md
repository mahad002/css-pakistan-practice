This is the most repeated science section and it also feeds Essay, Current Affairs and Pakistan Affairs.

**Environment** = everything around us, living and non-living. It has four linked spheres: atmosphere (air), hydrosphere (water), lithosphere (land), biosphere (life).

## §1 Atmosphere

**Definition.** The envelope of gases held around the Earth by gravity.

### Composition (dry air, by volume)

| Gas | Share |
|---|---|
| Nitrogen (N₂) | 78.08% |
| Oxygen (O₂) | 20.95% |
| Argon (Ar) | 0.93% |
| Carbon dioxide (CO₂) | about 0.04% (about 425 parts per million, rising) |
| Trace gases | Neon, helium, methane, krypton, hydrogen, nitrous oxide, ozone |
| Water vapour | 0–4%, varies with place and time |

Also dust, smoke, salt and pollen (aerosols).

### Layered structure (asked 2020, 2021, 2023)

**Basis of division:** the way temperature changes with height. Each layer ends at a "pause" where the trend reverses.

| Layer | Height | Temperature trend | Key features |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Troposphere** | 0 to about 12 km (8 km at poles, 18 km at equator) | Falls about 6.5 °C per km, to about −55 °C | Holds about 75–80% of the air and nearly all water vapour. All weather happens here. Top: tropopause. |
| **Stratosphere** | 12–50 km | **Rises** with height | Contains the **ozone layer** (15–35 km), which absorbs UV and warms the layer. Calm; jet aircraft cruise in its lower part. Top: stratopause. |
| **Mesosphere** | 50–85 km | Falls to about −90 °C | Coldest layer. Meteors burn up here. Top: mesopause. |
| **Thermosphere** | 85 to about 600 km | Rises to over 1,000 °C | Very thin air absorbs X-rays and extreme UV. Contains the **ionosphere**, which reflects radio waves. **Auroras** form here. The International Space Station and other low-orbit **satellites** fly here (about 400 km and above). |
| **Exosphere** | Above about 600 km | — | Thins out into space. Hydrogen and helium. Higher satellites orbit here. |

```
 Height
 (km)
 600+ | EXOSPHERE     -> space; satellites
      |- - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - -
  85  | THERMOSPHERE  temp rises; ionosphere, auroras, ISS
      |- - - mesopause (about -90 C) - - - - -
  50  | MESOSPHERE    temp falls; meteors burn
      |- - - stratopause - - - - - - - - - - -
  12  | STRATOSPHERE  temp rises; OZONE LAYER
      |- - - tropopause - - - - - - - - - - - -
   0  | TROPOSPHERE   temp falls; weather, clouds
```
In the exam, draw this as horizontal bands with a zig-zag temperature line running up through them.

**Factors that decide the layers:** absorption of solar radiation at different heights (ground heating at the bottom, ozone absorbing UV in the stratosphere, oxygen and nitrogen absorbing X-rays in the thermosphere), gravity (density falls with height), and composition.

**Role in the radiation balance (asked 2020)**
- Of incoming sunlight, about 30% is reflected back to space by clouds, air, ice and bright surfaces (albedo). About 20% is absorbed by the atmosphere. About 50% is absorbed by the ground.
- The thermosphere absorbs X-rays; the stratosphere's ozone absorbs harmful UV.
- The warm ground sends out long-wave infrared. Greenhouse gases in the troposphere absorb part of it and send it back down. This keeps the average surface at about 15 °C instead of −18 °C.
- Over time, energy in equals energy out. If more is trapped, the planet warms.

**Importance of the atmosphere:** oxygen for breathing, CO₂ for photosynthesis, shields life from UV and meteors, keeps the planet warm, carries water as part of the water cycle, carries sound and radio waves.

## §2 Hydrosphere

**Definition.** All the water on, under and above the Earth's surface: oceans, ice, groundwater, lakes, rivers, soil moisture and water vapour. Water covers about 71% of the surface.

### Major water compartments

| Compartment | Share of all water |
|---|---|
| Oceans and seas (salt water) | about 97.5% |
| **Fresh water** | about 2.5% |

| Fresh water is held in | Share of fresh water |
|---|---|
| Ice caps and glaciers | about 69% |
| Groundwater | about 30% |
| Lakes, rivers, soil, air, living things | about 1% |

- Less than 1% of all water is easily usable fresh water.
- Five oceans: Pacific (largest and deepest; Mariana Trench about 11 km), Atlantic, Indian, Southern, Arctic (smallest). Average salinity: 35 parts per thousand.
- Water stays in the air about 9 days, in rivers weeks, in deep groundwater and oceans for hundreds to thousands of years.

### Water (hydrological) cycle (asked 2016, 2021, 2026)

**Definition.** The continuous movement of water between oceans, air and land, driven by the Sun's heat and by gravity.

| Process | What happens |
|---|---|
| Evaporation | Sun turns liquid water from seas, lakes and soil into vapour |
| Transpiration | Plants give off vapour from their leaves |
| Sublimation | Ice turns straight to vapour |
| Condensation | Rising vapour cools and forms droplets: clouds, fog, dew |
| Precipitation | Water falls as rain, snow, hail |
| Infiltration and percolation | Water soaks into soil and down to groundwater |
| Surface run-off | Water flows over land into streams and rivers |
| Groundwater flow | Slow movement underground to springs, rivers and the sea |
| Storage | Held in oceans, ice, lakes, aquifers |

```
            condensation (clouds)  ---- wind ---->   precipitation
                 ^                                      |
   evaporation   |          transpiration               v
  ~~~~ OCEAN ~~~~|             ^  ^           snow/ice, run-off, infiltration
                 |<---- rivers / groundwater flow <-----|
```
Draw mountains on the right, sea on the left, a sun, and arrows in a loop.

**Importance:**
1. It is nature's distillation: evaporation leaves salt behind and delivers fresh water to land.
2. Refills rivers, lakes, glaciers and aquifers; keeps fresh water moving to places far from the sea.
3. Moves heat around the planet and moderates climate.
4. Supports farming, drinking water and hydropower.
5. Shapes the land by erosion and deposition; carries nutrients.
6. Cleans the air and water.

## §3 Biosphere

**Definition.** The part of the Earth where life exists: the thin zone where air, water and land meet. It reaches from the deep ocean floor to about 8–10 km up.

- **Ecosystem:** a community of living things interacting with their non-living surroundings.
- **Biome:** a very large ecosystem with a typical climate, plants and animals.

### Major biomes

| Biome | Climate | Life | Where |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tropical rainforest | Hot, wet all year (over 2,000 mm rain) | Greatest biodiversity; tall evergreen trees in layers | Amazon, Congo, Indonesia |
| Savanna (tropical grassland) | Hot; wet and dry seasons | Grass with scattered trees; large grazing herds | East Africa, Brazil |
| Desert | Under 250 mm rain; large daily temperature range | Cacti, thorny shrubs; camels, reptiles | Sahara, Arabian, Thar, Cholistan |
| Temperate grassland | Hot summer, cold winter, moderate rain | Grasses; fertile soil; wheat belts | Prairies, steppes, pampas |
| Temperate deciduous forest | Four seasons | Oak, maple, beech; trees shed leaves | Europe, eastern USA, China |
| Mediterranean scrub | Hot dry summer, mild wet winter | Olive, evergreen shrubs | Mediterranean coast, California |
| Taiga (boreal coniferous forest) | Long cold winter, short summer | Pine, spruce, fir; largest land biome | Siberia, Canada |
| Tundra | Very cold; frozen subsoil (permafrost) | Mosses, lichens, no trees; reindeer | Arctic rim |
| Mountain (alpine) | Changes with height | Zones from forest to meadow to snow | Himalaya, Karakoram |
| Freshwater | Lakes, rivers, wetlands | Fish, amphibians, reeds | Worldwide |
| Marine | Oceans, coral reefs, estuaries, mangroves | Plankton to whales; largest biome | 71% of the Earth |

Pakistan's range: Indus-delta mangroves, Thar and Cholistan deserts, irrigated plains, Ziarat juniper forest, northern coniferous forests, alpine meadows and glaciers.

**Energy in the biosphere (asked 2023)**
- The Sun is the source. Green plants (producers) trap about 1% of sunlight by photosynthesis and store it as chemical energy.
- Energy passes along food chains: producers → herbivores → carnivores → decomposers. Only about 10% passes to the next level; the rest is lost as heat.
- Energy resources the biosphere gives us: food; **biomass** (wood, crop waste, dung); **biofuels** (biogas, ethanol, biodiesel); and **fossil fuels**, which are ancient biomass.

## §4 Lithosphere

**Definition.** The rigid outer shell of the Earth: the crust plus the solid top of the mantle. It is about 100 km thick and is broken into tectonic plates.

**Lithosphere vs asthenosphere (asked 2021)**

| Point | Lithosphere | Asthenosphere |
|---|---|---|
| Position | Outermost; crust + uppermost mantle | Below the lithosphere, in the upper mantle |
| Depth | 0 to about 100 km | About 100 to 350 km (or deeper) |
| State | Cold, rigid, brittle | Hot, soft, plastic; flows slowly |
| Behaviour | Breaks into plates; earthquakes occur | Convection currents carry the plates |
| Density | Lower | Higher |

**Components of the lithosphere:**
- **Continental crust:** 30–70 km thick, granitic, lighter, older.
- **Oceanic crust:** 5–10 km thick, basaltic, denser, younger.
- **Uppermost mantle:** rigid peridotite rock.
- Materials: minerals, rocks and soil. Most common elements in the crust: oxygen (about 47%), silicon (28%), aluminium (8%), iron (5%).

### Minerals

**Definition.** A naturally occurring, inorganic solid with a definite chemical composition and an ordered crystal structure.

- Examples: quartz (SiO₂), feldspar (the most abundant in the crust), mica, calcite (CaCO₃), halite (rock salt, NaCl), gypsum, haematite (iron ore).
- Groups: silicates (about 90% of the crust), carbonates, oxides, sulphides, sulphates, halides, native elements (gold, copper, diamond).
- Identified by: hardness (Mohs scale, 1 = talc to 10 = diamond), lustre, colour, streak, cleavage, density.
- Pakistan: Khewra rock salt, Reko Diq and Saindak copper and gold, Thar coal, chromite at Muslim Bagh, gypsum, marble, gemstones in the north.

### Rocks and rock types

A **rock** is a natural mass of one or more minerals.

| Type | How formed | Features | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Igneous** | Cooling of molten magma or lava | Crystalline; no fossils. Intrusive rocks cool slowly underground (large crystals). Extrusive rocks cool fast at the surface (fine grains). | Granite (intrusive); basalt, pumice (extrusive) |
| **Sedimentary** | Sediments are deposited in layers, compacted and cemented | Layered; contain fossils; hold oil, gas, coal | Sandstone, shale, limestone, rock salt, coal |
| **Metamorphic** | Existing rock changed by heat and pressure without melting | Hard; banded or crystalline | Limestone → marble; shale → slate; sandstone → quartzite; granite → gneiss |

**Rock cycle**
```
 MAGMA --cooling--> IGNEOUS --weathering, erosion, deposition--> SEDIMENTARY
   ^                    |                                            |
   |                heat + pressure                           heat + pressure
   |                    v                                            v
   +------melting---- METAMORPHIC <----------------------------------+
```

### Plate tectonics

**Theory.** The lithosphere is broken into about seven large plates and several small ones. They move a few centimetres a year over the soft asthenosphere. Mantle convection, the push at ridges and the pull of sinking slabs drive them.

- Major plates: Pacific, North American, South American, Eurasian, African, Indo-Australian, Antarctic.
- Background: Alfred Wegener's continental drift (1912; one supercontinent, Pangaea) and Harry Hess's sea-floor spreading (1960s).
- Evidence: jigsaw fit of continents, matching fossils and rocks across oceans, magnetic stripes on the sea floor, the pattern of earthquakes and volcanoes, GPS measurements.

| Boundary | Motion | Result | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Divergent (constructive) | Plates move apart | New crust, mid-ocean ridges, rift valleys, volcanoes | Mid-Atlantic Ridge, East African Rift |
| Convergent (destructive) | Plates collide | Ocean plate sinks (subduction): trenches, volcanoes, strong earthquakes. Two continents: fold mountains. | Andes, Japan; Himalayas (Indian and Eurasian plates) |
| Transform (conservative) | Plates slide past | Earthquakes; no crust made or lost | San Andreas Fault; Chaman Fault (Pakistan) |

**Diagram:** three small sketches side by side: arrows apart with magma rising; one plate diving under another with a volcano above; two plates sliding in opposite directions.

## §5 Atmospheric (air) pollution: overview

**Definition.** The presence in air of substances in amounts that harm people, other living things, materials or the climate.

| Basis | Types |
|---|---|
| Origin | **Primary** pollutants are emitted directly (CO, SO₂, NOx, soot, VOCs). **Secondary** pollutants form in the air (ozone, PAN, sulphuric and nitric acid). |
| Source | Natural (volcanoes, dust storms, forest fires, pollen) and human-made |
| Place | Outdoor (ambient) and indoor (cooking smoke, tobacco) |
| Form | Gases and particles |

**Human sources:** vehicles, power plants, factories, brick kilns, burning of crop residue and rubbish, household stoves, construction dust, refrigerants.

**Effects (ten for a list answer, asked 2025)**
1. Asthma, bronchitis and other lung disease.
2. Heart attacks and strokes.
3. Lung cancer.
4. Eye, nose and throat irritation; harm to unborn children.
5. Smog and poor visibility; road and flight disruption.
6. Acid rain.
7. Ozone-layer depletion.
8. Greenhouse warming and climate change.
9. Lower crop yields and forest damage.
10. Corrosion of buildings, monuments and metals; harm to wildlife and water bodies.

WHO links about 7 million early deaths a year to air pollution.

**Control**
- Cleaner fuels (low-sulphur, Euro-5), renewable energy, energy saving.
- Vehicles: catalytic converters, emission testing, public transport, electric vehicles.
- Industry: electrostatic precipitators and bag filters (remove particles), scrubbers (remove SO₂), taller stacks, zig-zag brick kilns.
- Ban open burning of waste and crop residue; give farmers machines to manage stubble.
- Plant trees; monitor air quality; enforce emission standards.

## §6 Major air pollutants

| Pollutant | Sources | Effects |
|---|---|---|
| **Carbon monoxide (CO)** | Incomplete burning of fuel: vehicles, stoves, generators | Colourless, odourless. Binds to haemoglobin over 200 times more strongly than oxygen, so blood carries less oxygen: headache, dizziness, death in closed spaces. |
| **Carbon dioxide (CO₂)** | Burning fossil fuels, deforestation, cement | Main greenhouse gas; ocean acidification |
| **Particulate matter (PM)** | Diesel engines, coal, kilns, crop burning, dust, secondary particles | **PM10** (under 10 micrometres) lodges in the nose and throat. **PM2.5** (under 2.5 micrometres) reaches deep into the lungs and the blood: asthma, heart disease, cancer. Haze; soot warms the climate. |
| **Nitrogen oxides (NO, NO₂)** | High-temperature burning in engines and power plants; lightning | Lung irritation; main cause of photochemical smog; form ozone and nitric acid (acid rain) |
| **Sulphur oxides (SO₂, SO₃)** | Burning coal and oil that contain sulphur; smelters; volcanoes | Choking gas; bronchitis; sulphuric acid (acid rain); damages plants and stone |
| **Tropospheric ozone (O₃)** | Secondary: NOx + VOCs + sunlight | Irritates lungs, worsens asthma, cuts crop yields, cracks rubber; a greenhouse gas. Ozone is "good up high, bad nearby". |
| **Volatile organic compounds (VOCs)** | Petrol vapour, solvents, paints, exhaust, industry; some from trees | Irritation, headaches; benzene causes leukaemia; they form ozone and smog |
| **Dioxins** | Burning waste that contains chlorine (PVC, hospital waste), open rubbish fires, chlorine bleaching of paper, some pesticide manufacture | Very toxic and persistent; collect in animal fat and move up the food chain (most human intake is from meat, milk and fish); cancer, skin disease (chloracne), harm to immunity, hormones and reproduction |

**Organic particulate matter (asked 2020).** Particles made of carbon compounds: soot (black carbon), unburnt hydrocarbons and tar-like compounds (PAHs). Entry routes: incomplete burning of diesel, coal, wood, crop waste and rubbish; cooking and tobacco smoke; wildfires; and gases (VOCs from vehicles, solvents and trees) that react in sunlight and condense into particles. Pollen and spores are natural organic particles.

### Smog (asked 2018, 2023)

**Smog = smoke + fog.**

| Point | Fog | Smog |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | Natural; tiny water droplets near the ground | Fog or haze mixed with smoke and chemical pollutants |
| Colour | White | Grey, yellow or brown |
| Health | Harmless | Harmful |
| Clears | When the Sun warms the air | Stays for days under an inversion |

| Type | Classical (London) smog | Photochemical (Los Angeles) smog |
|---|---|---|
| Made of | Smoke + SO₂ + fog | NOx + VOCs + sunlight → ozone, PAN, aldehydes |
| Weather | Cold, damp, winter mornings | Warm, sunny, afternoons |
| Source | Coal burning | Vehicle exhaust |
| Example | London, December 1952 (thousands died) | Los Angeles |

**Lahore and Punjab smog (October–January):** vehicle exhaust, industry and brick kilns, burning of rice stubble, waste burning and dust. In winter a **temperature inversion** (a lid of warm air over cold air) and weak wind trap the pollutants near the ground.

**Health effects:** cough, wheezing, asthma attacks, bronchitis, burning eyes, heart attacks and strokes, weaker lung growth in children, shorter life expectancy. Also road accidents, flight delays, school closures.

**Control:** cleaner fuel and vehicle checks, public transport, zig-zag kilns, alternatives to stubble burning, industrial filters, urban trees, air-quality alerts.

**Newer technologies against smog:** smog towers (giant outdoor air filters), cloud seeding for artificial rain (tried in Lahore in December 2023), anti-smog guns that spray fine water mist, photocatalytic paints and paving that break down NOx, satellite tracking of fires.

## §7 Acid rain (asked 2017, 2018)

**Definition.** Rain, snow or fog with a pH below 5.6. Normal rain is slightly acidic (about 5.6) because CO₂ dissolves in it to form weak carbonic acid.

**Cause:** sulphur dioxide and nitrogen oxides from power plants, factories, smelters and vehicles. (Natural: volcanoes, lightning.)

**Formation**
- 2SO₂ + O₂ → 2SO₃, then SO₃ + H₂O → H₂SO₄ (sulphuric acid)
- 4NO₂ + O₂ + 2H₂O → 4HNO₃ (nitric acid)
- The acids fall as wet deposition (rain, snow, fog) or dry deposition (gases and particles). Wind can carry them hundreds of kilometres.

**Effects**
1. **Lakes and rivers:** water turns acidic; fish, eggs and insects die; toxic aluminium is released from soil.
2. **Forests:** leaves are damaged; calcium and magnesium are washed out of soil; trees weaken.
3. **Soil and crops:** loss of nutrients and soil microbes; lower yields.
4. **Buildings:** marble and limestone are eaten away. CaCO₃ + H₂SO₄ → CaSO₄ + H₂O + CO₂. The Taj Mahal is a known case.
5. **Metals:** faster rusting of bridges, vehicles, rails.
6. **Health:** the same gases and fine sulphate particles irritate lungs.

**Prevention:** low-sulphur coal and oil, washing coal, scrubbers on chimneys (flue-gas desulphurisation with lime), catalytic converters, switching to gas and renewables, saving energy, adding lime to acid lakes, international emission agreements.

**Diagram:** factory and car on the left giving off "SO₂, NOx"; arrow up into a cloud "H₂SO₄, HNO₃"; wind arrow to the right; rain falling on forest, lake and building labelled "acid rain".

## §8 Ozone depletion (asked 2017, 2019)

- **Ozone (O₃)** is a form of oxygen with three atoms. The **ozone layer** lies in the stratosphere at 15–35 km. It absorbs most of the Sun's harmful ultraviolet (UV-B and UV-C).
- Thickness is measured in **Dobson units**. Normal is about 300; under 220 is called a "hole".

**Definition.** Ozone depletion is the thinning of the ozone layer caused by chemicals released by human activity.

**Ozone-depleting substances**

| Substance | Use |
|---|---|
| Chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) | Refrigerators, air conditioners, aerosol sprays, foam |
| Halons | Fire extinguishers |
| Carbon tetrachloride, methyl chloroform | Solvents |
| Methyl bromide | Soil fumigant |
| HCFCs | Replacement refrigerants (milder) |

**Mechanism**
1. CFCs are stable and drift up to the stratosphere.
2. UV breaks them and frees a chlorine atom.
3. Cl + O₃ → ClO + O₂
4. ClO + O → Cl + O₂
5. The chlorine atom is free again. One atom can destroy about 100,000 ozone molecules.

- The Antarctic **ozone hole** was reported in 1985. It opens each southern spring (September–November) because ice clouds in the polar stratosphere speed the reactions.

**Effects:** more skin cancer and sunburn, eye cataracts, weaker immunity, damage to crops, harm to plankton (base of the sea food chain), faster breakdown of plastics and paints.

**Protection**
- **Vienna Convention (1985)** and the **Montreal Protocol (1987)**: see §10.
- Use ozone-friendly refrigerants (hydrocarbons, ammonia, CO₂).
- Recover and destroy CFCs from old fridges and air conditioners.
- Avoid halon extinguishers and methyl bromide.
- Public awareness. World Ozone Day: 16 September.
- Result: the layer is healing and is expected to return to its 1980 state around 2040 over most of the world and around 2066 over Antarctica.

Do not mix this up with global warming. Ozone loss lets in more UV; it is not the main cause of warming.

## §9 Greenhouse effect and global warming (asked 2016, 2019, 2022)

**Greenhouse effect.** A natural process in which certain gases in the air trap heat leaving the Earth and keep the surface warm.

**How it works**
1. Sunlight (short-wave) passes through the air and warms the ground.
2. The ground gives off heat as long-wave infrared.
3. Greenhouse gases absorb much of this and send it back down.
4. The surface stays near 15 °C. Without the effect it would be about −18 °C.

So the natural greenhouse effect is a **blessing**: it makes life possible.

**Greenhouse gases**

| Gas | Main human sources | Warming power over 100 years (CO₂ = 1) |
|---|---|---|
| Carbon dioxide (CO₂) | Fossil fuels, deforestation, cement | 1 |
| Methane (CH₄) | Rice fields, cattle, landfills, gas leaks | about 28 |
| Nitrous oxide (N₂O) | Fertilizers, burning | about 270 |
| Fluorinated gases (HFCs, SF₆) | Refrigeration, industry | hundreds to thousands |
| Water vapour | Natural; rises as air warms | — |

**Enhanced greenhouse effect.** Human activity has raised greenhouse gas levels. CO₂ has gone from about 280 ppm before industry to about 425 ppm now. More heat is trapped. The result is **global warming**: a rise in the Earth's average temperature. **Climate change** is the wider shift in weather patterns that follows.

**Evidence:** the world is about 1.3 °C warmer than in 1850–1900. 2024 was the hottest year on record and the first calendar year above 1.5 °C. Glaciers and Arctic ice are shrinking. Sea level has risen over 20 cm since 1900.

**Effects**
- More and stronger heatwaves, floods, droughts, cyclones and wildfires.
- Melting glaciers and ice sheets; rising seas; coastal flooding.
- Acidic oceans; coral bleaching.
- Lower crop yields; water shortage; food insecurity.
- Spread of dengue and malaria; heat deaths.
- Loss of species; climate migration.

**Effects on Pakistan (asked 2022).** Pakistan emits under 1% of world greenhouse gases but ranks among the most vulnerable countries.
1. Floods: 2010 and 2022 (33 million affected; about US$30 billion in damage and loss).
2. Heatwaves: Karachi 2015 (over 1,200 deaths); Jacobabad above 50 °C.
3. Glacier melt: over 7,000 glaciers; thousands of glacial lakes; outburst floods (GLOFs) in Gilgit-Baltistan and Chitral.
4. Erratic monsoon; droughts in Thar and Balochistan.
5. Lower wheat, rice and cotton yields; water stress.
6. Sea intrusion in the Indus delta; cyclone risk to Karachi.
7. More dengue and malaria; smog; migration.

**Steps to control climate change**
- **Mitigation (cut the cause):** renewable energy, energy efficiency, public transport and electric vehicles, stop deforestation and plant trees (Ten Billion Tree Tsunami), cut methane from waste and farms, carbon pricing.
- **Adaptation (cope with the effect):** flood protection and early warning, water storage, drought- and heat-tolerant crops, climate-proof buildings and roads, heat-action plans, mangrove restoration.
- **Policy:** National Climate Change Policy (2012, updated 2021); pledges under the Paris Agreement (60% renewable power and 30% electric vehicles by 2030, conditional on finance); National Adaptation Plan (2023); the Loss and Damage Fund agreed at COP27 (2022).

### Why rising CO₂ worries scientists; the carbon cycle (asked 2019)

- CO₂ is needed for photosynthesis and for the natural greenhouse effect. The worry is the rapid rise.
- It is the main driver of warming and stays in the air for centuries.
- Oceans absorb it and turn more acidic, which harms corals and shellfish.

**Carbon cycle**
```
                 CO2 in ATMOSPHERE
   photosynthesis |   ^ respiration   ^ combustion    ^ decay   <-> exchange with OCEAN
                  v   |               |               |
               PLANTS -> ANIMALS -> dead matter -> FOSSIL FUELS (coal, oil, gas) over millions of years
```
Removing CO₂: photosynthesis, dissolving in oceans. Adding CO₂: respiration, decay, burning, volcanoes. Humans have tipped the balance by burning fossil fuels and clearing forests.

**Diagram for the greenhouse effect:** Sun at top left; arrow "short-wave radiation" to the ground; arrow up from the ground "infrared heat"; a band of dots "greenhouse gases: CO₂, CH₄, N₂O, H₂O"; some arrows escaping to space, others bending back down "trapped heat".

## §10 International agreements on air pollution

### Montreal Protocol

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Full name | Montreal Protocol on Substances that Deplete the Ozone Layer |
| Under | Vienna Convention (1985) |
| Signed | 16 September 1987; in force 1989 |
| Aim | Phase out production and use of ozone-depleting substances (CFCs, halons, carbon tetrachloride, methyl bromide, HCFCs) |
| Members | Every UN member state: the first treaty with universal ratification |
| Features | Fixed phase-out dates; longer time for developing countries; a Multilateral Fund to help them; trade limits with non-parties |
| Kigali Amendment (2016) | Phase-down of HFCs, which do not harm ozone but are strong greenhouse gases |
| Result | About 99% of controlled substances phased out; ozone layer recovering. Often called the most successful environmental treaty. |

### Kyoto Protocol (asked 2016)

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Under | UN Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC, 1992) |
| Adopted | December 1997, Kyoto, Japan; in force February 2005 |
| Aim | Cut greenhouse gas emissions of industrialised countries |
| Target | Average cut of about 5% below 1990 levels in 2008–2012 for developed ("Annex I") countries |
| Gases | CO₂, CH₄, N₂O, HFCs, PFCs, SF₆ |
| Principle | "Common but differentiated responsibilities": rich countries act first; no binding targets for developing countries |
| Second period | Doha Amendment: 2013–2020 |

**Three flexible mechanisms**
1. **Emissions trading:** countries with spare allowance sell to those over their limit.
2. **Clean Development Mechanism (CDM):** a developed country pays for an emission-cutting project in a developing country and earns credits.
3. **Joint Implementation:** the same between two developed countries.

**Objectives of the CDM:** (1) help developing countries achieve sustainable development and receive clean technology and investment; (2) help developed countries meet their targets at lower cost; (3) reduce global emissions. One credit (CER) equals one tonne of CO₂.

**Why developed countries criticised Kyoto**
- It put no binding limits on large developing emitters such as China and India.
- It was said to harm their economies and jobs. The USA signed but never ratified. Canada withdrew in 2011. Japan and Russia stayed out of the second period.
- It covered a shrinking share of world emissions and had weak penalties.

**Paris Agreement (2015)** replaced Kyoto's approach. All countries set their own targets (Nationally Determined Contributions). Goal: keep warming well below 2 °C and try for 1.5 °C.

| Point | Montreal Protocol | Kyoto Protocol |
|---|---|---|
| Problem | Ozone depletion | Global warming |
| Year | 1987 | 1997 |
| Controls | CFCs, halons and other ozone-depleting substances | Six greenhouse gases |
| Who is bound | All countries | Developed countries only |
| Outcome | Success | Limited; replaced by Paris |

## §11 Water pollution (asked 2018, 2022, 2023, 2024, 2025)

**Definition.** Any change in the physical, chemical or biological quality of water that makes it harmful to living things or unfit for its use.

**How it happens (sources)**
- **Point sources:** a single outlet, such as a factory pipe or sewage drain.
- **Non-point sources:** spread out, such as run-off from farms, roads and cities.
- Causes: untreated sewage, industrial effluent, farm run-off (fertilizers, pesticides, manure), solid waste dumping, oil spills, mining, hot water from power plants, washing and bathing in rivers, polluted rain.

### Major water pollutants

| Pollutant | Sources | Effects |
|---|---|---|
| **Oxygen-demanding wastes** | Sewage, animal manure, food, paper and sugar mill waste | Bacteria use up dissolved oxygen while breaking them down; fish die; foul smell |
| **Plant nutrients** (nitrates, phosphates) | Fertilizers, detergents, sewage | **Eutrophication**: algal bloom → light blocked → algae die → decay uses oxygen → dead zones. Nitrate in drinking water harms infants. |
| **Synthetic organic chemicals** | Pesticides, detergents, plastics, solvents, medicines, oil products | Toxic and long-lasting; build up in food chains; cancer, hormone disruption; foam |
| **Thermal pollution** | Hot cooling water from power plants and factories | Warm water holds less oxygen; fish die of thermal shock; species change |
| **Infectious agents** | Human and animal waste | Cholera, typhoid, dysentery, hepatitis A and E, polio, worms |
| **Sediments** | Soil eroded from farms, cleared forests, building sites, mines | Muddy water blocks light, clogs fish gills, buries eggs, fills dams and canals |
| **Radioactivity** | Uranium mining, nuclear plants, hospitals, weapons tests | Cancer, genetic damage; builds up in food chains |
| **Heavy metals** | Tanneries (chromium), batteries and paint (lead), coal burning and gold mining (mercury), natural rock and mining (arsenic), fertilizers (cadmium) | Do not break down; build up in the body; damage brain, kidney, liver; cancer |
| **Acids** | Mine drainage, acid rain, industrial discharge | Low pH kills aquatic life, frees toxic metals, corrodes pipes |

**Oxygen-demanding wastes in detail (asked 2023)**
- **Dissolved oxygen (DO):** healthy water has over 5–6 mg/L. Fish suffer below about 4 mg/L.
- **Biochemical oxygen demand (BOD):** the oxygen that microbes need to break down the organic matter in a water sample in 5 days at 20 °C. Clean water: under 3 mg/L. Raw sewage: 200–600 mg/L. High BOD means heavy pollution.
- **Prevention:** treat sewage before discharge; septic tanks; treat factory effluent at source; constructed wetlands; aerate water; stop dumping of waste and manure in drains.
- **Sewage treatment stages:** primary (screens and settling tanks remove solids); secondary (bacteria digest organic matter: activated sludge or trickling filters); tertiary (remove nutrients; disinfect with chlorine or UV).

**Role of sewage bacteria after a river is polluted (asked 2025)**
1. Sewage brings food for bacteria. Aerobic bacteria multiply fast.
2. They break organic matter down to CO₂, water, nitrates and phosphates, using dissolved oxygen.
3. Oxygen falls downstream of the outfall (the "oxygen sag"). Fish and insects die or leave.
4. If oxygen runs out, anaerobic bacteria take over. They give off methane, ammonia and hydrogen sulphide. The water turns black and smells.
5. Further downstream the organic matter is used up. Oxygen re-enters from the air and from algae. Clean-water life returns.
This is the river's **self-purification**. It fails when the load is too large. Sewage also carries disease bacteria; coliform bacteria are counted as the sign of faecal pollution.

```
 Dissolved O2
   high |\                           ____ clean
        | \                      ___/
        |  \____             ___/   recovery
   low  |       \___________/   septic zone
        +-----|---------------------------------> distance downstream
          sewage outfall
```

**Heavy metals and living organisms (asked 2024)**

| Metal | Effect |
|---|---|
| Lead | Brain damage and low IQ in children, anaemia, kidney damage |
| Mercury | Nerve and brain damage (Minamata disease, Japan); passes up the fish food chain |
| Arsenic | Skin sores, cancers of skin, lung and bladder; a serious groundwater problem in parts of Punjab and Sindh |
| Cadmium | Kidney failure, brittle bones (itai-itai disease) |
| Chromium (VI) | Cancer, skin ulcers; from tanneries (Kasur, Sialkot) |

They do not break down; they accumulate in tissues (bioaccumulation) and concentrate up the food chain (biomagnification). They poison enzymes. Plants take them up from irrigation water, and they reach humans through food. (MCQ 2026: mercury is most harmful because it biomagnifies.)

**Effects of water pollution overall:** water-borne disease, death of aquatic life, loss of fisheries, unsafe food, costlier water treatment, loss of tourism and beauty.

**Control:** sewage and effluent treatment plants, enforcing discharge standards (National Environmental Quality Standards), less and better-timed fertilizer and pesticide, tree cover to stop erosion, cooling towers for hot water, safe disposal of solid and hospital waste, monitoring, public awareness.

## §12 Drinking water quality and standards (asked 2022, 2024)

**Safe drinking water** is free of disease germs and harmful chemicals and is clear, colourless and without bad taste or smell.

**Indicators (parameters) of water quality**

| Group | Parameters |
|---|---|
| Physical | Colour, taste, odour, temperature, turbidity, total dissolved solids (TDS) |
| Chemical | pH, hardness, dissolved oxygen, BOD, COD, chloride, fluoride, nitrate, arsenic, lead and other metals |
| Biological | Total coliforms, *E. coli* (MCQ 2026: the key microbiological indicator) |

**Standards (WHO guidelines; Pakistan's national standards of 2010 are close to these)**

| Parameter | Limit |
|---|---|
| Colour, taste, odour | Not objectionable |
| Turbidity | Under 5 NTU |
| pH | 6.5–8.5 |
| Total dissolved solids | Under 1,000 mg/L |
| Total hardness | Under 500 mg/L |
| *E. coli* / coliforms | **Zero** in a 100 mL sample |
| Arsenic | 0.01 mg/L (WHO); Pakistan allows up to 0.05 mg/L |
| Lead | 0.01 mg/L (WHO) |
| Fluoride | 1.5 mg/L |
| Nitrate | 50 mg/L |
| Chloride | 250 mg/L |
| Residual chlorine at the tap | 0.2–0.5 mg/L |

**Water treatment steps:** screening → coagulation and flocculation (alum makes particles clump) → sedimentation → sand filtration → disinfection (chlorine, ozone or UV) → storage and supply.
At home: boil for one minute, chlorine tablets, filters, reverse osmosis.

**Pakistan:** surveys by the Pakistan Council of Research in Water Resources keep finding that more than half of tested sources are unsafe, mainly from bacteria, then arsenic, nitrate and salts. Causes: sewage lines leaking into water pipes, untreated waste, old pipes.

## §13 Land pollution: solid waste management and disposal (asked 2016, 2022)

**Solid waste.** Unwanted or discarded solid material from homes, shops, industry, farms, hospitals and building sites.

| Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| Municipal | Food scraps, paper, plastic, glass, metal, cloth |
| Industrial | Ash, sludge, scrap, chemicals |
| Hazardous | Toxic, flammable, corrosive or reactive wastes; batteries, pesticides |
| Biomedical | Used syringes, dressings, body parts |
| E-waste | Old phones, computers, TVs |
| Construction | Rubble, wood |
| Agricultural | Crop residue, manure |

Biodegradable waste rots (food, paper). Non-biodegradable waste does not (plastic, glass, metal).

**Solid waste management** covers: generation → storage → collection → transport → processing and recovery → disposal.

**Waste hierarchy (best first):** Reduce → Reuse → Recycle → Recover (compost, energy) → Dispose.

**Disposal and treatment methods**

| Method | How | For | Against |
|---|---|---|---|
| Open dumping | Waste piled on open land | Cheap | Smell, flies, rats, fires, polluted water; the usual practice in Pakistan |
| **Sanitary landfill** | Waste spread in layers, compacted, covered with soil each day in a lined pit; leachate and gas are collected | Safe if well run; gas can be used for power | Needs land; risk of leaks; methane |
| Incineration | Burning at 850–1,100 °C | Cuts volume by about 90%; destroys germs; can make power | Costly; air pollution (dioxins); toxic ash |
| Composting | Aerobic rotting of organic waste into manure | Returns nutrients; cheap | Only for organic waste; needs sorting |
| Anaerobic digestion | Bacteria without air make biogas | Energy plus fertilizer | Needs sorted organic waste |
| Recycling | Paper, glass, metal, plastic made into new goods | Saves resources and energy | Needs separation at source |
| Pyrolysis, gasification | Heating with little oxygen | Fuel gas and oil | Costly, complex |

**Parts of a sanitary landfill:** clay and plastic liner at the base, leachate collection pipes, waste cells with daily soil cover, gas vents or wells, final cap of clay and soil, monitoring wells.

**Sanitary vs industrial landfill (asked 2016)**

| Point | Sanitary (municipal) landfill | Industrial landfill |
|---|---|---|
| Waste | Household and commercial rubbish | Non-hazardous waste from industry: ash, sludge, foundry sand, construction debris |
| Operator | City or its contractor | Often the industry itself, sometimes on site |
| Main concern | Rotting organics: leachate, methane, pests | Chemical content of the waste |
| Design | Standard liner, daily cover, gas system | Tailored to the waste |
Hazardous waste needs a separate "secure" landfill with double liners.

**Criteria for choosing a landfill site**
1. Far from homes, schools and hospitals (at least 500 m to 1 km) and downwind of the city.
2. Far from airports (birds endanger aircraft).
3. Not on a flood plain, wetland or near rivers, lakes or wells.
4. Deep water table.
5. Clay-rich soil that water passes through slowly.
6. Stable ground: away from faults, landslides and sinkholes.
7. Large enough for 15–20 years of waste.
8. Good road access and a reasonable haul distance.
9. Cover soil available nearby.
10. Not on prime farmland, forest, wildlife habitat or heritage sites.
11. Affordable land and public acceptance.

**Problems of solid waste management in Pakistan (asked 2022)**
- Very large and growing volume (tens of millions of tonnes a year).
- Only about half to two-thirds of city waste is collected.
- Open dumping and burning; very few engineered landfills.
- No sorting at source; hospital and hazardous waste mixed with household waste.
- Recycling left to informal scavengers working in unsafe conditions.
- Shortage of funds, vehicles, trained staff and data.
- Weak enforcement of laws; overlapping agencies.
- Plastic bags choke drains and cause urban flooding.
- Low public awareness; littering.

**Effects:** disease (flies, rats, mosquitoes), polluted groundwater from leachate, air pollution from burning, methane emissions, blocked drains, ugly surroundings.

**Solutions:** the 3Rs, separate bins for wet and dry waste, composting and biogas for organic waste, sanitary landfills, waste-to-energy plants, public–private waste companies, charges on producers, bans on thin plastic bags, safe hospital-waste incinerators, awareness.

## §14 Role of remote sensing and GIS in environmental science (asked 2016, 2022, 2024)

### Remote sensing

**Definition.** Collecting information about an object or area from a distance, without touching it, by recording the electromagnetic energy it reflects or emits. Sensors are carried on satellites, aircraft or drones.

**Basic principle.** Each kind of surface reflects, absorbs and emits energy in its own way at different wavelengths: its **spectral signature**. Healthy plants reflect strongly in near-infrared and absorb red light. Water absorbs infrared and looks dark. Comparing bands lets us identify and measure what is on the ground.

**Steps:** (1) energy source (the Sun or the sensor) → (2) passage through the atmosphere → (3) interaction with the target → (4) recording by the sensor → (5) transmission to a ground station → (6) processing and interpretation → (7) use.

| Type | Energy source | Examples | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Passive | Sunlight or the Earth's own heat | Cameras, multispectral scanners (Landsat, Sentinel-2) | Needs daylight and clear sky (except thermal) |
| Active | The sensor sends its own signal | Radar (SAR), LiDAR | Works at night and through cloud |

**Resolution and its types (asked 2016)**

| Type | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Spatial | Size of the smallest feature seen (ground size of one pixel) | 30 m for Landsat; under 1 m for commercial satellites |
| Spectral | Number and narrowness of wavelength bands | Multispectral (a few bands); hyperspectral (hundreds) |
| Temporal | How often the same place is imaged | Landsat every 16 days; weather satellites every few minutes |
| Radiometric | Smallest difference in energy the sensor can detect | 8-bit = 256 grey levels |

### GIS

**Definition.** A Geographic Information System is a computer system for capturing, storing, analysing and displaying data that are tied to locations on the Earth.

- Parts: hardware, software (ArcGIS, QGIS), data, people, methods.
- Data: **spatial** (vector: points, lines, polygons; raster: grid cells) and **attribute** (facts about each feature).
- Strength: stacking many map **layers** (rivers, soils, roads, population) and analysing them together.
- Remote sensing supplies the data; GPS supplies positions; GIS does the analysis.

### Roles in environmental science

| Field | Use |
|---|---|
| Land use and land cover | Mapping change, urban sprawl, loss of farmland |
| Forests | Deforestation, forest health, fire detection |
| Water | Mapping rivers, lakes, groundwater zones; water quality (algae, turbidity); irrigation |
| Glaciers and snow | Retreat of glaciers; glacial lakes and GLOF risk |
| Disasters | Flood mapping and forecasting, drought monitoring, cyclone tracking, earthquake and landslide damage, relief routes |
| Air | Smoke, dust, NO₂, aerosols; ozone hole |
| Agriculture | Crop area, health and yield forecasts; drought stress |
| Coasts and oceans | Sea-surface temperature, sea level, oil spills, mangroves, coastal erosion |
| Climate change | Ice cover, sea level, vegetation shifts |
| Wildlife | Habitat mapping, protected-area planning |
| Planning | Environmental impact studies, landfill and dam siting, pollution source mapping |

(MCQ 2026: remote sensing is used to collect large-scale Earth surface data.)

**Advantages:** covers large and remote areas quickly, repeated coverage shows change, cheaper than ground surveys, objective digital data.
**Limits:** cloud cover, cost of high-resolution images, need for skilled staff and ground checks.

**Pakistan:** SUPARCO (space agency, founded 1961) runs remote-sensing satellites (PRSS-1, launched 2018; an indigenous Earth-observation satellite in January 2025). Satellite maps were used in the 2010 and 2022 floods, in crop reporting and in glacier monitoring.

## §15 Population planning (asked 2022)

**Definition.** Planned efforts by a state and its people to manage the size, growth and distribution of population so that it stays in balance with resources. At family level it is family planning: choosing the number and spacing of children.

**Key terms**

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Birth rate / death rate | Births / deaths per 1,000 people per year |
| Growth rate | Natural increase (plus net migration), as a percentage |
| Total fertility rate (TFR) | Average children per woman; 2.1 keeps a population stable |
| Doubling time | About 70 ÷ growth rate (%) |
| Demographic transition | Shift from high births and deaths to low births and deaths as a country develops |
| Demographic dividend | Economic gain when most people are of working age |
| Carrying capacity | The largest population an area can support for long |

Malthus (1798) argued that population grows faster than food supply.

**World:** 8 billion reached in November 2022. India passed China as the most populous country in 2023.

**Pakistan:** 241.5 million in the 2023 census; fifth most populous country; growth about 2.55% a year (doubling in about 27 years); fertility about 3.6 children per woman; only about a third of couples use contraception; about two-thirds of people are under 30.

**Benefits of population planning**
1. Healthier mothers and babies: spacing births cuts maternal and infant deaths.
2. Better food, schooling and care for each child.
3. Women gain time for education and work.
4. Less poverty; higher savings and income per person.
5. Less pressure on food, water, housing, energy, jobs, schools and hospitals.
6. Protects the environment: less deforestation, pollution and overuse of water.
7. Lets the country gain a demographic dividend instead of a youth unemployment crisis.

**Methods and measures:** contraceptives (pills, condoms, injections, IUDs, implants, sterilisation); female education; raising and enforcing the minimum age of marriage; Lady Health Workers and clinics that reach villages; mother-and-child health care (parents have fewer children when more survive); awareness through media and religious scholars; jobs for women.

**Obstacles in Pakistan:** low female literacy, preference for sons, early marriage, religious and social misgivings, poor access to services in rural areas, weak and interrupted programmes, poverty (children seen as earners).

**Success stories to cite:** Bangladesh and Iran cut fertility fast through community health workers, female education and support from religious leaders.
