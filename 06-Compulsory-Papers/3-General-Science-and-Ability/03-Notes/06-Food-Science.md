# Food Science

Syllabus lines covered:
- Concept of Balance Diet: Vitamins, Carbohydrates, Protein, Fats and oil, Minerals, Fiber.
- Quality of Food: Bioavailability of Nutrients, Appearance, Texture, Flavor, Quality of Packed and Frozen Food, Food Additives, Preservatives and Antioxidants.
- Food Deterioration and its Control: Causes of Food Deterioration, Adulteration, Food Preservation.

Small section, asked almost every year (one or two 5-mark parts). Tables score well here.

## §1 Concept of a balanced diet (asked 2018, 2025)

**Definition.** A balanced diet contains all the nutrients (carbohydrates, proteins, fats, vitamins, minerals, fibre and water) in the right amounts and proportions for a person's age, sex, activity and state of health.

**Energy needs:** about 2,000 kcal a day for an adult woman and 2,500 kcal for an adult man. More for manual workers, teenagers, and pregnant or nursing women.

| Nutrient | Share of daily energy | Energy value |
|---|---|---|
| Carbohydrates | 45–65% | 4 kcal/g |
| Fats | 20–35% | 9 kcal/g |
| Proteins | 10–35% | 4 kcal/g |

**Food groups by nutrient (asked 2025)**

| Group | Main nutrient | Function | Sources |
|---|---|---|---|
| Energy-giving foods | Carbohydrates | Main fuel for body and brain | Wheat, rice, maize, potatoes, sugar |
| Energy-giving foods | Fats and oils | Concentrated energy; carry vitamins A, D, E, K; insulation | Ghee, butter, cooking oil, nuts |
| Body-building foods | Proteins | Growth and repair; enzymes, hormones, antibodies | Meat, fish, eggs, milk, pulses, beans |
| Protective foods | Vitamins | Regulate body processes; prevent deficiency diseases | Fruit, vegetables, milk, liver |
| Protective foods | Minerals | Bones, blood, nerves, fluid balance | Milk, green vegetables, meat, salt |
| Regulating foods | Fibre | Keeps the bowel moving | Whole grains, vegetables, fruit, pulses |
| Regulating foods | Water | Transport, temperature control, medium for reactions | Drinks and foods |

**Features of a good diet:** variety from all groups; at least five portions (400 g) of fruit and vegetables a day; whole grains; less salt (under 5 g a day), sugar and saturated fat; no trans fat; plenty of water.

**Diagram:** a food pyramid (cereals at the wide base, then fruit and vegetables, then milk, meat and pulses, with fats and sugar at the small tip) or a plate divided into four with a glass of milk.

## §2 Macronutrients and fibre

**Carbohydrates.** Sugars (quick energy) and starches (slow release). Role: main energy source; spare protein; excess stored as glycogen and fat. See Biological Sciences §4.

**Proteins.** Made of amino acids, nine of them essential. Animal proteins are "complete". Plant proteins can be combined (dal with roti or rice) to give all the essential amino acids. Lack causes kwashiorkor and poor growth. See Biological Sciences §2.

**Fats and oils**

| Type | Nature | Sources | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Saturated | Solid at room temperature | Ghee, butter, fatty meat, palm oil | Raise LDL cholesterol if eaten in excess |
| Unsaturated | Liquid | Olive, canola, sunflower oil, fish, nuts | Protect the heart |
| Trans fat | Made by partial hydrogenation | Banaspati, bakery items, fried snacks | Most harmful |

Functions: energy store, absorption of fat-soluble vitamins, cell membranes, hormones, protection of organs, flavour. Essential fatty acids (omega-3 and omega-6) must come from food.

**Fibre (roughage).** The part of plant food that human enzymes cannot digest (cellulose, pectin).

| Type | Sources | Benefit |
|---|---|---|
| Soluble | Oats, beans, apples, citrus | Lowers blood cholesterol; steadies blood sugar |
| Insoluble | Wheat bran, whole grains, vegetables | Adds bulk; prevents constipation |

Fibre also makes one feel full (helps weight control) and lowers the risk of bowel cancer. Need: about 25–30 g a day.

**Water.** About 60–70% of body weight. Carries nutrients and wastes, controls temperature by sweat, cushions joints. Need: about 2–3 litres a day.

## §3 Vitamins and minerals (asked 2017, 2019, 2024)

**Vitamins** are organic compounds needed in very small amounts for normal growth and body processes. The body cannot make most of them.

| Point | Fat-soluble | Water-soluble |
|---|---|---|
| Vitamins | A, D, E, K | B group, C |
| Storage | Stored in liver and body fat | Not stored; excess passed in urine |
| Needed | Not every day | Every day |
| Excess | Can be toxic | Rarely toxic |
| Lost in cooking | Little | Easily (heat, water) |

**Vitamin table**

| Vitamin | Sources | Function | Deficiency |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** (retinol) | Carrots, liver, milk, eggs, green leaves, mango | Vision in dim light, healthy skin, immunity | **Night blindness**, dry eyes |
| **D** (calciferol) | Sunlight on skin, fish oil, eggs, fortified milk | Absorption of calcium; strong bones | **Rickets** in children; soft bones (osteomalacia) in adults |
| **E** (tocopherol) | Vegetable oils, nuts, seeds | Antioxidant; protects cell membranes | Nerve and muscle weakness (rare) |
| **K** | Green leafy vegetables; made by gut bacteria | Blood clotting | Slow clotting, bleeding |
| **B1** (thiamine) | Whole grains, pulses, yeast | Energy release; nerves | **Beriberi** |
| B2 (riboflavin) | Milk, eggs, liver | Energy release | Cracked lips, sore tongue |
| B3 (niacin) | Meat, fish, groundnuts | Energy release | Pellagra (skin rash, diarrhoea, confusion) |
| B9 (folic acid) | Green leaves, liver, pulses | Making DNA and red cells | Anaemia; birth defects of the spine |
| **B12** | Meat, fish, eggs, milk (animal foods only) | Red cells, nerves | **Pernicious anaemia** |
| **C** (ascorbic acid) | Citrus fruit, guava, tomato, green chilli | Collagen, wound healing, iron absorption, antioxidant | **Scurvy** (bleeding gums) |

Matching list from the 2019 paper: poor night vision → A; bleeding gums → C; rickets → D; beriberi → B1; anaemia → B12 or folic acid (iron is the mineral cause).

**Minerals** are inorganic elements needed for body structure and regulation.

| Mineral | Sources | Function | Deficiency |
|---|---|---|---|
| Calcium | Milk, yoghurt, cheese, green vegetables | Bones, teeth, clotting, muscle action | Rickets, brittle bones (osteoporosis) |
| Phosphorus | Milk, meat, grains | Bones, ATP, DNA | Weak bones |
| Iron | Red meat, liver, spinach, pulses | Haemoglobin | **Anaemia** |
| Iodine | Iodised salt, sea fish | Thyroid hormone | **Goitre**; mental slowness in children |
| Sodium | Salt | Fluid balance, nerve signals | Cramps (excess raises blood pressure) |
| Potassium | Bananas, potatoes, pulses | Nerves, muscles, heartbeat | Weakness, irregular heartbeat |
| Zinc | Meat, nuts, whole grains | Enzymes, immunity, growth, healing | Poor growth, slow healing |
| Fluoride | Water, toothpaste | Hard tooth enamel | Tooth decay |
| Magnesium | Nuts, green leaves | Enzymes, muscles, nerves | Cramps |

### Malnutrition (asked 2021)

**Definition.** Poor health caused by too little, too much or the wrong balance of energy and nutrients.

| Form | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Wasting | Too thin for height (recent hunger or illness) |
| Stunting | Too short for age (long-term undernutrition) |
| Underweight | Low weight for age |
| Micronutrient deficiency ("hidden hunger") | Lack of iron, iodine, vitamin A, zinc |
| Overnutrition | Overweight and obesity |

Severe protein–energy malnutrition: **kwashiorkor** (lack of protein: swollen belly and legs, thin hair) and **marasmus** (lack of energy: extreme thinness).

**Causes:** poverty and high food prices; diets with little variety; lack of breastfeeding and poor weaning foods; repeated infections (diarrhoea, worms); unsafe water and sanitation; low education of mothers; large families and short birth gaps; floods and droughts; junk food and inactivity (for obesity).

**Consequences:** poor growth; weak immunity and more infections; child deaths (undernutrition lies behind nearly half of deaths under five); poor brain development and school results; anaemia and risky pregnancies; lower work output and national income; obesity leads to diabetes, heart disease and high blood pressure.

**Pakistan (National Nutrition Survey 2018):** about 40% of children under five are stunted and about 18% are wasted; about 42% of women of child-bearing age are anaemic.

**Remedies:** breastfeeding only for six months, then good weaning foods; fortified foods (iodised salt, flour with iron and folic acid, oil with vitamins A and D); supplements for mothers and children; nutrition education; cash support for poor mothers; school meals; clean water and toilets.

## §4 Bioavailability of nutrients

**Definition.** The fraction of a nutrient in food that is actually absorbed and used by the body. (MCQ 2026.) The amount on the label is not the amount the body gets.

**Factors**

| Factor | Examples |
|---|---|
| Chemical form | Haem iron (meat) is absorbed far better than non-haem iron (plants) |
| Enhancers | Vitamin C raises absorption of plant iron. Fat is needed to absorb vitamins A, D, E, K. Vitamin D raises calcium absorption. |
| Inhibitors | Phytates (whole grains, pulses) bind iron, zinc, calcium. Oxalates (spinach) bind calcium. Tannins (tea) cut iron uptake. |
| Processing and cooking | Cooking tomatoes frees more lycopene. Soaking, sprouting and fermenting cut phytates. Over-boiling destroys vitamin C and folate. |
| The person | Age, gut health, pregnancy, existing stores (a body short of iron absorbs more), medicines |

**Use:** it guides advice (lemon with dal or spinach; no tea with meals), food fortification, and the design of supplements.

## §5 Appearance, texture and flavour

**Food quality** is the sum of the features that make food acceptable: sensory quality, nutritional value, safety and keeping quality.

Sensory (organoleptic) quality has three parts:

| Attribute | What it covers | What changes it |
|---|---|---|
| **Appearance** | Colour, size, shape, gloss, freshness, absence of defects | Natural pigments: chlorophyll (green), carotenoids (yellow-orange), anthocyanins (red-purple), myoglobin (red meat). Browning: by enzymes (cut apple) or by heat (toast; the Maillard reaction between sugars and amino acids). |
| **Texture** | How food feels in the hand and mouth: crisp, tender, juicy, chewy, creamy | Moisture gained or lost; ripening softens fruit; heat sets proteins and swells starch; staling of bread |
| **Flavour** | Taste (sweet, sour, salty, bitter, umami) + aroma (smell) + mouthfeel | Ripeness, cooking, spices, fermentation; off-flavours from rancid fat or microbes |

- The buyer judges appearance first, so it has the most effect on choice.
- Sensory quality is measured by trained tasting panels and scoring scales, and by instruments (colour meters, texture analysers).
- Safety is a "hidden" quality: food that looks and tastes fine may still carry germs or toxins.

**Quality control systems:** HACCP (Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points), ISO 22000, Codex Alimentarius (FAO/WHO food standards). In Pakistan: PSQCA and the provincial food authorities.

## §6 Quality of packed and frozen food

### Packed food

**Functions of packaging:** contain the food; protect it from germs, moisture, air, light and knocks; lengthen shelf life; inform the buyer (label); make transport and use easy.

| Material / method | Use |
|---|---|
| Glass | Jams, pickles, drinks; inert, reusable |
| Metal cans | Canned fruit, fish, drinks; heat-sterilised and airtight |
| Plastics (PET, HDPE, PP) | Bottles, pouches; light and cheap |
| Laminated cartons (paper + plastic + aluminium foil) | UHT milk and juice; aseptic packing keeps them for months without a fridge |
| Vacuum packing | Air removed; meat, cheese |
| Modified-atmosphere packing | Air replaced by nitrogen or CO₂; crisps, salads |

**What to check on packed food:** intact seal; no dents, rust, leaks or **bulging** in cans (gas from bacteria; danger of botulism); manufacture and expiry dates; list of ingredients and additives; nutrition facts; storage instructions; quality mark.

**Concerns:** high salt, sugar and fat in processed foods; additives; some loss of vitamins; chemicals moving from plastic into food; packaging waste.

### Frozen food

- Stored at **−18 °C or below**.
- Freezing does not kill most microbes. It stops their growth and slows enzymes because water is locked up as ice.
- **Quick freezing** (blast freezing at −30 to −40 °C) makes small ice crystals that do little damage. **Slow freezing** makes large crystals that tear cells, so the food leaks juice (drip) and goes soft when thawed.
- Vegetables are **blanched** (dipped briefly in boiling water) first to stop enzymes.

| Quality problem | Cause |
|---|---|
| Freezer burn (dry, pale patches) | Poor wrapping; surface dries out |
| Drip loss, soft texture | Slow freezing; repeated thawing |
| Rancid taste | Slow oxidation of fat in long storage |
| Large ice crystals in the pack | Temperature going up and down (broken cold chain) |

**Rules:** keep the cold chain unbroken from factory to home; thaw in the refrigerator; cook soon after thawing; do not refreeze thawed food.
**Nutrition:** frozen vegetables keep their vitamins well, often better than "fresh" ones stored for days.

## §7 Food additives, preservatives and antioxidants (asked 2016, 2023, 2024)

**Food additive.** A substance added on purpose, in small amounts, to improve a food's keeping quality, appearance, flavour, texture or nutritional value.

| Class | Job | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Preservatives | Stop microbes | Sodium benzoate, sulphites, nitrites |
| Antioxidants | Stop oxidation | Vitamin C, vitamin E, BHA, BHT |
| Colours | Restore or add colour | Carotene, caramel, tartrazine |
| Flavours and enhancers | Add or boost flavour | Vanillin, monosodium glutamate (MSG) |
| Sweeteners | Sweeten without sugar | Saccharin, aspartame, sucralose, stevia |
| Emulsifiers, stabilisers, thickeners | Keep oil and water mixed; give body | Lecithin, pectin, gelatin, guar gum |
| Acidity regulators | Control pH | Citric acid |
| Anti-caking agents | Keep powders free-flowing | Silicon dioxide |
| Raising agents | Make dough rise | Baking soda |
| Nutrients | Fortify | Iodine in salt; vitamins A and D in oil |

Safety: each permitted additive has an acceptable daily intake. Some cause problems in sensitive people (sulphites and asthma; some dyes and hyperactivity; nitrites can form cancer-causing nitrosamines).

### Preservatives

**Definition.** Substances that prevent or slow the spoilage of food by bacteria, yeasts and moulds.

| Class | Examples | Used in |
|---|---|---|
| Natural (traditional) | Salt, sugar, vinegar, oil, spices, honey, wood smoke | Pickles, jams, salted fish and meat |
| Chemical | Sodium benzoate | Soft drinks, juices, ketchup, pickles |
| | Potassium sorbate, sorbic acid | Cheese, baked goods |
| | Sulphur dioxide, sulphites, potassium metabisulphite | Dried fruit, squashes, juices |
| | Nitrites and nitrates | Cured meats (stop botulism; keep pink colour) |
| | Calcium propionate | Bread (stops mould) |

**Importance:** longer shelf life; prevent food poisoning; cut waste; make food available out of season and far from where it is grown; keep prices steady.

### Antioxidants

**Definition.** Substances that delay or prevent the oxidation of food, especially fats and oils. Oxidation causes rancid smell and taste, browning and loss of vitamins. Antioxidants work by mopping up free radicals or oxygen.

| Type | Examples | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Natural | Vitamin C (ascorbic acid), vitamin E (tocopherols), carotenoids (beta-carotene, lycopene), polyphenols (green tea, rosemary), selenium | From fruit, vegetables, nuts, tea, spices such as turmeric. Safer; preferred by consumers. |
| Synthetic | BHA (butylated hydroxyanisole), BHT (butylated hydroxytoluene), TBHQ, propyl gallate | Cheap, stable, effective in tiny amounts; used in oils, ghee, crisps, cereals; legal limits apply |

In the body, dietary antioxidants protect cells from free-radical damage linked with ageing, cancer and heart disease.

**Preservatives vs antioxidants (asked 2024)**

| Point | Preservative | Antioxidant |
|---|---|---|
| Acts against | Microbes (bacteria, yeasts, moulds) | Oxidation by air |
| Prevents | Rotting, souring, mould, food poisoning | Rancidity, browning, vitamin loss |
| Examples | Sodium benzoate, salt, sulphites | Vitamin C, vitamin E, BHA, BHT |
| Typical foods | Juices, pickles, bread, cured meat | Oils, ghee, fried snacks, cereals |
| Overlap | Sulphites do both | Vitamin C also slows browning |

## §8 Causes of food deterioration (asked 2021, 2026)

**Food deterioration (spoilage).** Any unwanted change in the colour, smell, taste, texture, nutritional value or safety of food that makes it less fit to eat.

| Cause | How | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Biological: microbes** | Bacteria, moulds and yeasts feed on food and release acids, gases and toxins | Milk sours (lactic acid bacteria); meat putrefies; bread goes mouldy (*Rhizopus*); fruit juice ferments (yeast); *Salmonella* and *Clostridium botulinum* cause food poisoning; *Aspergillus* makes aflatoxin on maize, groundnuts and chillies |
| **Biological: enzymes** | The food's own enzymes keep working after harvest or slaughter | Bananas over-ripen; cut apples and potatoes turn brown; fish softens |
| **Biological: pests** | Insects, rodents and birds eat and foul food | Weevils in wheat and rice; rats in stores |
| **Chemical** | Reactions in the food or with air and containers | Fats react with oxygen and turn **rancid** (old ghee, oil, nuts); vitamins are destroyed by oxidation; milk powder browns in storage; acid food corrodes cans |
| **Physical** | Temperature, moisture, light, pressure | Heat speeds every reaction; biscuits go soft in damp air; bread dries and stales; vegetables wilt; light fades colour and destroys riboflavin in milk; bruising in transport opens the way for microbes; freezer burn |

**What microbes need:** food, moisture, warmth (the danger zone is about 5–60 °C), time, suitable pH and, for many, oxygen. Remove one and growth slows. This is the basis of preservation.

| Class of food | Keeps for | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Perishable | 1–2 days | Milk, meat, fish, leafy vegetables |
| Semi-perishable | Weeks | Potatoes, onions, apples, eggs |
| Non-perishable | Months to years | Grain, pulses, sugar, flour |

**How fungus spoils a slice of bread (asked 2021)**
1. Mould spores (for example the black bread mould *Rhizopus*) are always in the air and settle on the bread.
2. Bread is moist, starchy and at room temperature, so the spores germinate.
3. Fine threads (hyphae) spread and form a cottony network (mycelium). Root-like hyphae grow into the bread.
4. The fungus releases enzymes that digest starch and protein outside its body. It absorbs the sugars and amino acids. This is saprophytic feeding.
5. Upright stalks carry black or green spore cases, which release new spores.
6. The bread softens, changes colour, smells musty and may contain toxins. It must not be eaten.
Warmth, damp and darkness speed this up. A refrigerator, dry storage and the preservative calcium propionate slow it.

## §9 Adulteration and contamination (asked 2021, 2025)

**Adulteration.** Deliberately lowering the quality of food by adding cheaper or harmful substances, or by removing something valuable, to increase profit.

| Food | Common adulterants |
|---|---|
| Milk | Water, urea, detergent, starch, formalin |
| Ghee and butter | Banaspati, animal fat |
| Red chilli powder | Brick powder, artificial dye |
| Turmeric | Lead chromate, yellow dye |
| Tea | Used leaves, dyed husk |
| Honey | Sugar syrup |
| Black pepper | Papaya seeds |
| Spices and flour | Sawdust, husk, chalk powder |
| Fruit | Ripened with calcium carbide; injected colour |
| Meat | Injected water; meat of banned or dead animals |
| Sweets and drinks | Non-food colours |

**Effects:** stomach upsets and diarrhoea, liver and kidney damage, lead poisoning, cancer in the long run, malnutrition (the buyer gets less nutrition), deaths in severe cases; cheating of consumers.

**Control:** food laws and standards (Pure Food Ordinance 1960; PSQCA; Punjab Food Authority 2011 and the other provincial food authorities); inspection, testing laboratories, fines, sealing of premises; consumer awareness; buying sealed and certified products.

**Food contaminants vs food adulterants (asked 2021 and 2025)**

| Point | Contaminant | Adulterant |
|---|---|---|
| Entry | Accidental, unintended | Deliberate |
| Motive | None; due to carelessness or the environment | Profit |
| When | Any stage: growing, processing, storage, transport, cooking | Usually at processing or sale |
| Kinds | Biological (bacteria, moulds, parasites), chemical (pesticide residues, heavy metals, cleaning agents), physical (hair, stones, glass) | Cheaper bulking agents, dyes, chemicals |
| Examples | *Salmonella* in eggs, aflatoxin in maize, arsenic in rice, a hair in food | Water in milk, brick powder in chilli powder |
| Control | Hygiene, good practice, HACCP, residue limits | Law enforcement and punishment |
| Legal view | Limits are set (maximum residue levels) | Prohibited |

## §10 Food preservation (asked 2020)

**Definition.** Treating and handling food so as to stop or slow spoilage and keep it safe, nutritious and acceptable for longer.

**Principles**
1. Keep microbes out, remove them, stop their growth, or kill them.
2. Stop the food's own enzymes and chemical reactions.
3. Protect from insects, rodents and physical damage.

**Methods**

| Method | How it works | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Pasteurisation** | Mild heat kills disease germs: 72 °C for 15 seconds, or 63 °C for 30 minutes | Milk, juices (still need a fridge) |
| **UHT treatment** | 135–140 °C for a few seconds, then sterile packing | Long-life milk |
| **Canning and sterilisation** | Food sealed in a can and heated to about 121 °C | Canned fruit, fish, beans |
| **Refrigeration** | 0–4 °C slows microbes and enzymes | Fresh food, for days |
| **Freezing** | −18 °C stops microbial growth | Meat, peas, fish, for months |
| **Drying (dehydration)** | Removes the water microbes need | Sun-dried dates, apricots, chillies; milk powder; freeze-dried coffee |
| **Salting and curing** | Salt draws water out of food and microbes by osmosis | Salted fish and meat |
| **Sugaring** | High sugar does the same | Jam, jelly, murabba |
| **Pickling** | Acid (vinegar), salt and oil stop microbes | Achar |
| **Smoking** | Dries the surface and adds preserving chemicals | Fish, meat |
| **Chemical preservatives** | Chemicals stop microbes | Sodium benzoate in squashes |
| **Fermentation** | Helpful microbes make acid or alcohol that blocks spoilers | Yoghurt, cheese, vinegar |
| **Irradiation** | Gamma rays or electron beams kill microbes and insects and stop sprouting; the food does not become radioactive | Spices, potatoes, onions |
| **Vacuum and modified-atmosphere packing** | Removes or replaces oxygen | Meat, crisps |
| **Newer methods** | High-pressure processing; combining several mild methods ("hurdle" approach) | Juices, ready meals |

**Benefits:** less waste, food all year round, food security, lower prices, easier trade and transport.
**Drawbacks:** some loss of vitamins and flavour, added salt, sugar or chemicals, cost and energy.

Pasteurisation is named after Louis Pasteur. Canning was invented by Nicolas Appert in the early 1800s.
