## Physical sciences

### 1. Describe the most accepted theory of the origin of the universe. Give the evidence for it. (Asked 2021; related 2018)

**Big Bang theory.** The universe began about 13.8 billion years ago from an extremely hot, dense state and has been expanding and cooling ever since. Space itself expanded; it was not an explosion into empty space.

**Stages**
1. First fraction of a second: rapid expansion (inflation); basic particles form.
2. First three minutes: hydrogen and helium nuclei form.
3. About 380,000 years: atoms form; light travels freely.
4. A few hundred million years: first stars and galaxies.

[Diagram: a cone widening to the right; labels along it "Big Bang → nuclei → atoms (CMB released) → first stars → galaxies → today (13.8 billion years)".]

**Evidence**
1. **Redshift.** Hubble (1929) showed galaxies are moving apart; the farther, the faster.
2. **Cosmic microwave background.** Faint radiation at 2.7 K from all directions, found in 1965. It is the left-over glow.
3. **Light elements.** About 75% hydrogen and 25% helium, as predicted.

Today the universe is about 68% dark energy, 27% dark matter and 5% ordinary matter.

### 2. Describe the structure of the solar system, including terrestrial planets, gas giants and dwarf planets. (Asked 2026)

**Definition.** The solar system is the Sun and all bodies held by its gravity. It is about 4.6 billion years old. The Sun has 99.8% of its mass.

[Diagram:]
```
SUN | Mercury Venus Earth Mars | asteroid belt | Jupiter Saturn | Uranus Neptune | Kuiper belt
       terrestrial planets        (Ceres)        gas giants       ice giants      (Pluto, Eris)
```

**Terrestrial (inner) planets:** Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars. Small, dense, rocky, solid surface, few or no moons, no rings.

**Asteroid belt:** rocky bodies between Mars and Jupiter.

**Gas giants:** Jupiter (largest) and Saturn (rings). Mostly hydrogen and helium, no solid surface, many moons. **Ice giants:** Uranus and Neptune; ices of water, ammonia and methane.

**Dwarf planets:** Ceres, Pluto, Haumea, Makemake, Eris. They orbit the Sun and are round, but have not cleared their orbits. This is why Pluto was reclassified in 2006.

**Outer regions:** Kuiper belt and Oort cloud, the sources of comets.

All planets move in ellipses round the Sun (Kepler's first law), in nearly the same plane and direction.

### 3. Differentiate between solar and lunar eclipses. (Asked 2017, 2018)

**Eclipse.** One heavenly body passes into the shadow of another. The shadow has a dark centre (umbra) and a lighter fringe (penumbra).

[Diagram: two rows.]
```
Solar:  SUN ---->  Moon  --shadow-->  EARTH     (new moon)
Lunar:  SUN ---->  EARTH --shadow-->  Moon      (full moon)
```

| Point | Solar eclipse | Lunar eclipse |
|---|---|---|
| Position | Moon between Sun and Earth | Earth between Sun and Moon |
| Phase | New moon | Full moon |
| Hidden body | Sun | Moon |
| Types | Total, partial, annular | Total, partial, penumbral |
| Seen from | A narrow strip of the Earth | The whole night side |
| Length | A few minutes | Up to about 1¾ hours |
| Viewing | Dangerous without filters | Safe |

**Why not every month?** The Moon's orbit is tilted about 5° to the Earth's, so the three bodies line up only a few times a year.

In a total lunar eclipse the Moon looks red because the Earth's atmosphere bends red light into the shadow.

### 4. Explain the effects of the Earth's rotation and revolution. (Asked 2017, 2025)

**Rotation** is the spin of the Earth on its axis, west to east, once in about 24 hours.
**Revolution** is its journey round the Sun in about 365¼ days. The axis is tilted 23.5°.

**Effects of rotation**
1. Day and night.
2. Apparent east-to-west movement of the Sun and stars.
3. Time differences: 15° of longitude = 1 hour.
4. Deflection of winds and ocean currents (Coriolis effect): right in the northern hemisphere, left in the southern.
5. Bulge at the equator; two tides a day.

**Effects of revolution (with tilt)**
1. Seasons.
2. Unequal days and nights through the year.
3. Solstices (21 June, 22 December) and equinoxes (21 March, 23 September).
4. Changing height of the noon Sun.
5. The year; leap year every fourth year.

[Diagram: Sun in the centre; Earth at four positions on its orbit, axis tilted the same way each time; label 21 June "summer in north", 22 December "winter in north", and the two equinoxes.]

### 5. What is an earthquake? Describe the types of seismic waves and the scales used to measure earthquakes. (Asked 2016, 2020, 2024)

**Definition.** An earthquake is a sudden shaking of the ground caused by the release of energy stored in rocks. Most occur when stressed rock slips along a fault at plate boundaries. The point of rupture is the **focus**; the point on the surface above it is the **epicentre**.

[Diagram: block of crust with a fault; star "focus" at depth; "epicentre" above; arcs spreading out "seismic waves".]

**Seismic waves**

| Wave | Nature | Feature |
|---|---|---|
| P (primary) | Longitudinal body wave | Fastest; passes through solids and liquids |
| S (secondary) | Transverse body wave | Slower; solids only |
| Love | Surface wave | Side-to-side shaking |
| Rayleigh | Surface wave | Rolling motion; most damage |

**Measurement**
- **Richter scale:** magnitude (energy). Each step is 10 times the wave amplitude and about 32 times the energy.
- **Moment magnitude:** modern scale for large earthquakes.
- **Mercalli scale:** intensity (observed damage), I to XII.
- Instrument: seismograph.

Example: Kashmir, 8 October 2005, magnitude 7.6; over 73,000 deaths.

### 6. What is a tsunami? How is it generated and how does it differ from a tidal wave? (Asked 2016, 2018)

**Definition.** A tsunami is a series of very long sea waves caused by the sudden displacement of a large body of water.

**Generation**
1. An undersea earthquake at a subduction zone lifts or drops the sea floor (the main cause).
2. Undersea landslides.
3. Volcanic eruptions.
4. Meteorite impact (rare).

[Diagram: sea-floor fault with one side pushed up; low, long waves in deep water; a tall steep wave at the coast.]

**Characteristics**
- Deep sea: wavelength 100–200 km, speed 700–800 km/h, height under 1 m.
- Near shore: slows, shortens and rises to 10–30 m.
- The sea often withdraws first. Several waves follow.

| Point | Tsunami | Tide |
|---|---|---|
| Cause | Earthquake, landslide, eruption | Pull of the Moon and Sun |
| Timing | Sudden, irregular | Regular, twice a day |
| Effect | Destructive | Normal rise and fall |

**Examples:** Indian Ocean, 26 December 2004 (about 230,000 deaths); Makran coast, 1945.
**Safety:** warning buoys and sirens, evacuation routes, mangrove belts.

### 7. What are hurricanes? How are they formed and what damage do they cause? (Asked 2019, 2025)

**Definition.** A hurricane is a tropical cyclone: an intense, rotating low-pressure storm over a warm ocean with winds of 119 km/h or more. It is called a typhoon in the north-west Pacific and a cyclone in the Indian Ocean.

**Conditions:** sea surface at 26.5 °C or more; at least 5° from the equator; moist air; little wind shear.

**Formation**
1. Warm, moist air rises from the sea; pressure falls.
2. Vapour condenses and releases latent heat, which drives more rising.
3. Air rushes in and spirals because of the Coriolis effect.
4. The storm grows: depression → tropical storm → hurricane. It dies over land or cold water.

[Diagram: side view: clear central "eye"; towering "eyewall" on each side; spiral "rain bands"; arrows up in the eyewall and down in the eye.]

**Structure:** calm eye; **eyewall** (strongest winds, the most dangerous part); rain bands.

**Damage:** storm surge and coastal flooding (the main killer), violent winds, heavy rain and landslides, loss of crops, power and homes, disease afterwards.

Example: Bhola cyclone, 1970 (over 300,000 deaths).

### 8. Differentiate between a mid-latitude cyclone and a tornado. (Asked 2020, 2024)

**Mid-latitude cyclone.** A large low-pressure system that forms between 30° and 60° latitude where cold polar air meets warm tropical air. It has warm and cold fronts.

**Tornado.** A narrow, violently rotating column of air reaching from a thunderstorm cloud to the ground.

| Point | Mid-latitude cyclone | Tornado |
|---|---|---|
| Size | 1,000–2,500 km across | Under 2 km |
| Duration | 3–10 days | Minutes to an hour |
| Origin | Meeting of air masses along the polar front | Wind shear inside a severe thunderstorm |
| Energy | Temperature contrast | Instability and rotating updraft |
| Fronts | Present | Absent |
| Wind speed | 30–120 km/h | Up to about 480 km/h |
| Weather | Widespread cloud, rain, snow | Funnel cloud; extreme local damage |
| Movement | West to east with the westerlies | Short, irregular path |
| Scale | — | Enhanced Fujita (EF0–EF5) |
| Warning | Days | Minutes |

[Diagram: left, a map-view "L" with a warm front (semicircles) and cold front (triangles); right, a funnel hanging from a cloud to the ground.]

Western disturbances that bring Pakistan's winter rain are mid-latitude systems. Tornadoes are most common in the USA.

### 9. Discuss the causes of floods in Pakistan and the measures to manage them. (Predicted; floods are in the syllabus but not yet asked)

**Definition.** A flood is an overflow of water onto normally dry land. Types: river, flash, urban, coastal and glacial-lake outburst floods.

**Causes**
1. Heavy monsoon rain and cloudbursts (July–September).
2. Rapid melting of snow and glaciers.
3. Deforestation in catchments; silted rivers.
4. Settlements and farming on flood plains.
5. Poor drainage in cities; breaches of embankments.
6. Climate change: warmer air holds more moisture.

**Effects:** 2010: about 20 million people affected. 2022: 33 million affected, over 1,700 deaths, about US$30 billion in damage and loss; crops, roads and houses destroyed; water-borne disease.

**Management**

| Structural | Non-structural |
|---|---|
| Dams, reservoirs, barrages | Forecasting and early warning |
| Embankments and spurs | Flood-plain zoning; no building in river beds |
| Diversion channels, drainage | Watershed tree planting |
| Dredging | Evacuation plans, drills, insurance |

[Diagram: river cross-section with channel, embankments, flood plain, normal level and flood level.]

Institutions: NDMA, provincial PDMAs, Federal Flood Commission, Flood Forecasting Division.

### 10. Explain the key phases of disaster risk management. (Asked 2026)

**Definition.** Disaster risk management is the planned use of policies, organisation and skills to reduce disaster risk and to deal with disasters when they occur. Risk = hazard × exposure × vulnerability ÷ capacity.

[Diagram: a circle of four arrows: Mitigation → Preparedness → (disaster) → Response → Recovery → back to Mitigation.]

| Phase | When | What is done |
|---|---|---|
| **Mitigation** | Before | Remove or reduce risk: building codes, embankments, land-use zoning, tree planting |
| **Preparedness** | Before | Get ready: early warning, evacuation plans, drills, stockpiles, training |
| **Response** | During and just after | Save lives: search and rescue, first aid, shelter, food, water |
| **Recovery** | After | Restore and improve: rebuild homes, roads and livelihoods; "build back better" |

The phases form a cycle. Lessons from recovery feed into mitigation.

**Framework:** Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction, 2015–2030.
**Pakistan:** National Disaster Management Act 2010; NDMA at the centre, PDMAs in provinces, DDMAs in districts. The approach has moved from relief after the event to reducing risk before it.

### 11. Differentiate between renewable and non-renewable sources of energy, with examples. (Asked 2019, 2020, 2022)

**Renewable sources** are replaced by nature as fast as they are used. **Non-renewable sources** took millions of years to form and will run out.

| Point | Renewable | Non-renewable |
|---|---|---|
| Supply | Unlimited in practice | Finite |
| Examples | Solar, wind, hydro, geothermal, biomass, tidal | Coal, oil, natural gas, uranium |
| Pollution | Little or none in use | CO₂, SO₂, NOx, ash; nuclear waste |
| Cost | High to install, low to run | Lower to install; fuel cost continues |
| Reliability | Many depend on weather and site | Available on demand |
| Climate effect | Small | Main cause of global warming |

**Examples in brief**
- **Solar:** photovoltaic cells turn sunlight directly into electricity.
- **Wind:** moving air turns turbine blades that drive a generator.
- **Hydro:** falling water turns turbines (Tarbela, Mangla).
- **Biofuels:** biogas, ethanol and biodiesel from plant and animal matter.
- **Fossil fuels:** burned in thermal power plants and vehicles.

Pakistan has large untapped potential: the Gharo–Jhimpir wind corridor, strong sunshine, and the Indus hydropower sites.

### 12. Describe how a solar photovoltaic cell converts sunlight to electricity. How do irradiance, orientation and temperature affect output? (Asked 2026)

**Principle: the photovoltaic effect.** Light falling on a semiconductor junction produces a voltage.

**Process**
1. A cell has a layer of n-type silicon on p-type silicon. Their meeting surface is the p-n junction, which has a built-in electric field.
2. A photon with enough energy frees an electron and leaves a hole.
3. The field drives electrons to the n-side and holes to the p-side.
4. Through an external wire the electrons flow as direct current.
5. An inverter changes it to alternating current. Cells form panels; panels form arrays.

[Diagram:]
```
   sunlight
   \  \  \  \
 ===== glass =====
 --- metal grid ---  (−) ---> load
 |    n-type      |
 |--- junction ---|
 |    p-type      |
 --- back contact -  (+) <--- load
```

**Factors**
- **Irradiance:** output rises almost in proportion to light intensity; cloud and dust reduce it.
- **Orientation:** best when rays strike at right angles. In Pakistan, face south with a tilt near the latitude; trackers add output.
- **Temperature:** hotter cells give lower voltage; output falls about 0.4% per °C above 25 °C.

### 13. What is electromagnetic radiation? Give its types and their applications. (Asked 2016, 2023, 2024, 2025)

**Definition.** Electromagnetic radiation is energy that travels as waves of electric and magnetic fields vibrating at right angles to each other and to the direction of travel. It needs no medium and moves at 3 × 10⁸ m/s in a vacuum. c = fλ and E = hf.

[Diagram: a horizontal arrow "increasing frequency and energy →"; seven boxes in order: Radio | Microwave | Infrared | Visible | Ultraviolet | X-ray | Gamma; above it "← increasing wavelength".]

| Type | Applications |
|---|---|
| Radio waves | Radio and TV broadcasting; MRI |
| Microwaves | Cooking; mobile phones; radar; satellite links |
| Infrared | Remote controls; night-vision and thermal cameras; fibre-optic signals |
| Visible light | Sight; photosynthesis; lasers |
| Ultraviolet | Sterilising water and instruments; vitamin D formation; detecting forged notes |
| X-rays | Imaging bones; CT scans; baggage scanners |
| Gamma rays | Cancer treatment; sterilising medical supplies and food; checking welds |

High-frequency radiation (UV, X-rays, gamma rays) can damage living cells and must be used with protection.

### 14. Differentiate between ionic and covalent bonding, with examples. (Asked 2019, 2024)

**Why atoms bond.** Atoms bond to gain a full outer shell (octet) and so become more stable.

**Ionic bond.** Formed when a metal atom transfers electrons to a non-metal atom. The resulting positive and negative ions attract each other.
Example: Na (2,8,1) gives one electron to Cl (2,8,7) → Na⁺ Cl⁻.

**Covalent bond.** Formed when two non-metal atoms share one or more pairs of electrons.
Example: in H₂O, oxygen shares one pair with each hydrogen.

[Diagram: left, Na with an arrow passing one dot to Cl, then "Na⁺  Cl⁻"; right, two overlapping circles H and H with two dots in the overlap, and H–O–H.]

| Point | Ionic | Covalent |
|---|---|---|
| Electrons | Transferred | Shared |
| Between | Metal and non-metal | Non-metals |
| Particles | Ions | Molecules |
| Melting point | High | Usually low |
| In water | Usually soluble | Often insoluble |
| Conducts | When molten or dissolved | Usually not |
| Examples | NaCl, MgO, CaCl₂ | H₂, O₂, H₂O, CH₄, CO₂ |

A third type, the metallic bond, holds metal atoms together by a "sea" of free electrons.

### 15. What is radioactivity? Differentiate between natural and artificial radioactivity. (Asked 2020, 2022, 2024)

**Definition.** Radioactivity is the spontaneous emission of radiation from an unstable nucleus. Becquerel discovered it in 1896.

| Radiation | Nature | Stopped by |
|---|---|---|
| Alpha | Helium nucleus, +2 | Paper |
| Beta | Fast electron, −1 | Thin aluminium |
| Gamma | Electromagnetic wave | Thick lead or concrete |

[Diagram: a source sending three rays; alpha stops at a sheet of paper, beta at aluminium, gamma is weakened by a lead block.]

**Laws.** Decay is random and unaffected by temperature or pressure. The rate is proportional to the number of undecayed nuclei. In one **half-life**, half of them decay.

| Point | Natural | Artificial (induced) |
|---|---|---|
| Occurs | By itself in nature | When stable nuclei are bombarded with particles |
| Elements | Heavy ones: uranium, thorium, radium | Any; made in reactors and accelerators |
| Control | None | Controlled |
| Discovered | Becquerel, 1896 | Joliot-Curies, 1934 |
| Examples | U-238, Ra-226 | Co-60, I-131 |

**Uses:** cancer treatment, medical tracers, carbon dating, power. **Hazards:** cancer and genetic damage.

### 16. Evaluate the impact of excessive fertilizer use on soil chemistry and groundwater quality. (Asked 2019, 2025, 2026)

**Fertilizers** supply plant nutrients, mainly nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P) and potassium (K). They raise yields, but overuse harms soil and water.

**Effect on soil chemistry**
1. **Acidification:** ammonium fertilizers release acid as they turn to nitrate; pH falls.
2. **Salinity and hardening:** salts build up; soil structure breaks down.
3. **Loss of organic matter and microbes:** earthworms and useful bacteria decline.
4. **Nutrient imbalance:** excess N and P lock up zinc, iron and other micronutrients.
5. Release of nitrous oxide, a strong greenhouse gas.

**Effect on groundwater**
1. **Nitrate leaching:** nitrate dissolves easily and washes down to the water table.
2. Nitrate above 50 mg/L in drinking water causes **blue baby syndrome** in infants.
3. Run-off carries N and P to lakes and rivers: **eutrophication**, algal blooms, loss of oxygen, fish deaths.
4. Impurities such as cadmium accumulate.

[Diagram: field with fertilizer; arrows down "nitrate leaching" to "groundwater"; arrow sideways "run-off" to a pond with "algal bloom".]

**Remedies:** soil testing, balanced and split doses, slow-release urea, organic manure, legume rotation.

**Judgement:** fertilizer is necessary for food security; the harm comes from excess and poor timing.

### 17. Differentiate between vaccines and antibiotics. Why is casual use of antibiotics dangerous? (Asked 2016, 2019, 2022)

**Vaccine.** A preparation of weakened or killed germs, or parts of them, that trains the immune system to make antibodies and memory cells. It **prevents** disease.
**Antibiotic.** A drug that kills bacteria or stops their growth. It **treats** bacterial infection.

| Point | Vaccine | Antibiotic |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Prevention | Cure |
| Given | Before infection | After infection |
| Acts on | Viruses and bacteria | Bacteria only |
| How | Stimulates the body's own immunity | Attacks the bacteria directly |
| Effect lasts | Years or for life | Only while taken |
| Examples | Polio, measles, hepatitis B, BCG | Penicillin, amoxicillin, ciprofloxacin |

**Dangers of casual antibiotic use**
1. **Resistance:** surviving bacteria multiply and the drug stops working (drug-resistant typhoid and TB in Pakistan).
2. Kills useful gut bacteria; causes diarrhoea and fungal infections.
3. Allergic reactions; kidney or liver damage.
4. No effect on viral illnesses such as colds and flu.
5. An incomplete course leaves the toughest bacteria alive.

**Rule:** only on prescription, in the right dose, for the full course.

### 19. What is double circulation? How is the heart adapted to maintain it? (Asked 2021; heart topics 2016, 2017, 2019, 2025)

**Definition.** In double circulation the blood passes through the heart twice in one complete circuit of the body.

1. **Pulmonary circulation:** right ventricle → pulmonary artery → lungs → pulmonary veins → left atrium. Blood gains oxygen.
2. **Systemic circulation:** left ventricle → aorta → body → venae cavae → right atrium. Blood delivers oxygen.

[Diagram: heart as four boxes: RA and RV on the left of the page, LA and LV on the right; a loop up to "LUNGS" from RV returning to LA; a loop down to "BODY" from LV returning to RA; arrows; thick wall on LV.]

**Adaptations of the heart**
1. **Four chambers** with a **septum**, so oxygen-rich and oxygen-poor blood never mix.
2. **Thick left ventricle** to pump at high pressure to the whole body; thinner right ventricle gives gentle pressure to the lungs.
3. **Valves** (tricuspid, bicuspid, semilunar) prevent backflow.
4. **Cardiac muscle** contracts without tiring; the pacemaker (SA node) sets the rhythm.
5. **Coronary arteries** supply the heart muscle itself.

**Advantage:** efficient oxygen supply for an active, warm-blooded body.

### 20. Describe the digestion of carbohydrates, proteins and fats in the human body. (Asked 2018, 2023)

**Digestion** is the breakdown of large food molecules into small, soluble ones that can be absorbed.

[Diagram: outline of the gut labelled mouth, oesophagus, stomach, liver, gall bladder, pancreas, small intestine, large intestine.]

| Place | Carbohydrates | Proteins | Fats |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mouth | Salivary amylase: starch → maltose | — | — |
| Stomach | — | Pepsin (with HCl): proteins → peptides | — |
| Duodenum | Pancreatic amylase: starch → maltose | Trypsin: proteins → peptides | Bile emulsifies; lipase: fats → fatty acids + glycerol |
| Small intestine wall | Maltase, sucrase, lactase → glucose, fructose, galactose | Peptidases → amino acids | Completed |
| **End product** | **Simple sugars** | **Amino acids** | **Fatty acids and glycerol** |

**Absorption.** Finger-like villi in the small intestine absorb sugars and amino acids into the blood and fatty acids into the lymph. The large intestine absorbs water.

**Role of helpers:** bile (from the liver) has no enzymes but breaks fat into droplets; the pancreas supplies amylase, trypsin and lipase.

### 21. "The liver is the chief chemist of the body." Comment. (Asked 2016, 2024)

The liver is the largest gland (about 1.5 kg), in the upper right abdomen. It carries out hundreds of chemical tasks, so the statement is justified.

[Diagram: liver as a wedge shape above the stomach; gall bladder beneath it; bile duct to the duodenum; "hepatic portal vein" entering from the intestine.]

**Functions**
1. **Bile production:** bile emulsifies fats and neutralises stomach acid.
2. **Sugar control:** stores extra glucose as glycogen and releases it when blood sugar falls.
3. **Fat metabolism:** makes cholesterol and carries fats.
4. **Protein metabolism:** breaks down surplus amino acids and turns toxic ammonia into urea.
5. **Blood proteins:** makes albumin and clotting factors.
6. **Detoxification:** makes drugs, alcohol and poisons harmless.
7. **Storage:** iron and vitamins A, D, E, K and B12.
8. **Breakdown of old red cells:** excretes bilirubin in bile.
9. **Heat and defence:** produces heat; its cells engulf germs.

It receives digested food directly from the intestine through the hepatic portal vein, which lets it process nutrients before they reach the rest of the body. It can also regenerate.

### 22. What is hepatitis? Give its types, symptoms and prevention. (Asked 2020, 2023, 2024, 2025)

**Definition.** Hepatitis is inflammation of the liver, most often caused by viruses; also by alcohol and some drugs.

| Type | Spread | Course | Vaccine |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Contaminated food and water | Short illness | Yes |
| B | Blood, unsafe injections, sex, mother to baby | May become chronic; cirrhosis, liver cancer | Yes |
| C | Blood: reused syringes, unscreened transfusion, unsterile instruments | Usually chronic | No, but curable with antiviral tablets |
| D | Blood; only with B | Worsens B | B vaccine protects |
| E | Contaminated water | Short illness; dangerous in pregnancy | Limited |

**Symptoms:** tiredness, fever, loss of appetite, nausea, pain in the upper right abdomen, dark urine, pale stools, jaundice. B and C may stay silent for years.

**Prevention**
- A and E: safe water, sanitation, hand washing.
- B, C, D: a new syringe every time, screened blood, sterilised dental and barber tools, no sharing of razors, hepatitis B vaccination at birth.

Pakistan has one of the world's highest hepatitis C burdens, mainly from unsafe injections.

### 23. Explain the causative agent, mode of transmission and prevention of dengue. What is the role of the Aedes mosquito? (Asked 2016, 2026)

**Causative agent.** Dengue virus, an RNA virus of the Flavivirus group, with four types (DENV-1 to 4). Infection gives immunity only to that type; a later infection with another type can be severe.

**Vector.** The female *Aedes aegypti* mosquito: black with white stripes, bites by day, breeds in clean standing water in containers.

**Transmission cycle**
[Diagram: a circle: infected person → mosquito bites and takes up virus → virus multiplies in mosquito for 8–12 days → mosquito bites healthy person → illness after 4–10 days → back to start.]
The virus does not pass directly from person to person.

**Role of Aedes.** It is the only natural link between people. It lives near homes, bites several people for one meal, stays infective for life, and its eggs survive dry for months. Numbers rise after the monsoon.

**Symptoms:** high fever, severe headache, pain behind the eyes, joint and muscle pain, rash; in severe cases bleeding and shock.

**Prevention:** remove standing water weekly, cover tanks, larvicides and spraying, repellents, nets, full sleeves, community clean-ups.
**Treatment:** fluids, rest, paracetamol; no aspirin.

## Environmental science

### 24. Describe the layered structure of the atmosphere. On what basis is it divided? (Asked 2020, 2021, 2023)

**Basis.** The atmosphere is divided by the way temperature changes with height.

[Diagram:]
```
 600+ km  EXOSPHERE     merges with space; satellites
  85 km   THERMOSPHERE  temp rises; ionosphere; auroras
  50 km   MESOSPHERE    temp falls to −90 °C; meteors burn
  12 km   STRATOSPHERE  temp rises; ozone layer
   0 km   TROPOSPHERE   temp falls; weather, clouds
```
Draw a zig-zag temperature line through the bands.

| Layer | Features |
|---|---|
| Troposphere (0–12 km) | About 75–80% of the air; all weather; temperature falls 6.5 °C per km |
| Stratosphere (12–50 km) | Ozone layer absorbs ultraviolet, so temperature rises; calm; jets fly here |
| Mesosphere (50–85 km) | Coldest layer; meteors burn up |
| Thermosphere (85–600 km) | Very hot, very thin; ionosphere reflects radio waves; auroras form here; space station orbits here |
| Exosphere (above 600 km) | Fades into space; high satellites |

**Composition:** nitrogen 78%, oxygen 21%, argon 0.93%, carbon dioxide about 0.04%, plus water vapour and trace gases.

**Importance:** supplies oxygen, shields from ultraviolet and meteors, keeps the Earth warm.

### 25. Describe the water cycle and its importance. (Asked 2016, 2021, 2026)

**Definition.** The water (hydrological) cycle is the continuous movement of water between the oceans, the atmosphere and the land, driven by the Sun's energy and gravity.

[Diagram: sea on the left, mountains on the right, sun above; arrows: up from sea "evaporation", up from trees "transpiration", cloud "condensation", down on mountains "precipitation", along the surface "run-off", into the soil "infiltration", underground to the sea "groundwater flow".]

**Processes**
1. **Evaporation** from seas, lakes and soil; **transpiration** from plants.
2. **Condensation:** vapour cools and forms clouds.
3. **Precipitation:** rain, snow, hail.
4. **Infiltration:** water soaks in and recharges groundwater.
5. **Run-off:** water flows by streams and rivers back to the sea.

**Water stores:** oceans hold about 97.5%. Fresh water is about 2.5%, most of it in ice and groundwater.

**Importance**
- Acts as natural distillation: gives fresh water to the land.
- Refills rivers, glaciers and aquifers.
- Spreads heat and moderates climate.
- Supports farming, drinking water and hydropower.
- Shapes the land and carries nutrients.

### 26. What is the greenhouse effect? How is it linked to global warming? (Asked 2016, 2019, 2022)

**Greenhouse effect.** A natural process in which gases in the atmosphere trap heat radiated by the Earth and keep the surface warm.

[Diagram: Sun; arrow "short-wave sunlight" reaching the ground; arrow up "infrared heat"; band of dots "CO₂, CH₄, N₂O, water vapour"; some arrows escape to space, others bend back "trapped heat".]

**Mechanism**
1. Sunlight passes through the air and warms the ground.
2. The ground emits infrared heat.
3. Greenhouse gases absorb it and send part back down.
4. Average temperature stays near 15 °C instead of −18 °C. So the natural effect is a blessing.

**Enhanced greenhouse effect.** Burning fossil fuels, deforestation, farming and industry have raised CO₂ from about 280 to about 425 parts per million. More heat is trapped. The result is **global warming**: the world is already about 1.3 °C warmer than in pre-industrial times.

**Effects:** heatwaves, floods, droughts, melting glaciers, rising seas, crop losses, spread of disease. Pakistan emits under 1% of the gases but suffers heavily (2022 floods).

**Remedies:** renewable energy, energy saving, tree planting, the Paris Agreement targets.

### 27. What is ozone depletion? How can the ozone layer be protected? (Asked 2017, 2019)

**Ozone layer.** A region of the stratosphere (15–35 km) rich in ozone (O₃). It absorbs most harmful ultraviolet radiation.

**Ozone depletion** is the thinning of this layer by human-made chemicals: chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) from refrigerators, air conditioners and sprays, halons from fire extinguishers, methyl bromide and some solvents.

**Mechanism**
1. Ultraviolet light breaks a CFC molecule and frees a chlorine atom.
2. Cl + O₃ → ClO + O₂
3. ClO + O → Cl + O₂
4. The chlorine is free to repeat the cycle. One atom destroys about 100,000 ozone molecules.

[Diagram: Earth's surface; band labelled "ozone layer" with a gap "ozone hole"; arrows of "UV" stopped by the band but passing through the gap; a fridge and spray can with arrows up "CFCs".]

The Antarctic ozone hole was reported in 1985.

**Effects:** skin cancer, cataracts, weaker immunity, crop damage, harm to plankton.

**Protection**
- Montreal Protocol (1987): phase-out of ozone-depleting substances by all countries.
- Ozone-friendly refrigerants; safe disposal of old appliances.
- Avoid halons and methyl bromide; public awareness.

The layer is now recovering.

### 28. What is acid rain? Explain its causes, effects and prevention. (Asked 2017, 2018)

**Definition.** Acid rain is rain, snow or fog with a pH below 5.6.

**Causes.** Sulphur dioxide and nitrogen oxides from coal- and oil-fired power plants, factories, smelters and vehicles.

**Formation**
- 2SO₂ + O₂ → 2SO₃; SO₃ + H₂O → H₂SO₄
- 4NO₂ + O₂ + 2H₂O → 4HNO₃

[Diagram: factory and car emitting "SO₂, NOx" → cloud "H₂SO₄, HNO₃" → wind → rain falling on a forest, a lake and a building.]

**Effects**
1. Lakes and rivers turn acidic; fish die.
2. Forests: damaged leaves; nutrients washed from soil.
3. Crops and soil fertility decline.
4. Marble and limestone buildings corrode: CaCO₃ + H₂SO₄ → CaSO₄ + H₂O + CO₂ (Taj Mahal).
5. Metals rust faster.
6. Lung irritation from the gases and particles.

**Prevention**
- Low-sulphur fuel; washing coal.
- Scrubbers on chimneys; catalytic converters on vehicles.
- Renewable energy and energy saving.
- Liming of acid lakes.
- International agreements to cut emissions.

### 29. Differentiate between fog and smog. Give the causes, effects and control of smog. (Asked 2018, 2023)

### 30. How does water pollution occur? Explain the role of oxygen-demanding wastes and of sewage bacteria in a polluted river. (Asked 2018, 2022, 2023, 2025)

**Water pollution** is any change in water quality that makes it harmful to life or unfit for use.

**How it occurs:** untreated sewage, industrial effluent, farm run-off (fertilizers, pesticides), solid waste, oil, hot water from power plants.

**Oxygen-demanding wastes.** Organic matter such as sewage, manure and food-industry waste. Bacteria use dissolved oxygen to decompose it. The oxygen needed is the **biochemical oxygen demand (BOD)**. High BOD means heavy pollution.

**What sewage bacteria do**
1. Aerobic bacteria multiply and break the waste into CO₂, water, nitrates and phosphates.
2. Dissolved oxygen falls; fish die.
3. If oxygen runs out, anaerobic bacteria produce methane and hydrogen sulphide; the water turns black and smells.
4. Downstream the waste is used up, oxygen returns, and the river recovers (self-purification).

[Diagram: graph of dissolved oxygen against distance downstream: high, sharp fall after "sewage outfall", low "septic zone", gradual rise "recovery".]

**Other effects:** nutrients cause eutrophication; germs cause cholera and typhoid.

**Control:** sewage treatment (primary, secondary, tertiary), treating effluent at source, less fertilizer run-off, enforcement of standards.

### 31. What is solid waste? Discuss its management, and the problems of solid waste management in Pakistan. (Asked 2016, 2022)

**Solid waste** is unwanted solid material from homes, shops, industry, hospitals, farms and building sites. Types: municipal, industrial, hazardous, biomedical, electronic.

**Management steps:** generation → storage → collection → transport → processing → disposal.

[Diagram: inverted pyramid, top to bottom: Reduce → Reuse → Recycle → Recover (compost, energy) → Dispose (landfill).]

**Disposal methods**
- **Sanitary landfill:** waste compacted in a lined pit and covered daily; leachate and gas collected.
- **Composting:** organic waste turned into manure.
- **Incineration:** burning at high temperature, with energy recovery.
- **Recycling:** paper, glass, metal, plastic.
- **Biogas** from organic waste.

**Problems in Pakistan**
1. Large and growing volume; only half to two-thirds is collected.
2. Open dumping and burning; very few engineered landfills.
3. No separation at source; hospital waste mixed in.
4. Shortage of funds, equipment and trained staff.
5. Weak enforcement; low public awareness.
6. Plastic bags choke drains and cause urban flooding.

**Way forward:** the 3Rs, segregation, composting, sanitary landfills, waste-to-energy, public–private waste companies.

### 32. Discuss the role of remote sensing and GIS in environmental science. (Asked 2016, 2022, 2024)

**Remote sensing** is collecting information about the Earth's surface from a distance, usually by satellite or aircraft, by recording the electromagnetic energy that objects reflect or emit. Each surface has its own spectral signature.

**GIS (Geographic Information System)** is a computer system that stores, analyses and displays data linked to locations, as map layers.

[Diagram: Sun → rays to the ground → reflected rays to a "satellite sensor" → "ground station" → computer with stacked "GIS layers: land use, rivers, roads".]

**Roles**
1. **Land use:** mapping forests, farms and city growth; detecting deforestation.
2. **Water:** mapping rivers, lakes and groundwater; water quality.
3. **Disasters:** flood mapping and forecasting, drought monitoring, cyclone tracking, damage assessment.
4. **Glaciers:** watching retreat and dangerous glacial lakes.
5. **Air:** smoke, dust, ozone.
6. **Agriculture:** crop health and yield estimates.
7. **Coasts and oceans:** sea temperature, oil spills, mangroves.
8. **Planning:** siting landfills and dams; environmental impact studies; wildlife habitats.

**Advantages:** wide and repeated coverage, access to remote areas, speed, lower cost.

Pakistan: SUPARCO's satellites supported flood mapping in 2010 and 2022.

### 34. What are the causes of food deterioration? Describe the methods of food preservation. (Asked 2020, 2021, 2026)

### 35. Differentiate between preservatives and antioxidants, with examples. (Asked 2016, 2023, 2024)

Both are food additives that lengthen shelf life, but they act against different causes of spoilage.

**Preservatives** prevent or slow spoilage by microorganisms (bacteria, yeasts, moulds).
- Natural: salt, sugar, vinegar, oil, spices.
- Chemical: sodium benzoate (juices, ketchup), sulphites (dried fruit), nitrites (cured meat), calcium propionate (bread).

**Antioxidants** prevent or delay oxidation of fats, oils and pigments, which causes rancidity, browning and loss of vitamins.
- Natural: vitamin C (ascorbic acid), vitamin E (tocopherols), carotenoids, plant polyphenols.
- Synthetic: BHA, BHT, TBHQ, used in oils, ghee, crisps and cereals.

| Point | Preservative | Antioxidant |
|---|---|---|
| Acts against | Microbes | Oxygen |
| Prevents | Rotting, souring, mould, food poisoning | Rancidity, off-flavour, colour loss |
| Typical foods | Juices, pickles, jams, bread, meat | Oils, fats, fried snacks |
| Examples | Sodium benzoate, salt | Vitamin E, BHT |

Some substances do both (sulphites). All permitted additives must stay within safe daily limits.

In the body, dietary antioxidants also protect cells from free-radical damage.

## Information technology

### 36. What is an optical fibre? Describe its structure and working, and give its advantages. (Asked 2016, 2018, 2019, 2020, 2022, 2024, 2025)

**Definition.** An optical fibre is a hair-thin strand of very pure glass or plastic that carries data as pulses of light.

**Structure**
1. **Core:** central glass; higher refractive index; carries the light.
2. **Cladding:** glass layer of lower refractive index; reflects light back.
3. **Buffer coating and jacket:** plastic layers for protection.

[Diagram:]
```
 ================================== cladding
   \    /\    /\    /\    /\    /
    \  /  \  /  \  /  \  /  \  /     core (light zig-zags)
     \/    \/    \/    \/    \/
 ================================== cladding
```
Add a cross-section: small circle "core", ring "cladding", outer ring "coating".

**Working: total internal reflection.** Light striking the core–cladding boundary at more than the critical angle is completely reflected. It travels along the core by repeated reflection.
Transmitter (laser or LED: electrical signal → light) → fibre → repeaters → receiver (photodiode: light → electrical signal).

**Advantages**
- Very high bandwidth and speed.
- Low signal loss over long distances.
- Immune to electrical interference; secure.
- Thin, light, no sparks.

**Limits:** costly to install; fragile; skilled joining needed.
**Uses:** internet backbone and undersea cables, cable TV, endoscopes.

### 37. What is GPS? Explain how it determines the position of a receiver. (Asked 2016, 2020, 2023, 2026)

**Definition.** The Global Positioning System (NAVSTAR) is a satellite navigation system run by the United States that gives position, velocity and time anywhere on Earth, in all weather.

**Three segments**
1. **Space:** at least 24 satellites in six orbits about 20,200 km high.
2. **Control:** ground stations that track the satellites and correct their clocks.
3. **User:** receivers in phones, vehicles, ships and aircraft.

**Working**
1. Each satellite broadcasts its position and the exact time from an atomic clock.
2. The receiver measures how long the signal took. Distance = speed of light × time.
3. One satellite puts the receiver on a sphere; a second narrows it to a circle; a third to a point.
4. A fourth satellite corrects the receiver's clock error and gives height.
This method is **trilateration**.

[Diagram: Earth's surface with a receiver; four satellites above; a line from each to the receiver labelled d₁, d₂, d₃, d₄; note "position = where the spheres meet".]

**Uses:** navigation and maps, surveying, fleet tracking, farming, rescue, military guidance, precise timing for banks and networks.

Similar systems: GLONASS, Galileo, BeiDou.

**Definition.** Artificial intelligence (AI) is the branch of computer science that builds machines able to do tasks that normally need human intelligence: learning, reasoning, problem solving, and understanding language and images. John McCarthy coined the term in 1956.

**Branches**

## General ability

### 39. What are mental ability scales? How do they differ from IQ tests? (Asked 2016, 2020, 2022)

**Mental ability** is the capacity to learn, reason, solve problems and adapt.

**Mental ability scales** are standardised tests that measure specific abilities separately and give a profile of scores. The main constructs are:
- **Verbal:** understanding and using words.
- **Numerical:** working with numbers.
- **Mechanical:** understanding physical principles such as levers and gears.
- **Social:** understanding and dealing with people.
- Also spatial ability, memory and speed of perception.

| Point | Mental ability scales | IQ tests |
|---|---|---|
| Measure | Specific abilities | General intelligence |
| Result | Several scores (a profile) | One score (IQ; average 100) |
| Basis | Intelligence has many factors (Thurstone) | One general factor "g" (Spearman) |
| Use | Career guidance, selection, placement | Overall classification of ability |
| Examples | Differential Aptitude Tests | Stanford–Binet, Wechsler scales |

**Aptitude vs intelligence.** Aptitude is the potential to learn a particular skill; intelligence is general ability.

**Good tests** are reliable, valid and standardised.

Intelligence depends on both heredity and environment (nutrition, home, schooling).

### 40. A complete Section B question, laid out as it should be written (20 marks)

This shows the presentation that earns full marks. The four parts are of the types set most often.

**Q. (a)** In a 15%-off sale a fridge and a freezer cost Rs 57,120 together. The freezer's normal price is Rs 40,000. Find the normal price of the fridge. **(5)**

**Given:** sale total = Rs 57,120; discount = 15%; freezer (normal) = Rs 40,000.
**Required:** normal price of the fridge.
**Solution:**
Sale price = 85% of normal price.
Normal total = 57,120 ÷ 0.85 = 57,120 × 100/85 = Rs 67,200.
Fridge = 67,200 − 40,000.
**Answer: Rs 27,200**
**Check:** 85% of 67,200 = 57,120 ✓

**(b)** Divide Rs 4,320 among three people in the ratio 2 : 3 : 7. **(5)**

**Solution:**
Sum of ratio parts = 2 + 3 + 7 = 12.
One part = 4,320 ÷ 12 = Rs 360.
Shares = 2 × 360, 3 × 360, 7 × 360.
**Answer: Rs 720, Rs 1,080, Rs 2,520**
**Check:** 720 + 1,080 + 2,520 = 4,320 ✓

**(c)** A man walks 110 m east, turns right and walks 40 m, turns right and walks 50 m, then turns right and walks 120 m. How far is he from his starting point? **(5)**

**Solution:**
[Diagram: compass with N at top; path drawn from start S: east 110, south 40, west 50, north 120 to end point E; dotted line S to E.]
Directions: east → (right) south → (right) west → (right) north.
Net eastward distance = 110 − 50 = 60 m.
Net northward distance = 120 − 40 = 80 m.
Distance from start = √(60² + 80²) = √(3,600 + 6,400) = √10,000.
**Answer: 100 m (to the north-east of the start)**

**(d)** Find the correct word from each set of jumbled letters: (i) THRSI (ii) SCHAMOT (iii) HIODALY (iv) CKANS (v) MNIKPPU. **(5)**

**Answer:**
(i) SHIRT (ii) STOMACH (iii) HOLIDAY (iv) SNACK (v) PUMPKIN
