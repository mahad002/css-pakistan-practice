# Model Essay 5 (abstract / quotation topic)

**Topic:** "True peace is not merely the absence of tension; it is the presence of justice."

*Source of the quotation: Martin Luther King Jr. (Stride Toward Freedom, 1958; restated in the Letter from Birmingham Jail, 1963). Asked in CSS 2025. Type: abstract quotation. Notice the method: explain the statement, turn it into a claim, test it at the level of the person, the society, the state and the world, face the objections, apply it to Pakistan.*

## Essay

In the winter of 1955, the city of Montgomery in the American state of Alabama was, by the standards of its rulers, at peace. Black citizens sat at the back of the buses and gave up their seats to white passengers when told to. They drank from separate fountains and sent their children to separate and poorer schools. There were no riots. Then a seamstress named Rosa Parks refused to stand, the black population of the city stopped riding the buses for more than a year, and the authorities complained that a young preacher called Martin Luther King was disturbing the peace of a contented town. King's answer has outlived the quarrel that produced it. The peace he was accused of disturbing, he said, was no peace at all. True peace is not merely the absence of tension; it is the presence of justice.

The sentence draws a line between two things that are easily confused. One is quiet: the absence of open conflict, of protest, of the sound of gunfire. Later scholars of conflict, following the Norwegian sociologist Johan Galtung, have called this negative peace. The other is a condition in which people live together on terms that they can accept as fair, so that they have no reason to fight. This is positive peace. King's claim is that the first without the second is a counterfeit. A quiet that rests on fear, force or exhaustion is only a pause between conflicts. Peace that lasts, whether in a home, a nation or the world, has to be built on justice, because people will accept an order that treats them fairly and will sooner or later resist one that does not.

The terms need a little care. By tension King meant the visible disturbance that the comfortable dislike: marches, strikes, angry speech, and at the extreme, violence. By justice he meant something that every tradition recognises even when it defines the details differently: that each person is given what is due, that the same law applies to the strong and the weak, that burdens and benefits are shared on some principle other than power. The statement does not say that tension is good or that order is worthless. The word "merely" is important. Peace includes the absence of violence; it is not exhausted by it.

Why should this be so? The reasoning is not difficult. A grievance that is forbidden to speak does not vanish. It goes underground, where it grows more bitter and less reasonable, and it waits for the moment when the hand that holds it down is weak or distracted. Secondly, a peace kept by fear is expensive. It needs police, informers, prisons and soldiers in proportion to the resentment it must contain, and the cost rises every year. Thirdly, and this was Galtung's insight, injustice is itself a kind of violence. When a child dies of a disease that could have been prevented for a few rupees, or a labourer spends his life in debt bondage, no shot has been fired, but a human being has been destroyed as surely as by a bullet. To call such a condition peace because it is quiet is to mistake the silence of the victim for consent.

The truth of the statement can be seen first on the smallest scale. A household in which one member is beaten or belittled, and says nothing because protest would bring worse, is calm in the evenings. Nobody would call it a happy home. An office where employees dare not question an unfair superior has no arguments, and also no loyalty and no honest effort. In both cases the quiet is real, and so is the injury beneath it, and the day the fear is removed the quarrel long postponed will arrive with interest. Families and firms that are truly at peace are those in which complaints can be raised and are fairly answered. They often have more open disagreement, not less.

Within nations, history has run the experiment many times. The American South that King challenged had kept its racial order for nearly a century after the abolition of slavery, by law and by the lynch mob. The civil rights movement brought sit-ins, marches and violent reaction; it also brought the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965, and a society that, for all its remaining faults, is more at peace with itself than the one it replaced. South Africa under apartheid was an orderly state with a strong army and police. The order was maintained by denying the vote, the land and free movement to the great majority of the population, and it produced four decades of resistance, repression and killing. Peace came only when the injustice was dismantled, through negotiations and the election of 1994, and was secured by a Truth and Reconciliation Commission that preferred the telling of truth to revenge.

A more recent example comes from the Arab world. In December 2010 Tunisia was regarded as one of the most stable countries in the region. That month a young fruit seller, Mohamed Bouazizi, whose cart had been confiscated and who had been humiliated by local officials, set himself on fire in front of a government building. Within weeks the president had fled, and within months governments had fallen or were fighting for their lives from Egypt to Syria. The stability had been the stillness of a sealed vessel under pressure. Nothing had been visible, and everything had been wrong.

Between nations the lesson is written still more plainly. When the First World War ended, the victors met at Versailles in 1919 and imposed on Germany a settlement that it regarded as a humiliation: the loss of territory, crushing reparations, and sole blame for the war. The British economist John Maynard Keynes, who attended the conference, resigned and warned in The Economic Consequences of the Peace that a peace of punishment would ruin Europe. The French commander Marshal Foch is said to have remarked that it was not a peace but an armistice for twenty years. He was right almost to the year. After the Second World War, the victors chose differently. The United States helped to rebuild Germany and Japan through the Marshall Plan and the post-war reforms; France and Germany placed their coal and steel under a common authority in 1951, the seed of today's European Union. The defeated were given a fair place in the new order, and Western Europe, the battlefield of centuries, has not seen a war between its great powers since. The first settlement removed tension for a time. The second supplied justice, and with it peace.

Our own age provides examples of the first kind. In Palestine, every round of fighting for seventy-five years has ended in a ceasefire, and every ceasefire has been described as peace. None has lasted, because none has dealt with the cause: a people living under occupation without a state of their own. The International Court of Justice advised in July 2024 that the continued occupation of Palestinian territory is unlawful. The war that began in October 2023 killed tens of thousands of people in Gaza before a ceasefire was reached in October 2025. Whether that ceasefire becomes a peace will depend on whether it leads to the justice that earlier agreements, from Oslo onward, promised and postponed. A truce that leaves the grievance intact is a timetable for the next war.

Kashmir teaches the same lesson closer to home. The United Nations Security Council resolved in 1948 and 1949 that the future of the state should be decided by its people in a free plebiscite. The promise remains unkept. In August 2019 India revoked the region's special constitutional status and placed it under tight control, and its government spoke of a return to normality as tourists arrived and protests ceased. In April 2025 an attack on tourists at Pahalgam killed twenty-six people, and within three weeks two nuclear-armed states were exchanging missile and air strikes. Whatever view one takes of the events, the quiet of the previous years had plainly not been peace. A dispute that is frozen is not thereby settled.

Pakistan has reason to understand King's sentence from its own history. For two decades after independence, the eastern wing of the country complained that its language had been slighted, that its jute earned foreign exchange which was spent in the west, and that its majority did not translate into power. For long stretches the complaints were contained and the country was outwardly calm. When the party that won the general election of 1970 was not allowed to form a government, the grievance could no longer be contained, and by December 1971 the country had been cut in two. No external enemy could have achieved that result had justice been done in time.

The lesson has had to be relearned. In Balochistan, cycles of military action have several times restored order, and each time the insurgency has returned, because the province's sense that it receives too little of its own wealth and too small a voice in its own affairs was not addressed. The former tribal areas were governed until 2018 under a colonial regulation of 1901 that denied their people ordinary courts and rights, and they became a home for militancy. Their merger with Khyber Pakhtunkhwa was an act of justice, though an incomplete one. On a more ordinary level, more than two million cases are pending in Pakistan's courts, and the World Justice Project's index for 2025 places the country 130th of 143 for the rule of law. When militants seized Swat in 2008 and 2009, part of their early appeal lay in the promise of quick justice in a district where ordinary litigation took years. A state that does not provide justice leaves the field open to those who offer a brutal imitation of it.

King's principle is not foreign to the tradition from which Pakistan draws its ideals. The Quran commands the believers to stand firmly for justice even against themselves, their parents and their relatives, and warns that hatred of a people must not lead to unfair treatment of them. The Prophet of Islam (peace be upon him), on arriving in Madinah, did not simply impose order on a city torn by tribal feuds; he concluded a charter that defined the rights and duties of each community, Muslim and non-Muslim alike. Peace in this view is the fruit of justice, not a substitute for it. Other traditions reach the same conclusion by their own roads. Pope Paul VI summed up the teaching of his church in 1972 in seven words: if you want peace, work for justice. The Indian emperor Ashoka, sickened by the slaughter of his own conquest of Kalinga, turned from war to the fair government of his subjects and is remembered for the second achievement, not the first. The Charter of the United Nations, written in 1945 by people who had seen two world wars, lists among its purposes not only the prevention of war but respect for the equal rights of peoples. Wherever human beings have thought seriously about peace, they have found justice standing behind it.

There are, however, two serious objections. The first holds that order must come before justice, since without order there can be no justice at all. The philosopher Thomas Hobbes argued that where no power keeps the peace, life is "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short". The collapse of the state in Libya, Somalia or Syria has not brought justice to their peoples; it has brought warlords. Those who disturb an imperfect order in the name of justice, the argument runs, may destroy the only protection the weak possess. The second objection asks: whose justice? Every party to a conflict believes its cause is just. If peace must wait until justice, as each side defines it, has been fully done, wars will never end. Insisting on the punishment of every crime can make a ruler fight to the last soldier where an amnesty would have sent him into exile.

Both objections contain truth, and neither defeats the statement. To the first it may be answered that King never denied the value of order; he denied that it was enough. A ceasefire is the necessary first step, because nothing can be built while the guns are firing. But a first step is not a destination. Order that is used to begin righting wrongs grows into peace; order that is used to make wrongs permanent stores up the next explosion. To the second it may be answered that the justice which makes peace is not the complete victory of one side's claims. It is a fair process and a tolerable outcome: equal rights, a voice in decisions, a reasonable share of resources, and an honest account of what was done. South Africa did not hang the servants of apartheid, and Northern Ireland's agreement of 1998 released prisoners on both sides. In each case the victims received acknowledgement and the future received fair rules. That was justice enough for peace to take root.

What, in practice, does the presence of justice require? Within a state, it requires first the rule of law: courts that decide in reasonable time and that bind the powerful as they bind the poor. It requires political inclusion, so that every region and community has a real share in decisions, which in Pakistan means honouring the federal bargain and building local government. It requires economic fairness: not equality of wealth, but a floor below which no one falls and a ladder that the talented poor can climb. After conflict, it requires truth-telling and reconciliation before grievances harden into inheritance. Among states, it requires that international law be applied to friends as well as enemies. The Security Council's failure to act on Gaza and Ukraine because of the veto has damaged the belief that there is any law above power. A world that spent nearly 2.9 trillion dollars on its armed forces in 2025, according to the Stockholm International Peace Research Institute, has purchased a great deal of deterrence. It has not purchased peace. It is fitting that the nations of the world, when they wrote their development goals in 2015, joined "peace, justice and strong institutions" in a single goal, for they are a single task.

The city of Montgomery was quiet in 1955, and its quiet was a lie. Martin Luther King's achievement was to make his countrymen see the difference between a society in which nothing is heard and a society in which nothing is wrong. That difference is the whole meaning of his sentence. The absence of tension can be produced by fear, and fear can be produced by anyone with enough force; but what force holds down, it must go on holding, and it can never rest. The presence of justice is harder to bring about, for it asks the strong to give up advantages and the angry to give up revenge. Yet it is the only arrangement that keeps itself, because it leaves people with nothing to fight for that they do not already have. For individuals, for Pakistan and for the world, the choice is between the peace that must be guarded and the peace that guards itself.
