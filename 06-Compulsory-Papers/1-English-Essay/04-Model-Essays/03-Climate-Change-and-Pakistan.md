## Essay

In the last week of August 2022, a traveller on the national highway in Sindh would have seen water on both sides of the road as far as the horizon. It was not the sea, and it was not the Indus. It was rain. The province had received several times its normal rainfall for the month, on land so flat that the water had nowhere to go. Beneath it lay cotton fields, village schools, graveyards and the mud houses of people who were now living on the raised road itself, under plastic sheets, beside their surviving animals. By the time the water drained away, months later, about 33 million Pakistanis had been affected and more than 1,700 were dead. The Secretary-General of the United Nations, who visited in September, said that he had never seen climate carnage on such a scale.

For many years climate change was treated in Pakistan as a subject for seminars: an "environmental issue" to be listed after security, the economy and politics, and handled by a small ministry with a small budget. The statement under discussion claims that this view is out of date. An environmental issue concerns the quality of life: cleaner air, greener cities, protected wildlife. A question of survival concerns life itself: whether people can eat, drink, earn and remain in their homes. Climate change has moved from the margins of Pakistan's policy to the centre of its existence: it threatens the country's food, water, economy, health and internal peace all at once. Pakistan did little to cause the danger, but its survival now depends as much on reforming its own governance as on justice from the world.

The evidence of the past few years supports the stronger word. The floods of 2022 damaged or destroyed more than two million houses and displaced some eight million people. The official Post-Disaster Needs Assessment, prepared with the World Bank, the Asian Development Bank, the European Union and the United Nations, put the damage at 14.9 billion dollars and the economic losses at a further 15.2 billion, about thirty billion dollars in all, in an economy then worth roughly 350 billion. It estimated that the disaster would push between eight and nine million more people below the poverty line. Three years later it happened again. In the monsoon of 2025, cloudbursts in the mountains of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and river floods across Punjab killed more than a thousand people and displaced over four million. The Pakistan Economic Survey 2025-26 puts the losses at 822 billion rupees, more than half of them in agriculture. Two national catastrophes in four summers cannot be called exceptional events. They are the new pattern.

The manner of the 2025 disaster deserves a moment's attention, because it shows how the danger is changing. On a single morning in the middle of August, a cloudburst over the hills of Buner and the neighbouring districts of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa sent walls of water, mud and boulders down narrow valleys. Villages that had stood for generations disappeared within minutes, and hundreds of people were killed before any warning could reach them. Within a fortnight the Ravi, the Sutlej and the Chenab rose together and flooded the plains of Punjab, the country's granary. The first was a disaster of the mountains and the second of the rivers; the first struck the poor of the hills and the second the farms that feed the nation. A country can prepare for one kind of flood. Pakistan must now prepare for every kind at once.

Floods are only the most visible sign. In the spring of 2022, before the rains, temperatures in Jacobabad passed 50 degrees Celsius, close to the limit of what the human body can endure. A heat wave in Karachi in 2015 killed more than a thousand people in a few days. The Economic Survey records 2025 as the second-warmest year in Pakistan in sixty-five years. In the north, the country holds more than seven thousand glaciers, among the largest stores of ice outside the polar regions. They are melting faster as the air warms, and the lakes that form at their edges burst without warning. In May 2022 such a flood from the Shishper glacier swept away a bridge on the Karakoram Highway in Hunza. Germanwatch, a research organisation that publishes the Climate Risk Index, ranked Pakistan as the country most affected by extreme weather in the world for the year 2022, and its 2026 edition places Pakistan fifteenth for the whole period from 1995 to 2024.

To see why this amounts to a question of survival, consider first what Pakistanis eat. Agriculture provides 23.4 per cent of national income and about a third of all jobs, and it supplies the cotton on which the main export industry depends. Every major crop is sensitive to heat and to the timing of rain. A hot March shrivels wheat as the grain is forming, as it did in 2022. A late or violent monsoon drowns cotton and rice. The floods of 2025 alone cost agriculture an estimated 430 billion rupees. Cotton output, hit by heat, pests and floods over a decade, has fallen to about seven million bales, roughly half of what it was ten years ago, and the textile industry now imports what the country once grew. In a land where about forty per cent of young children are already stunted by poor nutrition, a climate that makes harvests uncertain strikes at the body of the next generation.

Consider next what Pakistanis drink. The country lives on a single river system, the Indus and its tributaries, which waters one of the largest irrigation networks on earth. That system is fed in large part by snow and glaciers. As they melt faster, rivers will first carry more water, bringing floods, and later, as the ice shrinks, less. Pakistan can store only about thirty days of its water needs, so it cannot save the surplus of a wet year for a dry one. The water available to each person has fallen from over five thousand cubic metres at independence to around a thousand, the level that experts define as scarcity. To this natural stress a political one has been added: in April 2025 India declared that it was holding the Indus Waters Treaty of 1960 in abeyance. A warming climate and a weakening treaty together make water the most dangerous question in the region.

The economy can no more escape than the farm. A country that loses thirty billion dollars in one flood and several billion more in the next cannot accumulate the capital it needs to grow. Roads, bridges, canals and power lines that took years to build must be built again. Each disaster forces the state to borrow for relief, adding to a public debt that already absorbs close to half of the federal budget in interest. The World Bank's Country Climate and Development Report of 2022 estimated that the combined effects of climate change and environmental damage could reduce Pakistan's national income by 18 to 20 per cent by 2050. No conceivable economic reform could compensate for a loss on that scale. Climate change is therefore not a separate chapter of Pakistan's economic problem; it is written through every page of it.

The human cost is counted in more than money. After the waters of 2022 stopped moving, malaria, dengue, cholera and skin diseases spread through the camps. Thousands of schools and clinics were destroyed, and many children who left school that summer never went back. Heat reduces the hours that labourers and farm workers, who are the poorest Pakistanis, can safely work. Women and children, who are less likely to be able to swim, to own a telephone or to leave home without permission, die in disasters in greater numbers. A country that already ranks 168th of 193 on the Human Development Index sees its slow gains in health and schooling washed away with its bridges.

Finally, climate change threatens the peace of the country. When land is ruined, people move, and they move to cities that cannot house them. Karachi, Lahore and other cities receive families from flooded or drought-stricken districts every year, while the cities themselves flood because their natural drains have been built over. On the coast, the sea is advancing into the Indus delta as the river's flow weakens, and salt has taken farmland in Thatta and Badin. Scarcity also sets province against province. The dispute over new canals on the Indus in 2025 showed how quickly water can become a question of national unity. A state whose citizens are fighting over a shrinking river is not secure, whatever the strength of its army.

Why is Pakistan so exposed? Part of the answer lies outside its borders, and it is a matter of plain injustice. The gases that are heating the planet were put into the air chiefly by the industrial economies of the West over two centuries and, more recently, by the largest Asian economies. Pakistan produces less than one per cent of global emissions. It is paying for a problem it did not create. The world has acknowledged this in words. Rich countries promised a hundred billion dollars a year in climate finance by 2020 and met the target two years late. A Loss and Damage Fund was agreed in 2022, partly under the impression made by the pictures from Sindh, but the sums pledged to it are a small fraction of what one Pakistani flood costs. At the Geneva conference of January 2023, donors pledged over nine billion dollars for reconstruction; most of it was loans, and it arrived slowly. The climate conference in Baku in 2024 set a new goal of 300 billion dollars a year by 2035, and the conference in Belém in 2025 called for adaptation finance to be tripled, without saying who would pay. Meanwhile the United States, historically the largest emitter, has again withdrawn from the Paris process. Pakistan is right to demand justice, and the advisory opinion of the International Court of Justice in July 2025, which held that states have legal duties to protect the climate, strengthens its case.

It would be unfair to say that Pakistan has done nothing for itself. A decade ago a farmer's petition led the Lahore High Court, in the Leghari case of 2015, to declare that the state's delay in acting on climate change violated the fundamental rights of citizens. The tree-planting campaign that began in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and was extended to the whole country has restored forest on a significant scale. The government has pledged that sixty per cent of its electricity will come from renewable sources by 2030, has prepared a National Adaptation Plan, and in 2025 obtained about 1.4 billion dollars from the International Monetary Fund's Resilience and Sustainability Facility for climate reforms. These are real steps. They are also small beside the size of the threat, and most of them remain on paper.

For the other part of the answer lies at home, and an honest essay must say so. Rain becomes a disaster when it meets bad governance. Hotels and houses are built on riverbeds, in Swat and elsewhere, in open breach of the law, and are swept away with their occupants. Forests that hold mountain soil have been cut; the country's forest cover is only about five per cent of its area. Natural drainage channels in the plains and the cities have been blocked by roads, housing schemes and encroachments. Warnings from the Meteorological Department do not reach the last village, because there is no functioning local government to carry them. Pakistan has a Climate Change Act, a national policy, a National Adaptation Plan and ambitious targets for renewable energy, but plans without budgets and enforcement do not hold back water. The lesson of 2022 was written down in detail, and in 2025 the same mistakes drowned people in the same places.

Some will argue that a poor country with debts to pay, schools to build and terrorism to fight cannot afford to place climate first, and that to call it a question of survival is the language of activists and donors. The objection deserves a serious answer. It is true that Pakistan cannot spend as Germany or China can, and true that poverty kills as surely as floods. But the objection assumes that climate change is one problem on a list, competing with the others for attention. It is better understood as a multiplier of all the others. It deepens poverty, raises food prices, widens the trade deficit through lost exports and extra imports, drives migration and strains relations between provinces and with neighbours. A government that ignores it will find every other target moving further away. Moreover, the cheapest protections, such as enforcing building rules, clearing drains, planting trees and warning people in time, cost far less than rebuilding after each disaster. Adaptation is an economy, not a luxury.

What, then, must be done to survive? The first priority is adaptation, since Pakistan cannot by itself change the world's emissions. This means early-warning systems that reach every mobile phone, strict removal of construction from floodplains, embankments and drainage built to new standards, and houses, schools and roads designed to withstand water and heat. The second is to reform water and agriculture together: lining canals, replacing flood irrigation with efficient methods, pricing water sensibly, storing it in reservoirs large and small, recharging groundwater, and moving towards seeds and crops that tolerate heat. The third is energy. Pakistan's households and factories have installed solar panels on a very large scale since 2023 because solar power is now the cheapest electricity available; the state should build on this with a grid that can absorb it, for clean energy also reduces the import bill that each oil shock inflates. The fourth is institutional. Disaster management has to start in the district, which requires elected local governments with funds and trained staff. The fifth is diplomacy: Pakistan should lead the vulnerable countries in pressing for grants in place of loans, for debt relief tied to climate investment, and for simple access to the Loss and Damage Fund, and it must show that the money it receives is spent openly and well. The last is awareness. A people that understands the danger will plant, conserve and vote accordingly; climate should be taught in every school and spoken of from every pulpit as the trust it is.

The word "environment" suggests something that surrounds us and can be attended to when more pressing business is done. Pakistan no longer has that choice. The water that covered Sindh in 2022 and Punjab in 2025 carried away houses, harvests, schools and lives, and with them any excuse for treating climate as a secondary concern. The danger was made largely by others, and the world owes Pakistan far more than it has paid. Yet waiting for justice is not a strategy for survival. The same country that cannot control the monsoon can decide where it builds, how it uses its river, what it plants and whom it empowers to act. Those who lived on that highway in Sindh under plastic sheets have already paid their share. What remains is for the state to do its own.
