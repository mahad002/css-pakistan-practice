# Model Essay 4

**Topic:** Artificial intelligence: the death of human creativity or its new instrument?

## Essay

In 1839, when the first photographs were shown in Paris, the painter Paul Delaroche is said to have exclaimed that from that day painting was dead. He had reason to fear. The chief business of a painter at the time was to record what people and places looked like, and here was a box that did it in minutes, more accurately than any hand. Yet painting did not die. Freed from the duty of copying the visible world, painters turned to what the camera could not do: the light of the Impressionists, the distortions of Picasso, the inner landscapes of the abstract artists. Photography, for its part, became an art of its own. The machine that was supposed to kill creativity ended by giving it two lives.

The same alarm is being sounded today, and more loudly. Since the release of ChatGPT in November 2022, systems of "generative" artificial intelligence have learned to write essays and poems, paint in any style, compose music, and produce computer code, all in seconds and at the request of anyone who can type a sentence. Hundreds of millions of people now use them every week. If creativity is the making of things that are new and valuable, and a machine can make such things without rest and without pay, what is left for the human creator? Artificial intelligence does not kill creativity; it kills the comfortable belief that producing text, images and tunes is the same thing as creating. By taking over imitation, it threatens the livelihoods of many who create and tempts everyone to stop thinking, but purpose, experience and judgement remain human, and whether the machine becomes an instrument or a crutch will be decided by how people use it and how societies govern it.

The fear deserves to be stated at its full strength before it is answered. The first ground for it is simple capability. Skills that used to take a decade to acquire, such as drawing a convincing human figure, writing clean prose, or arranging music for an orchestra, can now be imitated by anyone with a subscription. In 2022 an image generated with the program Midjourney won first prize in the digital art category at the Colorado State Fair, to the anger of the human entrants. When the result of long apprenticeship can be had for nothing, the apprenticeship itself begins to look pointless, and a young person may reasonably ask why he should spend years learning to do badly what a machine does well.

The second ground is economic. Most people who live by creative work do not produce masterpieces. They draw illustrations for advertisements, write product descriptions, translate documents, compose background music, design logos. This everyday work is exactly what generative systems do cheaply. The writers of Hollywood went on strike for nearly five months in 2023, and one of their central demands was protection against being replaced or undercut by AI. In Pakistan, where hundreds of thousands of young freelancers earn foreign exchange by writing content and designing graphics for clients abroad, the threat is immediate. A society in which only a few stars can earn a living from creative work will have fewer creators, and a thinner culture, whatever its machines can do.

The third ground is moral. These systems did not learn to write and paint from nowhere. They were trained on millions of books, articles, pictures and songs made by human beings, in most cases without permission or payment. Authors, artists and newspapers have gone to court in several countries; The New York Times sued the maker of ChatGPT in 2023. To many creators, AI is not a new colleague but a mechanism that has absorbed their life's work and now competes with them by imitating it.

The fourth and fifth grounds concern the culture and the mind. A language model works by predicting what is likely to come next on the basis of what has been written before. Its natural product is therefore the probable, the average, the already familiar. One study published in the journal Science Advances in 2024 found that people who wrote short stories with the help of AI produced work that was judged better individually but that was more similar to one another's than stories written without it. If everyone draws from the same well, everything begins to taste the same. And there is the question of what happens to abilities that are no longer used. A student who has every essay drafted by a machine does not learn to organise thought, just as a person who is always driven never learns the roads. Socrates, in Plato's dialogue Phaedrus, feared that writing would weaken memory. He was partly right. The fear that AI will weaken thought itself cannot be dismissed as old-fashioned.

These are serious arguments. But they prove less than they seem to, and history is the first witness against them. Every powerful tool of expression has been greeted as the end of creativity. Writing was to destroy memory and with it wisdom. Printing was to drown learning in a flood of worthless books. Photography was to kill painting. Recorded sound was to kill live music, and the synthesiser and the drum machine were to kill musicianship. In each case something was indeed lost: the bard who knew ten thousand lines by heart is gone. But in each case far more was created than destroyed, because the new tool removed a mechanical labour and released attention for something else. Printing gave Europe the novel and the scientific journal. Recording gave the world jazz, qawwali heard in Tokyo, and the possibility that a singer in Lahore could reach an audience of millions.

The games of chess and Go offer a still closer parallel. In 1997 a computer defeated the world chess champion, Garry Kasparov, and in 2016 the program AlphaGo defeated Lee Sedol, one of the greatest Go players in history. Many predicted that humans would lose interest in games they could no longer win. The opposite happened. More people play chess now than at any time before, and they play it better, because they train with machines. In the Go match, the program made a move so unexpected that commentators first took it for a mistake; in a later game Lee replied with a move that the program had judged almost impossible, and won. Professional players have said that the machine did not end their art but showed them regions of it they had never explored. The machine won the game. It did not take the meaning of playing away.

That last distinction leads to the heart of the matter: what creativity is. The philosopher Margaret Boden distinguishes three kinds. One combines familiar ideas in unfamiliar ways. Another explores the possibilities of an existing style. The third, the rarest, transforms the rules themselves, so that things can be thought which could not be thought before. Machines are already impressive at the first two. But creation, in the full human sense, involves more than the object produced. It involves a person who has something to say, who has lived in the world, and who addresses other persons.

Consider experience. Faiz Ahmed Faiz wrote some of his finest poems in prison in the 1950s. Beethoven composed his Ninth Symphony when he could no longer hear. Ghalib's verses carry the grief of a man who buried his children and saw Delhi destroyed in 1857. When we read or listen to such work, we are not only enjoying a pleasing arrangement of words or notes. We are receiving testimony: this is what it was like to be a human being in that hour. A system that has never been imprisoned, bereaved or afraid can produce verses in the manner of Faiz. It cannot bear witness, because it has witnessed nothing. Consider intention. A machine produces when asked, and does not care what it produces. A human creator begins from a need: to protest, to praise, to understand, to be remembered. The question "why make this?" is answered only by someone with a life at stake.

Consider, too, judgement. An image generator can supply a thousand pictures in an hour. Someone must decide which, if any, is worth keeping, and that decision requires taste formed by years of looking, reading and living. As production becomes effortless, selection becomes the real work, and selection is a human act. And consider courage. The great changes in art and science came when someone broke the rules that everyone else obeyed: when the Impressionists left the studio, when Iqbal turned the Persian and Urdu lyric into a call for the awakening of the self. A system built to produce the most probable continuation of the past is, by its design, the least likely rebel.

If these things remain human, then AI is better understood as an instrument, and a remarkable one. For the working writer it is a tireless assistant that offers a first draft to argue with, a dozen alternative titles, a translation to be corrected. For the film-maker without money it can sketch scenes that once required a studio. It widens the door to creation. A villager with a story to tell but no schooling in grammar, a designer who cannot draw, a researcher whose English is weak: all can now bring their ideas into a form that others will take seriously. This matters greatly in countries such as Pakistan, where talent is common and training is rare.

Nor should creativity be confined to the arts. Science is creative work, and here the instrument has already proved itself. The program AlphaFold predicted the three-dimensional structures of almost all known proteins, a problem that had resisted biologists for fifty years, and its makers shared the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 2024. The discovery did not make biologists unnecessary; it gave them a map and set them to asking questions that could not have been asked before. New art forms are also appearing, as they appeared after the camera, in which the artist's material is the machine itself.

There is even a sense in which the flood of machine-made material raises the value of what is truly original. When competent prose and pleasant pictures are free, competence ceases to impress. What stands out is the distinct voice, the unexpected idea, the work that could only have come from one particular life. Readers have always known the difference between a personal letter and a printed circular, and as the circulars multiply, the letter is prized more. The market may reward fewer people for routine work, which is a real hardship. But it is likely to reward real originality more, not less, and the hand-made thing, in art as in craft, acquires a worth that the mass-produced copy cannot have.

To call AI an instrument is not to call it harmless. An instrument can injure the hand that holds it, and the three real dangers named earlier remain. The danger to income and rights is a matter of justice. If creators are not paid when their work is used to train a system, and cannot compete with its output, the supply of new human work on which the systems themselves depend will dry up. The danger to the mind is a matter of education. The tool helps those who can already think and write; it harms those who use it to avoid learning how. A calculator is a blessing to an engineer and a curse to a child who has not learned to add. The danger to the culture is a matter of trust. When convincing text, pictures and voices can be manufactured without limit, the public square fills with imitation and deception, and people may cease to believe anything they see.

A determined pessimist will reply that all this underestimates the machine. The systems improve every few months. Perhaps they will soon write poems that move readers to tears and that no critic can tell from a human's. What then becomes of the claim that experience and intention are essential? The reply is that creation has never been only a matter of output. If a perfect forgery of a Ghalib ghazal were composed by a program, we might admire its skill, but on learning its origin we would feel that something had been taken away, namely the person behind the words. Art is an act of communication between minds. Even a flawless imitation leaves the human need to express and to be understood exactly where it was. People did not stop running races when the motor car was invented. They will not stop making songs because a machine can make them too.

Whether AI turns out to be an instrument or a crutch will therefore depend on choices. The first concerns education. Schools and universities must make sure that students build the underlying skills, such as reading closely, writing by hand, reasoning step by step, before they are handed the tool, and must then teach them to use it critically, as a partner to be questioned. Systems of examination that reward memorised answers, as Pakistan's largely do, are the worst possible preparation, for reproducing stored material is the one thing a machine does perfectly. The World Economic Forum's Future of Jobs Report 2025 lists creative and analytical thinking among the skills employers will need most. The second choice concerns law. Creators should be asked before their work is used for training, credited when it is, and paid. Content made by machines should be labelled as such, as the European Union's AI Act of 2024 begins to require. The third is personal: the discipline to write the first draft oneself, to use the machine to test an idea and not to replace it.

For Pakistan the stakes are practical. The government approved a National Artificial Intelligence Policy in 2025, and the country has one of the world's largest communities of freelancers and a median age of about twenty. Its languages, music, calligraphy, textiles and stories are creative wealth that the global systems, trained mostly on English, hardly know. Young Pakistanis who learn to command these tools can carry that wealth to the world. Those who merely lean on them will find that a machine can lean for less.

The painter who announced the death of painting in 1839 was wrong, but his mistake was a natural one. He had confused his art with its most mechanical part. The same confusion lies behind the fear that artificial intelligence means the death of creativity. What the machine has mastered is production: the fluent arrangement of words, shapes and sounds on the pattern of what already exists. What it has not touched is the reason anyone creates at all, which is that human beings live, suffer, hope and wish to tell one another about it. AI will end some careers, change most, and punish the lazy, and societies owe protection to those it displaces. But a mind that has something to say has just been handed the most powerful instrument since the printing press. The danger was never that the machine would learn to create. It is that human beings, finding production so easy, might forget to have something to say.
