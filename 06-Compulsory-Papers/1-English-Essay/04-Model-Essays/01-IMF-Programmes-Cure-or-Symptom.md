# Model Essay 1

**Topic:** IMF programmes: a cure for Pakistan's economy or a symptom of its illness?

## Outline

## Essay

In the first week of February 2023, the State Bank of Pakistan held less than three billion dollars in foreign exchange reserves, barely enough to pay for three weeks of imports. Containers of raw material were stuck at Karachi port because banks could not open letters of credit. Inflation was on its way to 38 per cent. A country of more than 240 million people stood a few signatures away from default. The signatures came in the summer, on a nine-month Stand-By Arrangement with the International Monetary Fund, and the immediate danger passed. It was not the first time. Pakistan has gone to the Fund more than twenty times since 1958, which makes it one of the institution's most frequent borrowers.

This record has divided opinion in Pakistan into two camps. One camp treats the IMF as a doctor whose medicine is bitter but necessary: without it the economy would have collapsed, and with it the patient is recovering. The other treats the Fund as the illness itself: an outside power that imposes taxes, raises electricity bills and keeps the country poor and obedient. Both camps are looking in the wrong direction. A doctor who is called to the same house every three or four years is evidence that something in the house is wrong. IMF programmes are neither the cure for Pakistan's economic illness nor its cause; they are the clearest symptom of it. Each programme has stopped a crisis, and none has prevented the next, because the disease, which is a state that cannot tax, an economy that cannot export and a political class that postpones reform, lies at home.

It helps to be clear about what an IMF programme is. The Fund is a lender of last resort for countries. When a member can no longer pay for its imports or service its foreign debt, and private lenders have withdrawn, the Fund provides dollars in instalments. In return the government promises to follow certain policies, usually a smaller budget deficit, a market-based exchange rate, tighter monetary policy and structural changes such as energy price reform. The instalments are released only after reviews confirm that the promises are being kept. A programme, in other words, is emergency finance tied to a plan of treatment. Whether the treatment works depends a great deal on whether the patient continues it after leaving the hospital.

The argument that the IMF is a cure rests on solid facts. The first is that it prevented default in 2023. A default would have cut Pakistan off from imported fuel, medicines and machinery, wrecked the banking system and thrown millions more into poverty, as Sri Lanka's default did in 2022. The second fact is the stabilisation that followed. Under the Stand-By Arrangement and then the 37-month Extended Fund Facility of about seven billion dollars approved in September 2024, reserves were rebuilt to roughly 21.8 billion dollars by March 2026. Inflation, which had peaked at 38 per cent in May 2023, fell to an average of under five per cent in the 2024-25 fiscal year. The government recorded a primary surplus, meaning that its revenue exceeded its spending before interest payments, of 3.2 per cent of GDP in the first nine months of 2025-26. Growth recovered to 3.7 per cent in that year, the fastest in four years, according to the Pakistan Economic Survey.

There are two further points in the Fund's favour. An IMF programme is a certificate that other lenders trust. The World Bank, the Asian Development Bank, friendly governments in the Gulf and China, and the international bond market all take their signal from it. Pakistan's credit ratings were upgraded in 2025 and again in 2026, and the country returned to the bond market. Secondly, the Fund's conditions have pushed through changes that Pakistani governments had avoided for decades. The provinces passed laws in 2025 to tax agricultural income on the same footing as other income, a reform discussed since the 1970s. The exchange rate is no longer held at an artificial level by burning reserves. These are real gains, and those who call the IMF a curse rarely explain how they would have been achieved without it.

Yet the opposing case also rests on facts, and they are felt in every household. Stabilisation has been bought by squeezing demand, and the squeeze has fallen on those least able to bear it. To meet revenue targets quickly, governments have raised the taxes that are easiest to collect: sales tax, the levy on petrol, and income tax deducted from salaries. Electricity and gas tariffs have risen steeply to cover the losses of the power sector. For a salaried family in Lahore or a small manufacturer in Faisalabad, the IMF programme has meant higher bills, higher prices and lower real income. The World Bank estimates that real wages fell by more than three per cent a year between 2018-19 and 2024-25. The Economic Survey of 2025-26 reports that national poverty rose from 21.9 per cent in 2018-19 to 28.9 per cent in 2024-25, which means roughly seventy million Pakistanis below the poverty line. Unemployment has risen to about seven per cent.

Critics add that the Fund's medicine weakens the patient's ability to recover. High interest rates, which touched 22 per cent in 2023, made borrowing impossible for business. Costly energy has made Pakistani exports uncompetitive, and several textile mills have closed or reduced shifts. Development spending is the first item to be cut when a deficit target must be met, so roads, schools and hospitals are postponed. Finally, there is the question of sovereignty. When the national budget is discussed with a visiting mission before it is presented to parliament, citizens are entitled to ask who governs. The economist Joseph Stiglitz made this argument about IMF programmes in East Asia a generation ago, and it resonates in Pakistan today.

Both cases are persuasive, and both stop too early. The defenders of the Fund explain why each programme was necessary; they do not explain why so many have been necessary. The critics describe the pain of the treatment; they do not explain why the patient keeps falling ill. The pattern itself is the most important fact. In 1988, 1993, 1997, 2001, 2008, 2013, 2019 and 2023, Pakistan signed a new arrangement, and each time the sequence of events beforehand was the same. Growth picked up, usually helped by cheap credit and an overvalued rupee. Imports of fuel, machinery and consumer goods surged. Exports did not keep pace. Reserves drained away, the rupee fell, and the government of the day, often a new one blaming its predecessor, went to Washington. Most of these programmes were abandoned or left incomplete once the immediate danger had passed. The Fund's own Independent Evaluation Office studied Pakistan as early as 2002 as a case of "prolonged use" of its resources. A problem that repeats with such regularity is not an accident, and it is not manufactured abroad. It is the symptom of a structural illness with at least five causes.

The two most recent cycles show the mechanism plainly. In the 2017-18 fiscal year the economy grew by about six per cent, its best performance in over a decade, and the current account deficit widened to roughly nineteen billion dollars. Within a year the reserves were nearly exhausted, and in July 2019 a new government signed a six-billion-dollar programme with the Fund. The adjustment was painful, the pandemic interrupted it, and by 2021-22 cheap credit had again lifted growth to about six per cent. Again the current account deficit swelled, this time to around seventeen billion dollars, and again the reserves drained away. The floods of 2022 and a period of political turmoil then turned a familiar shortage into the emergency of early 2023. Two different governments, two booms and two rescues followed one script within five years.

The first cause is that the Pakistani state does not collect enough revenue to pay for itself. Tax revenue is close to a tenth of GDP, well below the level of most comparable economies. The burden is also unfairly spread. Large landowners, traders and property investors have historically paid little, while manufacturers and salaried workers pay much. Because revenue is short, the government borrows to meet its ordinary expenses. Public debt stood at 83.3 trillion rupees in March 2026, and interest payments now consume close to half of the federal budget. A state in this position is always one bad year away from a crisis, whatever the IMF does.

The second cause is that Pakistan does not earn enough foreign exchange. Its exports of goods have stayed between about twenty-five and thirty-two billion dollars for more than a decade. Vietnam, which was poorer than Pakistan in 1990, exports more than four hundred billion dollars' worth of goods a year. Pakistan's exports are concentrated in low-value textiles and rice, and its industry depends on imported fuel and inputs. Whenever the economy grows by more than four per cent, the import bill outruns export earnings and remittances, and the reserves begin to fall. Growth in Pakistan is thus limited by the balance of payments. Remittances of about forty billion dollars a year from workers abroad hide the weakness without curing it.

The third cause is the energy sector and the state-owned enterprises. Pakistan has installed generating capacity of nearly 50,000 megawatts, far above its peak demand, and yet its electricity is among the most expensive in the region. The reason is a set of contracts that oblige the government to pay plants for capacity whether or not their power is used, combined with heavy losses in transmission, theft and unpaid bills. The resulting "circular debt" runs into trillions of rupees and lands, in the end, on the budget. Other public enterprises, from railways to distribution companies, add their losses to the pile. Every rupee spent covering these losses is a rupee borrowed.

The fourth cause is low investment, in machines and above all in people. Total investment is only about 14 per cent of GDP, roughly half the level of the fast-growing Asian economies. Some 26 million children are out of school, the literacy rate is 63 per cent, and public spending on education has fallen below one per cent of GDP. An economy cannot move from stitching garments to designing software with a workforce that has not been taught to read well. Low skills keep exports low, and low exports bring the country back to the Fund.

The fifth cause is political, and it explains why the other four persist. Reform has costs today and benefits tomorrow, and Pakistani governments have rarely been confident of seeing tomorrow. No prime minister has completed a five-year term. Under such uncertainty the rational politician borrows, subsidises and spends to produce a visible boom before the next election or the next upheaval, and leaves the bill to a successor. Those who would lose from reform, such as untaxed sectors and protected industries, are well represented in the assemblies. The IMF arrives when this game has run out of money. To blame it for the pain is to blame the fire brigade for the water damage.

The experience of other countries confirms that the Fund is an instrument whose result depends on its user. India went to the IMF in 1991 with reserves sufficient for only a few weeks of imports. Its government used the crisis to dismantle import licensing, open the economy and reform its finances, and India has not needed an IMF programme since. South Korea accepted a very large rescue in 1997, restructured its banks and corporations, and repaid the loans ahead of schedule within four years. At the other extreme stands Argentina, which has signed more than twenty agreements with the Fund, including the largest loan in the institution's history in 2018, and remains caught between inflation and default. The medicine was the same. The difference lay in whether the country treated the programme as a turning point or as a breathing space.

It may be objected that this lets the Fund off too lightly. Critics point out, with reason, that IMF programmes are designed around targets that can be measured within months: the deficit, the reserves, the money supply. This encourages governments to raise revenue by the quickest means, which are also the most regressive, and to cut the development budget, which harms future growth. The Fund has admitted that it underestimated the damage of austerity in Greece and elsewhere, and its insistence on rapid adjustment in Pakistan during the inflation of 2022-23 deepened the hardship. These criticisms are fair, and Pakistan's negotiators should press for better-sequenced programmes with stronger protection for the poor. But they do not overturn the argument. A flawed prescription can explain why one programme hurt more than it should have. It cannot explain why a country has needed more than twenty of them in under seven decades. Nor did the Fund prevent Pakistan from taxing agriculture, selling loss-making enterprises or educating its children in the years between programmes. Those were domestic choices.

If the diagnosis is correct, the way to end dependence on the IMF is not to denounce it but to make it unnecessary. Five tasks stand out. The first is to tax all incomes on the same principle, whatever their source, and to simplify the system so that paying is easier than evading. The second is to build an export economy by lowering the cost of energy and credit for exporters, supporting information technology, whose export earnings reached about 3.8 billion dollars in the first ten months of 2025-26, and moving up the value chain in textiles and food. The third is to fix the power sector and to privatise or close public enterprises that survive only on subsidies. The fourth is to protect the poor during adjustment through well-targeted cash transfers, and to invest in schools and skills so that the next generation can earn more. The fifth, without which the others will not last, is an agreement among the major political parties on a minimum economic agenda that survives changes of government. The present programme ends in late 2027. The oil shock that followed the outbreak of war in the Gulf in early 2026, which pushed inflation back to nearly 11 per cent in April of that year, has already shown how thin the new stability is. What Pakistan does in the remaining months will decide whether this arrangement is remembered as the last or merely as the latest.

To return to the question: an IMF programme is not a cure, because a cure removes the cause of an illness and the Fund has never been able to do that for Pakistan. It is not the disease either, because the disease was present before each programme and produced it. It is a symptom, as a fever is the symptom of an infection. Fever can be brought down with medicine, and it should be, since an untreated fever can kill. But a patient whose fever returns every few years must sooner or later treat the infection. Pakistan's infection is a state that spends what it does not collect and an economy that buys what it does not earn. The treatment is known, and most of it is written in the country's own policy documents. The reserves that stood at three billion dollars in February 2023 have been rebuilt. Whether they are still standing in 2033, without another signature in Washington, depends on decisions that no foreign institution can take on Pakistan's behalf.
