# Model Essay 2

**Topic:** Without local government, democracy in Pakistan remains incomplete.

## Essay

When the monsoon breaks over Karachi and the streets fill with water, a resident who wants the drain outside her house cleared faces a puzzle. She can call the municipal corporation, the provincial government, a cantonment board, a development authority or a water corporation, depending on which of them controls her street, and each is likely to send her to another. Her vote helped to elect a member of the National Assembly, who sits in Islamabad and debates foreign policy, and a member of the Provincial Assembly, who sits in the provincial capital. Neither was elected to clean drains, though both will be blamed for the flood. Between this citizen and the state there is a gap where a government ought to be.

That gap is the subject of this essay. Democracy is usually discussed in Pakistan as a matter of who rules in Islamabad: whether elections are fair, whether prime ministers complete their terms, whether parliament is supreme. These questions matter. But a democracy is more than a national contest held every five years. It is a system in which people govern themselves at every level at which they share a common life, and the level at which most common life is lived is the street, the village and the town. Democracy in Pakistan stops at the provincial capital. Until elected, funded and empowered local governments exist in every district and city, as Article 140A of the Constitution requires, citizens will have a vote but no voice in the matters that touch their daily lives, and democracy will remain a structure without a ground floor.

A federal democracy may be pictured as a building of three floors. The top floor is the federation, responsible for defence, currency and foreign affairs. The middle floor is the province, responsible for policing, education and health policy, and agriculture. The ground floor is local government: elected councils in union councils, towns, districts and cities, responsible for the services that people meet every morning, such as drinking water, sanitation, street lighting, local roads, markets, parks, and the upkeep of primary schools and clinics. In most of the democratic world this is the floor on which citizens meet the state most often. In Pakistan it is the floor that is most often missing.

The first reason why local government completes democracy is participation. A national election gives the citizen one vote among a hundred million and a representative who may live three hundred kilometres away. A union council gives her a representative who lives in the same neighbourhood, whom she can stop in the street. Self-government becomes a habit only when it is practised over small things. The nineteenth-century observer Alexis de Tocqueville wrote that town meetings are to liberty what primary schools are to science. People learn how to debate, compromise and hold office-holders to account in the council before they do so in the nation.

The second reason is practical. Services are delivered best by the level of government closest to the problem. A provincial secretary in Lahore cannot know which street in Rajanpur has a broken water pipe, and has no reason to care more about it than about ten thousand others. A local council knows, and its members will lose their seats if the pipe is not repaired. This is the principle of subsidiarity: a function should be carried out by the lowest level able to perform it. The principle matters all the more in a country whose provinces are larger than most states. Punjab had more than 127 million people in the 2023 census, which is more than Japan. To run the primary schools and drains of such a population from one secretariat is not federalism; it is centralisation at one remove.

Thirdly, local government sharpens accountability. In the present arrangement the citizen cannot tell who is responsible for a failed service, because responsibility is scattered among departments, authorities and legislators. When one elected mayor is answerable for a city's water, there is a name to praise or to vote out. Fourthly, local councils are the nursery of democratic leadership. Recep Tayyip Erdoğan was mayor of Istanbul before he led Turkey, and Joko Widodo was mayor of a provincial city before he became president of Indonesia. Where there is no such ladder, politics remains the property of a few families who can afford to begin at the top. Fifthly, councils open the door to groups that seldom reach the assemblies. The local government system of 2001 reserved a third of seats for women, and tens of thousands of women held public office for the first time. Finally, a working local tier lowers the stakes of national politics. When every decision and every rupee is controlled from the centre of power, losing a national election means losing everything, and politics becomes a fight to the finish. When power is spread across thousands of councils, those who lose in the capital still govern somewhere.

If the case is so strong, why has Pakistan not built this tier? The answer lies in a paradox of its history. Local governments have been created mainly by military rulers and neglected mainly by elected ones. General Ayub Khan introduced the Basic Democracies in 1959 and used its 80,000 members as an electoral college for his own presidency. General Zia-ul-Haq revived local bodies through ordinances in 1979 and held local elections on a non-party basis. General Pervez Musharraf's devolution plan of 2001 created district governments under elected nazims, with real control over the district administration. In each case the ruler wanted a class of local politicians loyal to him and a way around the national parties. The parties drew the lesson that local government was a weapon against them. When civilian rule returned in 2008, the provinces let the 2001 system lapse, and for years there were no elected local governments at all.

There is also a plainer motive. Members of the provincial and national assemblies in Pakistan are judged by their voters less on legislation than on jobs, roads and gas connections delivered to the constituency. Development funds and influence over local postings are their political capital. An elected district council with its own budget is a competitor for that capital. It is not surprising that the people who must pass strong local government laws are the people with the least interest in doing so.

The Constitution, however, leaves no room for doubt. Article 32 directs the state to encourage local government institutions. Article 140A, inserted by the Eighteenth Amendment in 2010, commands each province to establish a local government system and to devolve political, administrative and financial responsibility and authority to its elected representatives. The Eighteenth Amendment is rightly celebrated for moving power from the federation to the provinces. The devolution was meant to continue downward. It stopped at the provincial capital.

The breach of Article 140A has taken three forms. The first is delay. After 2010 it took repeated orders of the Supreme Court before local elections were held, in Balochistan in 2013 and in the other provinces in 2015. Since then, for most years, at least one province has been without elected councils. In Punjab the councils elected in 2015 were dissolved in 2019, two years before their term ended; the Supreme Court restored them in 2021; and after their term expired the province again went years without elections. Islamabad's local elections have been postponed again and again. The second form is weak law. The provincial acts passed since 2013 keep the important functions, such as building control, water, solid waste and development planning, with provincial departments or with authorities and companies that report to the chief minister. The third is finance. The National Finance Commission award of 2010 raised the provinces' share of federal revenue to 57.5 per cent, but the Provincial Finance Commissions that should pass a fair share on to districts have met rarely or not at all. A council without money or powers is a signboard, not a government.

The cost of the missing tier is visible wherever one looks. Consider the cities. Karachi, a city of more than twenty million people according to the 2023 census and the country's commercial capital, is divided among so many federal, provincial, military and municipal landowners that its elected mayor controls only a part of its territory and few of its services. Its problems of water, waste and transport are those of a city that nobody owns. Lahore has been run for years at a time by provincial officials who are transferred every year or two.

Consider next the legislatures. Because there is no councillor to turn to, citizens take their complaints about sewers and street lights to members of the assemblies. The legislator becomes a municipal contractor, and the work for which he was elected, which is to make laws and examine budgets, is neglected. The absence of local government thus weakens the upper floors as well.

Consider disasters. In the floods of 2022, which affected about 33 million people, and again in the floods of 2025, which killed more than a thousand, relief was organised from provincial capitals and by the armed forces. There were few elected local officials who knew which villages lay in the path of the water, who could warn them, and who could draw up honest lists of those in need. Countries that cope well with disasters do so through local institutions that exist before the disaster arrives. Consider, lastly, the social indicators. Some 26 million Pakistani children are out of school. No provincial department can know why a particular child in a particular village is absent or whether the teacher has come. A school council answerable to an elected local body can.

The deepest cost is harder to measure. A citizen who has never been able to change anything about her street by voting has little reason to believe that voting changes anything at all. The distance between people and state in Pakistan, which shows itself in low tax compliance, in the appeal of those who promise rough justice, and in the readiness of the young to emigrate, begins with this experience of powerlessness close to home.

Other countries faced the same choice and decided differently. India amended its constitution in 1992 to make village panchayats and municipalities a compulsory third tier, with elections every five years, state finance commissions and at least one-third of seats reserved for women. The results vary from state to state, but more than a million women now sit in India's local councils, and no state government can simply abolish them. Indonesia, after the fall of Suharto, transferred most public services and a large share of the budget to its districts from 2001. The reform helped to hold together a vast and varied country and produced a generation of leaders who had first proved themselves as mayors. In both cases the lesson is that local democracy survives when the constitution, not the convenience of the provincial government, guarantees it.

Pakistan's own experience, brief as it has been, points the same way. Under the system of 2001 a villager with a complaint about a school or a dispensary could, for the first time, take it to an elected union nazim in his own area instead of travelling to a district office to wait outside a civil servant's door. In 2013 Khyber Pakhtunkhwa passed a law that created councils at the level of the village and the neighbourhood and promised them a fixed share, not less than thirty per cent, of the province's development budget. Neither experiment was allowed to mature, and both were weakened by the governments that came afterwards. But they showed that when councils are given money and a clear task, citizens use them.

Three objections must be answered. The first is that local governments in Pakistan are tainted by their origin: they were the tools of dictators. That is true as history and irrelevant as an argument. Elections were also held by dictators, and no one concludes that elections should be abolished. The fault lay in using local bodies to replace national democracy; the remedy is to have both. The second objection is elite capture. In much of rural Pakistan, it is said, a union council will simply be taken over by the local landlord or clan chief, and devolution will hand him public money as well as private power. The danger is real. But the landlord already exercises that influence through the police station, the revenue office and the provincial legislator, where no villager can see or challenge it. A council that sits in public, with seats reserved for women, peasants and minorities and with elections held on schedule, gives the weak at least a foothold. The third objection is that local bodies lack capacity and will only decentralise corruption. Capacity, however, is built by use, and a councillor's misuse of funds is easier for neighbours to detect than a misuse buried in a provincial budget of trillions. Each of these objections is an argument for designing local government well. None is an argument for leaving the ground floor empty.

What would completing the structure require? First, constitutional protection. Article 140A should be strengthened to fix the term of local governments, to require elections within a set period of their expiry or dissolution, and to list their core functions, so that no provincial government can suspend them at will. Secondly, money. Provincial Finance Commissions must be constituted on time and must transfer funds by a published formula based on population, poverty and need, not on the favour of the chief minister. Local governments should also be able to raise their own revenue, above all through property tax. Thirdly, clarity of function. Water, sanitation, solid waste, local roads, land use and building control should belong to the local tier, and each large city should have one elected authority in place of a dozen overlapping agencies. Fourthly, legitimacy. Mayors and district chairmen should be directly elected on a party basis, so that parties build organisations from the ground up and citizens know whom to hold responsible. Fifthly, capacity and honesty: trained local staff, simple digital accounts open to the public, and regular audit. Lastly, and most importantly, the political parties must change their view. A party that governs thousands of councils well has a deeper base and a larger pool of tested leaders than one that depends on a handful of families. Local democracy is in the parties' own interest, if they look beyond the next election.

Pakistan has spent much of its history arguing about the top floor of its democratic house: who should live there, and who has the right to evict them. The argument is important, but it has left the ground floor unbuilt, and a house without a ground floor cannot be entered by the people for whom it was meant. Local government is where a citizen first meets the state, first learns that complaint can produce a result, and first discovers that power may be questioned by a neighbour. The Constitution has required it since 2010, and the floods, the uncollected garbage and the empty classrooms supply the evidence of what its absence costs. The resident of Karachi who stands in a flooded street with a telephone in her hand does not need a new philosophy of government. She needs someone to call who was elected to answer. When every Pakistani has that, democracy in Pakistan will be complete, and not before.
