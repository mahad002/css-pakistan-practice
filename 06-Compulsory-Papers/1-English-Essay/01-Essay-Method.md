## 1. What the examiner wants

Put the official words together (syllabus, paper instruction, examiners' reports) and you get five tests:

| Test | The examiner's silent question |
|---|---|
| Relevance | Did this candidate answer **this** topic, or a neighbouring one? |
| Argument | Is there a position, and is it defended with reasons? |
| Organisation | Can I see the plan? Does each part follow from the one before? |
| Content | Are there accurate facts and examples, and are they explained? |
| Language | Is the English correct and easy to read? |

A CSS essay is an **argued answer to a question**, about 2,500–3,000 words long, with an outline in front. It is not a speech, a sermon, a newspaper column, or a list of facts under headings.

## 2. Decoding the topic

The CE-2019 examiners said most candidates wrote without noticing the "twist and emphasis" in the topic. Decoding is how you notice it.

### 2.1 The four topic types

| Type | How to recognise it | What it demands | Past examples (paraphrased) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Argumentative** | A question, a claim, an "X or Y", a provocative statement | Take a side and defend it; deal with the other side | Is rule of law a myth in our country? Are IMF bailouts a road to stability or to disaster? Is the youth bulge a dividend or a bomb? |
| **Expository / issue-based** | A noun phrase naming a problem or field, often with "challenges", "prospects", "way forward" | Explain: what it is, why it matters, causes, effects, remedies. Still needs a thesis | Higher education: ills and remedies. Pakistan's informal economy. Revitalising agriculture |
| **Descriptive / analytical** | Asks you to describe or analyse a concept or trend | Define, break into parts, show how the parts work, judge | An analysis of the globalisation of markets. New waves of feminism and our culture |
| **Abstract: quote, proverb, philosophical statement** | A quotation or a general truth about life, power, truth, hope | Interpret the statement, turn it into a claim, test it against evidence from history, society and personal life | "Imagination is more important than knowledge." Hope as the greatest driving force. With hardship comes ease |

Many topics mix types. "Democracy in Pakistan: hopes and hurdles" is expository in form, but the best answer argues something (for example, that the hurdles are institutional and can be fixed).

**Rule:** whatever the type, you need a thesis. An expository essay without a position reads like notes.

### 2.2 The five-step decode

Do this on the rough page for every topic you shortlist.

1. **Underline the key terms.** Every noun and every qualifying word.
2. **Find the limiters.** Place (Pakistan, Muslim world, developing countries), time (new, emerging, no more), degree (only, always, greatest, worst, real).
3. **Find the tension.** Most CSS topics hide a contrast: X versus Y, appearance versus reality, promise versus result. The CE-2017 report called this the dormant contention.
4. **Rewrite the topic as a question** that can be answered yes, no, or "to this extent".
5. **Define the two or three key terms** in one line each, in your own words.

### 2.3 Worked decodes

**Topic A (2018, paraphrased):** real development should change people's lives, not only the economic statistics.

- Key terms: *real development*, *transform lives*, *economic statistics*.
- Limiter: "real" (so there is a false kind), "not just" (statistics are allowed but are not enough).
- Tension: GDP growth versus human development.
- Question: Is a country developed when its numbers rise but its people's lives do not improve?
- Wrong essay: a general essay on Pakistan's economy. Right essay: growth versus human development, with evidence such as HDI rank, poverty, schooling and health, and a judgement about what development should mean.

**Topic B (2020, paraphrased):** IMF bailouts: roads to stability or recipes for disaster.

- Key terms: *bailouts* (not the IMF in general), *stability*, *disaster*.
- Limiter: none stated, so you may write globally but should centre on Pakistan.
- Tension: short-term rescue versus long-term harm.
- Question: Do IMF programmes stabilise economies or damage them?
- Position options: (a) stability, (b) disaster, (c) they stabilise in the short run but fail without domestic reform. Option (c) is the easiest to defend with evidence.

**Topic C (2026, paraphrased):** cyber security as the new frontier of national security.

- Key terms: *cyber security*, *new*, *national security frontier*.
- Limiter: "new" means you must show what changed. "National security" means the state level, not personal online safety tips.
- Tension: traditional (land, sea, air) versus digital threats.
- Question: Has cyberspace become as important to a state's security as its borders?
- Wrong essay: "uses and abuses of the internet".

**Topic D (2024, paraphrased):** hope is the greatest driving force.

- Key terms: *hope*, *greatest*, *driving force*.
- Limiter: "greatest" forces a comparison with other motives (fear, need, greed, duty).
- Question: Is hope stronger than other motives in moving people and nations?
- Concrete plan: define hope as the belief that effort can improve the future; show it at three levels (individual, society, nation); compare with fear and necessity; admit that false hope can paralyse; conclude that hope tied to action is the strongest motive.

### 2.4 Three traps

| Trap | Example | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Half-topic** | Topic: "water crisis and national unity". Essay covers only the water crisis | Give each half its share. The word "and" means the link between the two is the real subject |
| **Neighbour topic** | Topic: "digital democracy". Essay written: "social media: pros and cons" | Check the rewritten question before the outline and again after it |
| **Ignoring the direction** | Topic says gender equality is a "popular slogan". Essay simply describes women's problems | The topic claims equality is talked about more than practised. Agree or disagree with that claim |

## 3. The thesis statement

The thesis is your answer to the topic in one or two sentences. Everything else in the essay exists to prove it.

### 3.1 What a good thesis has

1. **A position** someone could disagree with.
2. **Reasons**, usually two to four, which become the sections of the body.
3. **The topic's own key words**, so that relevance is obvious.
4. Often a **concession** ("although…"), which shows balance.

### 3.2 Two formulas

- **Although** [the opposing view or complication], [your position], **because** [reason 1], [reason 2] and [reason 3].
- [Subject] is [your judgement]; [the condition or way forward that follows].

### 3.3 Before and after

**Topic:** IMF bailouts: stability or disaster.

- Weak: *The IMF is an international organisation that gives loans to countries, and it has both advantages and disadvantages.* (A fact plus "both sides". No position.)
- Weak: *In this essay I will discuss whether IMF bailouts are good or bad.* (An announcement.)
- Strong: *Although IMF programmes have repeatedly saved Pakistan from default, they have not produced lasting stability, because each rescue treats the shortage of dollars while the causes of that shortage (a narrow tax base, weak exports and loss-making state enterprises) are left in place; the bailout is therefore neither cure nor poison but a painkiller.*

**Topic:** social media has destroyed real-life communication.

- Weak: *Social media is very common nowadays and has many effects on society.*
- Strong: *Social media has not destroyed real conversation, but it has thinned it: people now talk to more people, more often, and with less attention, and the cost is paid in families, friendships and public debate.*

**Topic:** hope is the greatest driving force.

- Weak: *Hope is very important in the life of every human being.*
- Strong: *Fear can push people and need can pull them, but only hope makes them build; from the patient in a hospital ward to a nation rebuilding after a flood, it is the belief that effort will be rewarded that keeps human beings working when every present fact argues against it.*

### 3.4 Where it goes

- At the top of the outline, under the title, labelled "Thesis statement".
- As the last one or two sentences of the introduction.
- Reworded (not copied) in the first sentence of the conclusion.

### 3.5 Thesis test

Ask three questions. If any answer is no, rewrite.

1. Could a sensible person argue the opposite?
2. Does it use the topic's key words?
3. Can I list the body sections just by reading it?

## 4. The outline

### 4.1 What examiners have said

The CE-2016 report criticised outlines that were not properly structured and outlines whose points never appeared in the essay. So an outline has two jobs: to show a logical plan, and to be a promise the essay keeps.

### 4.2 Format

There is no official FPSC outline format. The convention that candidates and teachers follow, and which recent papers support by asking you to "make an outline", is this:

- Heading: **Outline**.
- The topic written out.
- **Thesis statement** in one or two sentences.
- Numbered sections (1, 2, 3 or I, II, III), each with a heading that is a **claim**, not a label.
- Sub-points (a, b, c or 1.1, 1.2) under each, each a short phrase or sentence. Add the key example in brackets if it helps.
- 20–30 points in all, on 1.5 to 2 pages.
- The last sections are always: counter-argument and reply, way forward (if the topic is a problem), conclusion.

Use one numbering style and one grammatical form throughout. If point (a) is a sentence, make (b) a sentence too.

### 4.3 Labels versus claims

| Label (weak) | Claim (strong) |
|---|---|
| Political causes | Frequent dismissal of elected governments has kept institutions weak |
| Economic impacts | Floods wipe out in weeks what growth adds in years |
| Role of media | Ratings-driven television rewards outrage over information |
| Education | A graduate who cannot write a clear page is not educated |

A label tells the examiner you have a category. A claim tells them you have a point.

### 4.4 Three standard skeletons

**Skeleton 1: Problem topic** (governance, water, education, population…)

1. Introduction and thesis
2. The present picture (facts that show the problem is real)
3. Causes (3–5, grouped)
4. Consequences (3–4)
5. What has been tried and why it fell short
6. Counter-view and reply
7. Way forward (4–6 specific measures)
8. Conclusion

**Skeleton 2: Argumentative topic** (a claim, a question, X or Y)

1. Introduction and thesis
2. What the statement means; key terms
3. Arguments for your position (5–7, grouped into two or three sections)
4. The opposing case, stated fairly (2–3 points)
5. Why the opposing case does not hold, or holds only in part
6. Implications: what follows if you are right
7. Conclusion

**Skeleton 3: Abstract / quotation topic**

1. Introduction: explain the statement in plain words; thesis
2. Interpretation: what the key words mean; what the statement does not mean
3. The statement tested at three levels: individual life, society, nations/history
4. Evidence from different fields (history, science, literature, religion, current affairs)
5. The limits of the statement: when it fails or is misused
6. Reply to the limits; refined position
7. Relevance today, including Pakistan
8. Conclusion

### 4.5 Before and after

**Topic:** youth bulge: demographic dividend or demographic bomb (2026, paraphrased).

Before (typical weak outline):

> 1. Introduction
> 2. What is youth bulge
> 3. Advantages
> 4. Disadvantages
> 5. Problems of youth: a. education b. unemployment c. drugs
> 6. Suggestions
> 7. Conclusion

Problems: no thesis, labels only, no answer to "dividend or bomb", could be written for any country.

After:

> **Thesis:** A young population is neither a gift nor a threat in itself; Pakistan's youth bulge will become a dividend only if the state gives young people schooling, skills and jobs within the next two decades, and on present trends it is drifting towards the bomb.
>
> 1. What the terms mean
>    a. Youth bulge: a population in which the young far outnumber the old (median age about 20)
>    b. Dividend: growth that comes when workers outnumber dependants, if they are employed
> 2. Why the dividend is possible
>    a. A large workforce while East Asia and Europe age
>    b. A digital generation: freelancing and IT exports are growing
>    c. Remittances show what Pakistani labour earns when it finds opportunity
> 3. Why the bomb is more likely on present trends
>    a. Millions of children are out of school
>    b. Those in school learn little: degrees without skills
>    c. The economy grows too slowly to absorb new workers each year
>    d. Young women are largely outside the workforce
>    e. Idle youth are raw material for crime, extremism and street politics
> 4. What other countries show
>    a. East Asia turned its bulge into growth through schooling and export jobs
>    b. The Arab uprisings showed what educated but jobless youth can do
> 5. The opposing view: population itself is the problem
>    a. Reply: numbers cannot be undone; the quality of people can be changed
> 6. Turning the bulge into a dividend
>    a. Every child in school; teach reading and maths first
>    b. Technical training tied to real employers
>    c. Jobs through exports, IT and agriculture processing
>    d. Women's work: transport, safety, childcare
>    e. Slower population growth through family planning and girls' education
>    f. Youth in local government
> 7. Conclusion: the window is open but closing

Notice: the thesis answers the question; each heading is a claim; section 3 is longer than section 2 because the thesis leans that way; there is a counter-view; the remedies are specific.

## 5. The introduction

### 5.1 Job and size

The introduction must do three things: get the reader's attention, show that you understand the topic, and state the thesis. Length: **250–350 words** in one or two paragraphs. Longer introductions eat body time.

### 5.2 The four-part pattern

1. **Hook** (1–3 sentences): a fact, a contrast, a short scene, a question, or a quotation.
2. **Bridge** (2–4 sentences): from the hook to the topic; define the key terms.
3. **The debate** (2–3 sentences): what people disagree about; why it matters now.
4. **Thesis** (1–2 sentences), followed if you like by one sentence that maps the essay.

### 5.3 Five hooks

| Hook | Example (topic: climate change and Pakistan) |
|---|---|
| Striking fact | In the summer of 2022, floods affected about 33 million Pakistanis, in a country that produces less than one per cent of the world's greenhouse gases. |
| Contrast | Pakistan did almost nothing to cause the warming of the planet, and it is among the countries paying most for it. |
| Short scene | A farmer in Sindh stood on a road in September 2022 and looked at a lake where his cotton had been. |
| Question | What does a country owe its citizens when the danger they face was made elsewhere? |
| Quotation | The UN Secretary-General has warned that the world is on a "highway to climate hell". |

Avoid: dictionary definitions ("According to the Oxford Dictionary…"), "since the dawn of time", "in today's modern world", and any opening that would fit a hundred other topics.

### 5.4 Before and after

**Topic:** corruption is the root of Pakistan's governance crisis.

Before:

> Corruption is a very big problem of Pakistan. It is found in every department. Since the creation of Pakistan, corruption is increasing day by day. It has destroyed our institutions and economy. Every government promised to end corruption but failed. In this essay, I will discuss the causes, effects and solutions of corruption in Pakistan.

Problems: generalities, no evidence, a tense error ("is increasing" for "has been increasing"), no definition, no thesis, an announcement at the end.

After:

> On 11 August 1947, three days before independence, Muhammad Ali Jinnah told the Constituent Assembly that bribery and corruption were a poison that had to be put down with an iron hand. Almost eight decades later, Pakistan scores 28 out of 100 on Transparency International's Corruption Perceptions Index for 2025 and stands 136th among 182 countries. The warning was heard; it was not obeyed.
>
> Corruption, the use of public office for private gain, is often described as one item on the long list of Pakistan's troubles, next to poverty, weak schools and a narrow tax base. That description is too kind. When a posting can be bought, a tax bill negotiated and a building code ignored for a fee, every other public policy is quietly cancelled at the point of delivery. Some argue that corruption is only a symptom of deeper failures, such as low salaries and political instability, and there is truth in this. Yet a symptom that feeds its own causes becomes the disease. This essay argues that corruption lies at the root of Pakistan's governance crisis because it weakens the state's revenue, distorts its decisions and destroys public trust, and that it will yield only to institutional reform, not to slogans or selective accountability.

What changed: a specific hook, a verified figure, a definition in passing, the opposing view acknowledged, and a thesis with three reasons and a direction for the remedy.

## 6. Body paragraphs

### 6.1 The formula: Point, Explanation, Evidence, Link

| Part | What it does | Length |
|---|---|---|
| **Point** (topic sentence) | States the claim of this paragraph. It should match one outline point | 1 sentence |
| **Explanation** | Says why or how the claim is true | 2–3 sentences |
| **Evidence** | A fact, figure, example, comparison or authority, with year or source | 1–3 sentences |
| **Link** | Ties the paragraph back to the thesis or forward to the next point | 1 sentence |

Length: 120–170 words. One paragraph, one idea. If a paragraph passes 200 words, it probably holds two ideas: split it.

### 6.2 Before and after

**Outline point:** education spending is too low to fix the system.

Before:

> Education is very important for the development of any country. Pakistan is not spending on education. The budget of education is very low. There are many ghost schools and teachers are absent. Many children are out of school. Government should increase the budget of education. Without education no nation can progress.

Problems: seven sentences, seven loosely connected statements, no figure, no explanation, advice dropped into the middle of analysis.

After:

> The first reason the school system fails is simple: the state does not pay for it. Successive governments have promised to raise education spending to four per cent of national income. According to the Economic Survey 2025-26, public spending on education fell to about 0.8 per cent of GDP in 2024-25, less than it was two years earlier. Money alone does not teach a child to read, but its absence guarantees that she will not learn: it means classrooms without electricity, one teacher for five grades, and no one to inspect whether that teacher comes to work. The same survey reports that only 59 per cent of primary schools have electricity. A country that spends less than one rupee in a hundred on its schools has already decided what kind of schools it will have.

What changed: a topic sentence that states the claim; an explanation of the mechanism; two sourced figures; a closing sentence that drives the point home.

### 6.3 Types of evidence

Use a mix. An essay built only on statistics reads like a report; one built only on opinion reads like a speech.

- **Statistics** with year and source (see `05-Evidence-Bank.md`).
- **Examples**: a named event, law, policy or country.
- **Comparisons**: Pakistan and Bangladesh, Pakistan in 1990 and now.
- **Authority**: a report, a court judgment, a recognised scholar.
- **History**: a short reference, not a narrated chapter.
- **Reasoning**: if X, then Y; a cause-and-effect chain explained step by step.

Two rules. First, never invent a number. If you do not remember it, write "roughly a third" or "millions", or leave it out. Second, every fact needs a sentence of explanation after it. A fact you do not explain is decoration.

### 6.4 Grouping paragraphs into sections

Do not write headings in the body unless you are confident your examiner accepts them; opinion among teachers is divided, and a continuous essay with clear topic sentences is safe everywhere. What matters is that the order of paragraphs follows the outline exactly. Start each new section with a sentence that names it: "The causes of this failure are political before they are economic."

## 7. Counter-arguments

### 7.1 Why include them

The CE-2016 report criticised candidates who could not argue from more than one angle. A paragraph that states the other side fairly and then answers it is the quickest way to show critical thinking.

### 7.2 Where

Usually after your main arguments and before the way forward or conclusion. One or two paragraphs. On strongly two-sided topics ("X or Y"), you may give the other side a full section.

### 7.3 The three moves

1. **State** the opposing view in its strongest form. ("Critics argue, with reason, that…")
2. **Concede** what is true in it. ("It is true that…")
3. **Reply**: show why it does not overturn your thesis. ("Yet this overlooks… / This argument proves less than it claims…")

### 7.4 Example

Thesis: local government is the missing link in Pakistan's governance.

> It may be objected that local governments in Pakistan have a poor record of their own. Critics point out that military rulers, from Ayub Khan to Pervez Musharraf, built local bodies in order to bypass national politicians, and that union councils have often been captured by the same local elites who dominate everything else. The objection has weight: a badly designed local system can simply move patronage closer to the voter. But it is an argument about design, not about principle. The answer to captured councils is regular elections, guaranteed funds and an audit that works, not the absence of councils. A province of over a hundred million people run from a single secretariat is not protected from elite capture; it merely places the elite further from those who might hold them to account.

### 7.5 Mistakes

- A "straw man": making the other view sound foolish.
- Giving the other side so much space that the essay seems to change its mind.
- Raising an objection and never answering it.

## 8. The conclusion

### 8.1 Job and size

Close the argument; do not open a new one. **200–250 words**, one or two paragraphs.

### 8.2 Pattern

1. **Answer again**: restate the thesis in new words, now as something proved.
2. **Gather**: the two or three strongest reasons, in a sentence or two.
3. **Look ahead**: what must happen, or what is at stake.
4. **Final line**: a short, plain sentence that the reader will remember. A return to the image or fact used in the introduction works well.

### 8.3 Before and after

Before:

> In a nutshell, corruption is a curse and we should eliminate it. Government should take steps to control corruption. If we remove corruption, Pakistan will become a developed country. May Allah bless our country.

After:

> Corruption, then, is not one of Pakistan's problems; it is the reason so many of the others stay unsolved. It drains the revenue the state needs, bends the decisions the state takes and wears away the trust on which every state depends. None of this is fate. Countries that were once as badly ranked as Pakistan have cleaned their public offices within a generation, and they did it without miracles: by paying officials properly, by removing discretion through technology, by protecting those who report wrongdoing, and by applying one law to friend and foe. Accountability that changes its target with every government is not accountability, and the public has learned to tell the difference. Jinnah asked for an iron hand in 1947. What the task needs now is something harder to supply: a steady one.

### 8.4 Avoid

"In a nutshell", "to cut a long story short", "last but not the least", new facts, apologies, prayers, and slogans.

## 9. Coherence

Coherence means the reader never has to ask "why am I being told this now?" It comes from four tools.

1. **Order.** Follow the outline. General before specific; cause before effect; problem before solution.
2. **Signposts.** One sentence at each turn of the essay: "Having seen why the crisis arose, we can ask what it costs."
3. **Linking words**, used lightly and accurately (full list in `06-Language-Toolkit.md`). "However" must introduce a real contrast; "moreover" a real addition.
4. **Echo.** Repeat the topic's key words through the essay. If the topic says "missing link", the phrase "missing link" or "link" should appear in the introduction, in two or three topic sentences and in the conclusion. This is the simplest proof of relevance.

**The discourse instruction.** The paper asks for exposition, argumentation, description and narration. You do not need separate sections for each. In practice:

| Form | Where it naturally appears |
|---|---|
| Exposition (explaining) | Definitions, causes, how a system works |
| Argumentation (proving) | Thesis, topic sentences, counter-argument |
| Description (showing) | A hook, a picture of a flooded village or an overcrowded classroom |
| Narration (telling) | A short historical account or a brief case, three or four sentences |

One descriptive or narrative passage in the introduction and one or two short ones in the body are enough.

## 10. Length, handwriting and presentation

### 10.1 Length

| Part | Words | Share |
|---|---|---|
| Outline | 250–350 | — |
| Introduction | 250–350 | about 12% |
| Body | 1,900–2,300 | about 78% |
| Conclusion | 200–250 | about 10% |
| **Essay total** | **2,500–3,000** | |

- The CE-2019 report complained about essays that did not reach 1,200–1,300 words. Shortness is read as thin knowledge.
- Do not go far beyond 3,000. Extra pages bring extra errors.
- Coaching sites disagree on whether the outline counts towards the word total. FPSC has not said. Write the essay itself to at least 2,500 if your speed allows.
- Count your words per page once, on FPSC-style lined paper, so that in the hall you count pages, not words. Many people write 180–230 words a page; at 200 a page, 2,600 words is 13 pages.

### 10.2 Handwriting

You type for a living, so treat handwriting as a skill to rebuild.

- From February 2027, write by hand for 20 minutes, three times a week. From May, all essays by hand.
- Use the pen you will use in the exam (a smooth gel or ballpoint, blue or black) and buy several.
- Aim for legible, not beautiful: letters of even size, clear gaps between words.
- Measure speed monthly: words written in 20 minutes ÷ 20. Target 22 words a minute or more, sustained.

### 10.3 Presentation

- Leave a margin; write the topic exactly as printed, with its number.
- Outline on the first pages, then start the essay on a fresh page with the title.
- Indent each paragraph or leave a line between paragraphs. Do one or the other, consistently.
- No coloured pens, decorative headings or underlining of whole sentences. Underlining a few key terms is acceptable but not needed.
- Corrections: a single line through the word.
- Write on both sides if the answer book expects it; number continuation sheets.
- Follow the instructions printed on the answer book cover on the day. They override anything here.

## 11. Common language errors

Full list with fixes in `06-Language-Toolkit.md`. The ones examiners named are:

| Error | Wrong | Right |
|---|---|---|
| Subject–verb agreement | The number of out-of-school children are rising | The number of out-of-school children **is** rising |
| Tense | Since 1947 Pakistan faces many crises | Since 1947 Pakistan **has faced** many crises |
| Verb form | The government did not took action | The government did not **take** action |
| Sentence fragment | Because the institutions are weak. | Policies fail because the institutions are weak. |
| Run-on sentence | Inflation rose people protested the government fell | Inflation rose, people protested and the government fell. |
| Article | Pakistan is facing energy crisis | Pakistan is facing **an** energy crisis |
| Wrong word | Floods effected millions | Floods **affected** millions |

Style rules that protect you:

1. Average sentence length 15–20 words. One long sentence, then a short one.
2. Prefer the plain word: *use* not *utilise*, *help* not *facilitate*, *about* not *with regard to*.
3. No slang, no contractions (write *do not*), no "etc.", no rhetorical questions in a row.
4. Avoid "we" for Pakistan in analysis ("our country", "we Pakistanis") except in a deliberate closing appeal. Write "Pakistan" and "the state".
5. Do not use a word you learned last week. A wrong grand word costs more than a right simple one.
6. Stay moderate. No abuse of any institution, sect, party or country. The examiner is a serving or retired academic or official; a balanced tone is read as maturity.

## 12. A complete worked example: from topic to first page

**Topic (written for this pack):** "A nation's future is written in its classrooms."

**Step 1: Decode.**
- Key terms: *nation's future*, *written*, *classrooms*.
- Tension: schooling versus the other things that shape a nation (resources, leaders, geography).
- Question: Does the quality of a country's schooling decide its future more than anything else?
- Type: abstract statement about education; needs argument plus Pakistan evidence.

**Step 2: Brainstorm (grid).**
- Economic: skills, productivity, East Asia; remittances of unskilled versus skilled workers.
- Political: informed voters; democracy and literacy.
- Social: tolerance, mobility, women's status, child health.
- Pakistan: 26 million out of school; literacy 63%; spending 0.8% of GDP; learning poverty; three parallel systems.
- Counter: resources and leadership matter too; schooling without jobs breeds frustration; classrooms can also teach hatred.
- Way forward: spending, teachers, early reading, girls, technical education.

**Step 3: Thesis.**
> Armies, minerals and geography give a nation its opportunities, but its classrooms decide whether it can use them; a country that neglects its schools, as Pakistan has done, is not saving money but spending its future.

**Step 4: Outline (short version).**

> 1. What the statement claims
>    a. "Classroom" stands for the whole system by which a society forms its young
>    b. The claim: human capability, not natural wealth, decides national outcomes
> 2. Classrooms build the economy
>    a. Skills raise productivity and wages
>    b. East Asian states invested in schooling before they grew rich
>    c. An unskilled workforce exports labour; a skilled one exports products and services
> 3. Classrooms build the state
>    a. Literate citizens can question, vote on issues and resist manipulation
>    b. Competent officials, judges and teachers come from good schools
> 4. Classrooms build society
>    a. Girls' schooling lowers child deaths and family size
>    b. A shared classroom teaches people to live with difference
>    c. Education is the main ladder out of inherited poverty
> 5. Pakistan's classrooms and Pakistan's present
>    a. About 26 million children out of school
>    b. Literacy 63%; women 54%
>    c. Spending near 0.8% of GDP
>    d. Three systems (elite private, public, madrassa) produce three kinds of citizen
>    e. Results: low exports, weak institutions, easy prey for extremism
> 6. Objections
>    a. Resources, leadership and stability also write the future
>    b. Schooling without jobs produces angry graduates
>    c. Reply: each of these depends on capable people; schooling is the condition, not the whole

**Step 5: The introduction.**

> When Japan surrendered in 1945, its cities were ash and its industry was scrap. It had almost no oil, little iron and not enough farmland to feed its people. Within twenty-five years it had the second-largest economy outside the communist bloc. Many things explain that recovery, but one of them is older than the war itself: Japan had made elementary schooling compulsory in the 1870s, and the bombs that destroyed its factories did not destroy what its people knew.
>
> The statement that a nation's future is written in its classrooms makes a large claim. It says that what decides the fate of a country is not chiefly what lies under its soil or who sits in its capital, but what happens between a teacher and a child on an ordinary morning. The claim can be exaggerated. Leadership, peace and plain luck also matter, and a classroom can teach prejudice as easily as physics. Even so, the evidence of the last century points in one direction. Armies, minerals and geography give a nation its opportunities, but its classrooms decide whether it can use them; a country that neglects its schools, as Pakistan has done, is not saving money but spending its future.
