## 1. Connectors and transitions

A connector must state a real logical relation. Use one only when the relation exists. Two or three per paragraph is enough; a paragraph where every sentence starts with a connector reads like a list.

| Relation | Connectors | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Adding | moreover, furthermore, in addition, also, besides, equally | Tariffs are high. **Moreover**, supply is unreliable. |
| Contrast | however, yet, but, on the other hand, by contrast, whereas, while | Enrolment has risen. Learning, **however**, has not. |
| Concession | although, even though, admittedly, it is true that, granted, despite | **Although** exports grew, imports grew faster. |
| Cause | because, since, as, owing to, on account of | Yields are low **because** seed is poor. |
| Result | therefore, thus, hence, consequently, as a result, so | Revenue is short; the state **therefore** borrows. |
| Example | for example, for instance, such as, a case in point is, consider | **Consider** the floods of 2022. |
| Emphasis | indeed, in fact, above all, most importantly | **Indeed**, the cost is rising every year. |
| Sequence | first, secondly, thirdly, next, then, finally, lastly | **First**, the tax base must widen. |
| Time | meanwhile, since then, at the same time, in the 1990s, by 2030 | **Since then**, three plans have been written. |
| Condition | if, unless, provided that, as long as, otherwise | Reform will fail **unless** it is fair. |
| Comparison | similarly, likewise, in the same way, just as | **Just as** printing changed learning, AI will change work. |
| Clarifying | in other words, that is, put simply | **Put simply**, the state spends more than it earns. |
| Summing up | in short, in sum, to conclude, taken together, on balance | **Taken together**, these facts point one way. |

**Rules**

1. "However", "moreover", "therefore" and "consequently" join ideas, not clauses. Put a full stop or semicolon before them: *Prices rose; however, wages did not.* Not: *Prices rose, however wages did not.*
2. Do not use both halves of a pair: *Although… but* is wrong. Write *Although prices rose, wages did not.*
3. "On the other hand" needs a real other side. "On the contrary" denies what was just said; it does not mean "in contrast".
4. Write "first, secondly, thirdly" or "first, second, third". Avoid "firstly… second".
5. "Besides" is informal at the start of a sentence. "Last but not the least" is a cliché: write "finally".

**Signpost sentences** (one at each turn of the essay)

- Having seen how the problem arose, we can ask what it costs.
- The causes are political before they are economic.
- This is only half of the picture.
- The objection deserves a serious answer.
- If this diagnosis is correct, the remedy follows.
- Three measures stand out.
- What is true of the individual is true of the nation.

## 2. Sentence patterns

Good prose mixes short and long sentences. Learn these twelve patterns and you will never be stuck for a way to say something. Each example is on a different theme so you can see the pattern, not the content.

| # | Pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Simple and short**, for emphasis | The warning was heard. It was not obeyed. |
| 2 | **Concession + claim** (Although X, Y) | Although the IMF has saved Pakistan from default, it has not saved it from itself. |
| 3 | **Not X but Y** | The crisis is one not of resources but of management. |
| 4 | **Cause chain** | Because revenue is low, the state borrows; because it borrows, interest consumes the budget. |
| 5 | **Parallel list of three** | It drains the revenue, bends the decisions and destroys the trust of the state. |
| 6 | **The more… the more** | The longer reform is postponed, the more painful it becomes. |
| 7 | **Conditional** | If girls stay in school to sixteen, they marry later, earn more and raise healthier children. |
| 8 | **Appositive** (a phrase that renames) | Karachi, a city of more than twenty million, has no single authority responsible for its water. |
| 9 | **Colon to explain** | The lesson is simple: a road without goods is only a road. |
| 10 | **Semicolon to balance** | Force removes the fighter; it leaves the idea. |
| 11 | **Question and answer** (once or twice an essay) | Why has the tier never been built? The answer lies in history. |
| 12 | **Participle opening** | Faced with a falling rupee, the government raised interest rates. |

**Patterns for evidence**

- According to the Pakistan Economic Survey 2025-26, …
- The 2023 census recorded…
- Transparency International's index for 2025 places Pakistan…
- A World Bank assessment estimated that…
- The figures tell the same story: …
- This is not an isolated case.

**Patterns for analysis after a fact**

- This matters because…
- The figure conceals as much as it reveals.
- The implication is clear: …
- What this shows is that…
- Behind the number lies a simple mechanism.

**Patterns for counter-argument**

- It may be objected that…
- Critics argue, with some reason, that…
- The objection has weight. But it proves less than it claims.
- This is true as far as it goes.
- That is an argument for better design, not for abolition.

**Patterns for cautious claims** (hedging)

- is likely to, tends to, in most cases, on present trends, the evidence suggests, to a large extent
- Use them where you cannot be certain. Do not hedge your thesis.

## 3. Paragraph openers and closers

**Topic sentences that state a claim**

- The first cause is that the state does not collect enough revenue.
- Polarisation also carries an economic price.
- Nowhere is this failure clearer than in the cities.
- The same pattern appears in education.
- History supports this reading.

**Closing sentences that link back**

- A state in this position is always one bad year from a crisis.
- That is why the remedy cannot be merely financial.
- Until this changes, every other reform will leak.
- The point bears directly on the question before us.

**Opening a conclusion** (in place of "in a nutshell")

- To return to the question: …
- The argument of this essay can be stated briefly.
- What, then, is the answer?
- [The key term], then, is not… but…

## 5. Common errors of Pakistani candidates and their fixes

FPSC examiners have named subject–verb agreement, tense, verb forms, sentence structure and spelling. The list below covers these and the Urdu-influenced habits that most often appear in scripts.

### 5.1 Grammar

| # | Error type | Wrong | Right |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Subject–verb agreement with "of" phrases | The quality of our institutions are poor. | The quality of our institutions **is** poor. |
| 2 | "One of the" + singular | One of the main reason is poverty. | One of the main **reasons** is poverty. |
| 3 | Uncountable nouns made plural | informations, advices, equipments, furnitures, evidences, researches, infrastructures | information, advice, equipment, furniture, evidence, research, infrastructure |
| 4 | "Each/every" + plural | Every citizens have a duty. | Every **citizen has** a duty. |
| 5 | Present perfect with "since/for" | Pakistan is facing this problem since 1947. | Pakistan **has faced** this problem since 1947. |
| 6 | Past time with present perfect | The government has announced the policy in 2018. | The government **announced** the policy in 2018. |
| 7 | Continuous for general truths | Corruption is destroying institutions. People are thinking that… | Corruption **destroys** institutions. People **think** that… |
| 8 | Verb after "did" | They did not went. | They did not **go**. |
| 9 | Verb after modal | It should be remain. / It can be happen. | It should **remain**. / It can **happen**. |
| 10 | Missing article | Pakistan is agricultural country. Government should act. | Pakistan is **an** agricultural country. **The** government should act. |
| 11 | Extra article | The corruption is the major problem of the Pakistan. | Corruption is a major problem in Pakistan. |
| 12 | Conditionals | If government will act, problem will be solved. | If the government **acts**, the problem will be solved. |
| 13 | Double comparative / superlative | more better; most strongest | better; strongest |
| 14 | Double conjunction | Although he worked hard but he failed. Because of this reason that… | Although he worked hard, he failed. For this reason… |
| 15 | Passive misuse | The floods were happened in 2022. | The floods **happened** in 2022. |
| 16 | Dangling modifier | Being a poor country, the IMF dictates terms. | Because Pakistan is poor, the IMF dictates terms. |
| 17 | Pronoun without a clear noun | The state taxes the poor because they are weak. (Who?) | The state taxes the poor because **the poor** cannot resist. |
| 18 | Run-on / comma splice | Exports fell, the rupee weakened, reserves declined. | Exports fell, the rupee weakened **and** reserves declined. |
| 19 | Fragment | Which is why reform failed. | **That** is why reform failed. |
| 20 | Indirect question order | We must ask why is the system failing. | We must ask why the system **is** failing. |

### 5.2 Prepositions

| Wrong | Right |
|---|---|
| discuss about the issue | discuss the issue |
| emphasise on, stress on (verb) | emphasise; stress (but: put emphasis **on**) |
| comprise of | comprise; consist of; be composed of |
| according to me | in my view (better: state the claim without it) |
| cope up with | cope with |
| despite of | despite; in spite of |
| reason of | reason for |
| result into | result in |
| deprived from | deprived of |
| investigate about; order for; request for (verb) | investigate; order; request |
| superior than | superior to |
| at the cost of (for "because of") | at the cost of = losing something; do not use for "because of" |
| since two years | for two years; since 2024 |
| married with | married to |
| angry on | angry with / at |

### 5.3 Word choice

| Wrong or weak | Better |
|---|---|
| affect / effect confused | *affect* is the verb; *effect* is the noun |
| loose (for lose) | *lose* an election; a *loose* rope |
| economic / economical | *economic* policy; an *economical* car |
| historic / historical | a *historic* victory (important); *historical* records (of the past) |
| principal / principle | the *principal* cause; a moral *principle* |
| its / it's | *its* = belonging to it; do not write *it's* in a formal essay |
| amount of people | number of people (*amount* for uncountable things) |
| less people | fewer people |
| literally (as emphasis) | cut it |
| cent per cent | one hundred per cent; entirely |
| masses; common man; layman | the public; ordinary citizens |
| menace; curse; evil (overused) | problem; threat; harm |
| burning issue; need of the hour; crying need | an urgent problem; an urgent need |
| backbone of the economy | the largest sector; the main source of |
| prepone; do the needful; pass out (graduate); timely (adverb) | bring forward; take the necessary steps; graduate; in time, on time |
| today's modern era; since time immemorial; from the dawn of civilisation | now; for centuries (or cut) |
| play a vital/pivotal role (in every paragraph) | say what the thing does: *schools shape citizens* |

### 5.4 Style

| Problem | Example | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Over-long sentence | Sixty words with four "and"s and three "which"es | Break it into three sentences |
| Bombast | "The cataclysmic conflagration of corruption has engulfed the edifice of our polity." | "Corruption has weakened every institution." |
| Announcing | "In this essay I am going to discuss…" | State the thesis |
| Sermonising | "We should all be ashamed. We must wake up." | Give a cause and a remedy |
| Unsupported absolutes | "Everyone knows…", "All politicians are corrupt." | "Many…", "Surveys suggest…", or give evidence |
| Repetition of the same point in new words | Paragraph 9 restates paragraph 4 | Test: write each paragraph's point in six words; no two should match |
| Mixed registers | "Kids", "a lot of", "stuff", "gonna" | "Children", "many", "matters"; no contractions |
| Too many quotations | A quotation in every paragraph | Two or three in the essay; your argument should carry it |
| Rhetorical questions in a row | "Who is responsible? Who will answer? When will we learn?" | One question, then answer it |
| Capital letters for emphasis | "The REAL problem is…" | Let the sentence carry the emphasis |

### 5.5 Punctuation and mechanics

- **Apostrophes:** *the country's debt* (one country), *the provinces' share* (several provinces). No apostrophe in plurals: *1990s*, *NGOs*.
- **Commas:** after an opening clause (*When the rains came, the drains failed.*); around extra information (*Karachi, the largest city, …*). No comma between subject and verb.
- **Semicolon:** joins two complete sentences that are closely linked. If either side cannot stand alone, use a comma.
- **Colon:** introduces an explanation or a list after a complete sentence.
- **Quotation marks:** only around words you are quoting exactly.
- **Numbers:** words for one to ten, figures above; always figures with units and percentages (*3.7 per cent*, *26 million*). Do not begin a sentence with a figure.
- **Spelling:** choose British spelling and keep it: *programme, labour, defence, organise, centre, colour*. Words often misspelt: *government, environment, parliament, accommodate, occurred, separate, definitely, bureaucracy, privilege, committee, corruption, independent, argument, necessary, sovereignty, existence, receive, achieve, development, tomorrow.*
- **Capitals:** proper names and titles of specific bodies (*the Supreme Court, the Constitution of 1973*). Not for common nouns (*democracy, government* in general, *education*).
- **Abbreviations:** write the full name first: *the International Monetary Fund (IMF)*. No "etc.", "e.g.", "&" or "govt." in the essay.

## 6. Words and phrases to avoid

| Avoid | Because | Write instead |
|---|---|---|
| in a nutshell; to cut a long story short | cliché; informal | in short; to conclude |
| last but not the least | cliché | finally |
| it is a fact that; it goes without saying that; needless to say | empty | cut it |
| in this day and age; in today's world | empty | today; now |
| as we all know; it is crystal clear that | presumes agreement | give the evidence |
| a plethora of; a myriad of; multitude of | inflated | many |
| play havoc with; wreak havoc (repeatedly) | overused | damage; disrupt |
| the whole nation; each and every; one and all | padding | the nation; every |
| very, really, extremely, totally | weak intensifiers | a stronger word or a fact |
| I think; I believe; in my humble opinion | the essay is your opinion | state the claim |
| so-called (as a sneer) | tone | describe the thing neutrally |
| etc.; and so on | vague | finish the list or cut it |

## 7. A ten-minute proofreading routine

Do it in this order. Reading for everything at once catches nothing.

1. **Verbs (3 minutes).** Put your pen on every verb. Does it agree with its subject? Is the tense right for the time?
2. **Articles and plurals (2 minutes).** Check every singular countable noun for *a/an/the*. Check the uncountable list in 5.1.
3. **Sentence ends (2 minutes).** Is every sentence complete? Any sentence over three lines: can it be split?
4. **Names, numbers and spellings (2 minutes).** Dates, figures, names of people and reports.
5. **First and last sentences of each paragraph (1 minute).** Does each first sentence state a point that is in the outline?

Keep an **error log**: a page where you write every mistake you find in your own practice essays, with the correction. Read it before each new essay. Most writers make the same six or seven errors again and again; once you know yours, you can hunt them.
