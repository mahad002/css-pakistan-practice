## 18. Social media: a new public square or a marketplace of lies?

*Type: argumentative. Past relatives: social media prospects and problems (2018), digital democracy (2022), social media and real-life communication (2023 Special).*

**Decode.** Both images are partly true. The essay must decide which describes social media better today and what would tip the balance.

**Thesis.** Social media opened the public square to people who never had a voice, and that gain is real; but platforms built to hold attention reward anger and falsehood over accuracy, so the square is turning into a market where lies sell best, and it can be reclaimed only by rules for platforms and education for users.

**Outline**

1. The scale
   a. More than five billion people use social media worldwide
   b. In Pakistan, about 80 million user identities and 117 million internet users (late 2025)
2. The public square: real gains
   a. Voice for the ordinary citizen, women and minorities
   b. Faster exposure of abuse and misrule; citizen journalism
   c. Political participation: campaigns, fundraising, turnout among the young
   d. Relief in disasters; small businesses and freelancers
   e. Direct contact between rulers and ruled
3. The marketplace of lies: real harms
   a. Algorithms reward what provokes; falsehood travels faster than correction
   b. Echo chambers harden opinion; polarisation deepens
   c. Disinformation in elections; deepfakes and AI-made content
   d. Hate campaigns, blasphemy accusations and mob violence triggered online
   e. Harassment of women; damage to adolescents' mental health
   f. Data harvesting and manipulation (the Cambridge Analytica affair, 2018)
   g. Shorter attention, thinner conversation
4. The Pakistani experience
   a. Parties that built support online; youth mobilisation
   b. State responses: platform bans, throttling, tougher cyber laws
   c. Both rumour and repression have grown
5. The opposing view: the tool is neutral; users are to blame
   a. Reply: design is not neutral; a system that profits from outrage will produce it
6. The other opposing view: ban or strictly control it
   a. Reply: bans silence the honest and are evaded by the determined; they also cost the digital economy
7. Reclaiming the square
   a. Platform duties: transparency of algorithms, quick removal of incitement, labels on AI content
   b. Narrow laws against incitement and defamation, applied by courts
   c. Data protection law
   d. Digital literacy in schools: how to check a claim
   e. Independent fact-checkers; responsible conduct by parties
   f. Age-appropriate limits for children (several countries now set a minimum age)
   g. Individual discipline: think before sharing
8. Conclusion: the square is worth saving, and it will not save itself

**Evidence to carry.** DataReportal Digital 2026 (Pakistan); the Arab uprisings of 2011; Cambridge Analytica (2018); Australia's minimum-age law for social media (in force from December 2025); PECA amendment (2025).

**If the wording changes.** For "destroyed real-life communication", focus on 3(e) and 3(g) and on family and friendship. For "digital democracy", focus on 2(c), 3(c) and section 7.

## 19. In the digital age, a nation's borders run through its servers

*Type: argumentative. Past relatives: readiness for the digital revolution (2020), cyber security as the new frontier (2026).*

**Thesis.** As banking, power grids, identity records and military command move online, an attack on data can do what once required an invasion; Pakistan has built a large digital state faster than it has built its defences, and national security now requires laws, skills and institutions for cyberspace as serious as those for land, sea and air.

**Outline**

1. What has changed
   a. Critical services now run on networks
   b. Attackers need no army, only skill and a connection
   c. Attribution is hard; deterrence is weak
2. What cyber threats look like
   a. Sabotage of infrastructure (Stuxnet, 2010; attacks on Ukraine's grid)
   b. Ransomware that shut hospitals and firms worldwide (WannaCry, 2017)
   c. Espionage and theft of state secrets
   d. Financial crime and bank fraud
   e. Disinformation as a weapon in conflict
3. Pakistan's digital state
   a. A national identity database covering most adults
   b. Mobile banking, digital payments and e-government growing fast
   c. 207 million telecom subscriptions; 5G spectrum auctioned in March 2026
   d. IT exports rising; a large freelance workforce
4. Pakistan's exposure
   a. Reported breaches of public bodies and utilities in recent years
   b. Hostile neighbours; cyber activity during the May 2025 conflict
   c. Dependence on imported hardware and software
   d. Low public awareness; weak passwords, pirated software
   e. Shortage of trained professionals; brain drain
   f. No comprehensive data-protection law in force
5. What exists
   a. Electronic crimes law (2016); National Cyber Security Policy (2021)
   b. A national response team and a new cyber-crime investigation agency
   c. A good rating in the ITU's Global Cybersecurity Index 2024
6. The opposing view: cyber security is a pretext for surveillance and censorship
   a. The risk is real: firewalls and shutdowns hurt rights and business
   b. Reply: security and liberty both need law, oversight and transparency
7. Securing the frontier
   a. Classify and harden critical infrastructure; mandatory standards and audits
   b. Pass and enforce data protection
   c. Train a cyber workforce: universities, scholarships, competitions
   d. Public–private cooperation and information sharing
   e. Indigenous capability in key software and encryption
   f. Citizen awareness campaigns
   g. International cooperation and norms; cyber diplomacy
   h. Independent oversight of state powers
8. Conclusion: a state that cannot defend its data cannot defend its sovereignty

**Evidence to carry.** Economic Survey 2025-26 telecom figures; Stuxnet, WannaCry, Estonia (2007) as cases; PECA 2016; National Cyber Security Policy 2021; ITU GCI 2024.

**If the wording changes.** For "is Pakistan ready for the digital revolution?", widen to connectivity (internet penetration under half the population), skills, payments and regulation.

## 20. Artificial intelligence and the future of work: threat or opportunity for Pakistan?

*Type: argumentative. Past relatives: AI overstepping its bounds (2023 Special), AI and creativity (2024).*

**Thesis.** Artificial intelligence will not abolish work, but it will change which work is valued; for a young, low-skilled country such as Pakistan it is a threat to routine jobs and service exports if nothing is done, and an opportunity to leap ahead in education, health, farming and government if people are trained and rules are written in time.

**Outline**

1. What is new
   a. Machines that generate text, code, images and decisions, not only follow instructions
   b. Speed of adoption: hundreds of millions of weekly users within three years of 2022
2. What history suggests
   a. Earlier technologies destroyed tasks and created new occupations
   b. The gains went to those with skills and capital; the transition hurt those without
3. The threat
   a. The IMF estimates that about 40% of jobs worldwide are exposed to AI
   b. Routine office work, call centres, basic coding and content writing are most exposed, and these are Pakistan's service exports
   c. The traditional ladder of cheap-labour manufacturing may shorten
   d. Inequality: between countries that own the technology and those that only use it
   e. Deepfakes, bias and surveillance
4. The opportunity
   a. A personal tutor for every child; help for overstretched teachers
   b. Diagnosis support where doctors are few
   c. Advice to farmers on weather, pests and prices
   d. Faster courts and tax administration; less discretion
   e. Freelancers and small firms with the tools of large ones
   f. A young population that adopts technology quickly
5. Where Pakistan stands
   a. A National AI Policy approved in 2025
   b. IT exports of about $3.8 billion in the first ten months of FY26
   c. Internet penetration under half the population; weak schooling; power and connectivity gaps
6. The opposing view: AI is hype; Pakistan has more basic problems
   a. Reply: the basics and AI are linked; the technology can help deliver the basics, and ignoring it widens the gap
7. Making it an opportunity
   a. Foundational education first; then digital and AI literacy for all
   b. Reskilling programmes for exposed workers
   c. Affordable internet and devices; reliable power
   d. Public data and computing resources for local researchers; Urdu and regional-language tools
   e. Law on data, bias and accountability; human oversight in public decisions
   f. Support for start-ups; links with universities
   g. Social protection during transition
8. Conclusion: the machine will not take the future; those who learn to use it will

**Evidence to carry.** IMF staff note on AI and jobs (January 2024); WEF Future of Jobs Report 2025 (170 million jobs created and 92 million displaced by 2030); Pakistan's National AI Policy (2025); Economic Survey IT figures; DataReportal internet penetration.

**If the wording changes.** For "AI has overstepped its bounds", centre on 3(e) and 7(e): the limits are for humans to set.
