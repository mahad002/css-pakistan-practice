## 14. Pakistan's education emergency: more schooling, or better learning?

*Type: argumentative. Past relatives: higher education (2018), classrooms decide the future (2019), meaningful education (2021), investment in knowledge (2025).*

**Decode.** The "or" is a false choice that you should expose: Pakistan needs both access and quality, but an essay should say which failure is deeper and why.

**Thesis.** Pakistan faces two education emergencies at once: millions of children never enter a classroom, and millions who do leave it unable to read with understanding; the second is the deeper failure, because schooling without learning wastes the child's years and the nation's money, and both demand that education be treated as an investment, not an expense.

**Outline**

1. The promise
   a. Article 25-A: free and compulsory education for ages 5–16
   b. Education as the base of economy, democracy and social peace
2. The access emergency
   a. About 26 million children out of school, over half of them girls
   b. Literacy 63%; women 54%; wide provincial gaps (Balochistan lowest)
   c. Poverty, distance, child labour, early marriage
3. The learning emergency
   a. Most ten-year-olds cannot read and understand a simple text (World Bank "learning poverty")
   b. Rote memorisation; examinations that test recall
   c. Teachers poorly trained, poorly supervised, politically posted
   d. Schools without electricity, toilets or boundary walls
4. A divided system
   a. Elite private, low-fee private, public and madrassa streams
   b. Medium of instruction decides life chances
   c. Result: classes that do not share a language or a worldview
5. Higher education
   a. Rapid expansion in the number of universities since 2002
   b. Degrees without skills; weak research; graduate unemployment
6. Why it persists
   a. Public spending fell to about 0.8% of GDP in 2024-25
   b. Education is a provincial subject with weak district management
   c. Little political reward for slow, invisible gains
7. The opposing view: fix access first, quality later
   a. Reply: parents withdraw children from schools that teach nothing; quality drives enrolment
8. The way forward
   a. Raise and protect spending; spend on teachers and early grades
   b. Foundational reading and maths by grade three
   c. Merit hiring, training and monitoring of teachers
   d. Stipends and safe transport for girls; second-chance programmes
   e. A common minimum standard across all streams
   f. Technical and vocational tracks linked to employers
   g. Examination reform towards understanding
   h. Use technology where it helps: lessons by phone, data on attendance
9. Conclusion: a school that does not teach is a building; the task is to make it a school

**Evidence to carry.** Economic Survey 2025-26 education figures; Article 25-A (18th Amendment, 2010); World Bank learning-poverty estimate; ASER surveys; HEC founding (2002).

**If the wording changes.** For "purpose of education", argue that education must form a thinking citizen and a capable worker, not a certificate holder. For "online learning", weigh access and discipline against reach.

## 15. No nation can rise while half of it is held back

*Type: argumentative (statement to defend). Past relatives: gender equality as myth or slogan (2016, 2021), same chances as men (2023 Special), frailty no more (2025).*

**Thesis.** The exclusion of women is not only an injustice to them but a cost that Pakistan pays in lost income, poorer children and weaker institutions; laws have improved, yet practice lags, and the country will not rise until women's education, work, safety and voice are treated as national priorities.

**Outline**

1. The claim
   a. A nation's strength is the sum of its people's abilities
   b. Jinnah: no nation can rise to the height of glory unless women stand side by side with men
2. Where Pakistan stands
   a. Global Gender Gap Report 2026: third from the bottom; last in 2025
   b. Economic participation is the weakest area
   c. Female literacy 54% against 73% for men
   d. Only about a quarter of women are in the labour force
3. What exclusion costs
   a. Economy: unused talent; lower household income
   b. Children: a mother's schooling predicts child survival, nutrition and schooling
   c. Population: educated women marry later and have smaller families
   d. Governance: fewer women in decision-making means their needs are missed
4. What holds women back
   a. Custom: early marriage, restrictions on mobility, son preference
   b. Violence and harassment at home, on the street, online
   c. Unequal inheritance in practice despite law and religion
   d. Lack of safe transport, childcare and hostels
   e. Weak enforcement of good laws
5. Progress worth noting
   a. Laws: workplace harassment (2010), honour killing and rape (2016), women's property rights (2020), anti-rape (2021)
   b. Firsts: a woman prime minister (1988), Supreme Court judge (2022), provincial chief minister (2024)
   c. Reserved seats in assemblies; rising university enrolment
   d. Cash transfers paid to women through BISP
6. The opposing view
   a. "Women's rights are a Western agenda"
   b. Reply: Islam gave women property, consent and education rights fourteen centuries ago; the obstacle is custom, not faith
   c. "Laws already exist": yes, and enforcement does not
7. The way forward
   a. Girls in school to matriculation at least: stipends, transport, female teachers
   b. Jobs: quotas honoured, credit for women's businesses, digital work
   c. Safety: police desks, fast trials, safe transport
   d. Enforce inheritance and marriage laws
   e. Women in local government and senior service
   f. Men and boys as part of the change; media that shows capable women
8. Conclusion: equality is not a favour to women; it is the country's unused strength

**Evidence to carry.** WEF Global Gender Gap 2025 and 2026; Economic Survey literacy figures; the laws with years; Jinnah's 1944 remark; UNDP Gender Inequality Index (HDR 2025).

**If the wording changes.** For "frailty…", argue from achievement. For "feminism and our culture", distinguish rights from imported style and show that the core demands are local.

## 16. Extremism is defeated in classrooms, not only on battlefields

*Type: argumentative. Past relatives: terrorism and rights (2018), media and terrorism (2022).*

**Thesis.** Pakistan's soldiers and police have repeatedly cleared territory of militants, yet violence has returned, because force removes the fighter and leaves the idea, the grievance and the sanctuary; lasting security needs schools, justice, jobs and diplomacy working beside the gun.

**Outline**

1. Terms
   a. Terrorism: violence against civilians for political ends
   b. Extremism: the mindset that justifies it
2. What force achieved
   a. Operations after 2014 (Zarb-e-Azb, Radd-ul-Fasaad) sharply reduced attacks
   b. The National Action Plan followed the Army Public School attack of December 2014
3. Why violence returned
   a. Attacks have risen since 2021; the Global Terrorism Index 2025 ranked Pakistan second, with deaths up 45% in 2024
   b. Sanctuaries across the Afghan border after August 2021; clashes and a ceasefire in October 2025
   c. Separatist violence in Balochistan fed by local grievances
   d. The non-military parts of the National Action Plan were left unfinished
4. The roots that force cannot reach
   a. Ideas: sectarian and hate literature; unregulated seminaries and online preaching
   b. Schooling: millions out of school; curricula that do not teach critical thought
   c. Justice: slow courts make rough "justice" attractive
   d. Economy: idle young men in neglected districts
   e. Governance gaps in merged tribal districts and Balochistan
   f. Regional politics and proxy wars
5. Why classrooms matter
   a. Education builds resistance to simple, violent answers
   b. Schools mix children of different sects and classes
   c. Skills give a stake in peace
6. The opposing view: this is a security problem needing a security answer
   a. Force is necessary; no state can negotiate under the gun
   b. Reply: two decades show that force alone must be repeated every few years
7. A complete strategy
   a. Intelligence-led policing; stronger civilian counter-terrorism departments
   b. Finish the National Action Plan: seminary registration, hate-speech law, terror financing
   c. Courts that convict on evidence, within rights
   d. Development and political inclusion in Balochistan and the merged districts
   e. Curriculum and teacher reform; a counter-narrative from credible scholars (Paigham-e-Pakistan)
   f. Firm, patient diplomacy with Kabul
   g. Rights respected: abuses create the next recruit
8. Conclusion: the battlefield buys time; the classroom decides how it is used

**Evidence to carry.** APS attack (16 December 2014); NAP; GTI 2025; FATF grey list exit (October 2022); October 2025 Pakistan–Afghanistan ceasefire; the Jaffar Express hijacking (March 2025).

**If the wording changes.** For "security versus civil liberties", build on 7(c) and 7(g): rights are part of the strategy, not a limit on it.

## 17. A free press is the oxygen of democracy; a reckless one is its poison

*Type: argumentative. Past relatives: mass media and terrorism (2022), propaganda (2024).*

**Thesis.** Democracy cannot work without a press free to report and criticise, and it is damaged by a press that sells outrage and rumour; in Pakistan the press is both too restricted and too careless, and the cure for the second fault is professional standards and public literacy, never the censorship that worsens the first.

**Outline**

1. Why the press matters
   a. It informs voters, exposes wrongdoing and gives the weak a voice
   b. The "fourth estate" watches the other three
2. Freedom under pressure
   a. RSF World Press Freedom Index 2026: Pakistan 153rd of 180
   b. Journalists killed, abducted or charged; channels taken off air
   c. The 2025 amendment to the electronic crimes law made "false information" an offence, with prison terms
   d. Shutdowns and platform bans (X was blocked for over a year from February 2024)
   e. Financial pressure through government advertising
3. Recklessness within
   a. Ratings-driven talk shows; breaking news without checking
   b. Owners with business and political interests
   c. Sensational coverage of terror and crime that serves the attacker's purpose
   d. Trial by media; invasion of privacy
   e. Disinformation and deepfakes on social platforms
4. How the two feed each other
   a. Irresponsible content gives the state its excuse to restrict
   b. Restriction pushes audiences to unverified online sources
5. The opposing view: national security requires control of information
   a. Some secrecy is legitimate, especially in conflict
   b. Reply: broad controls hide failure and corruption, which are the real threats
6. The way forward
   a. Protect journalists: enforce the 2021 protection law; end impunity
   b. Narrow, precise laws on incitement and defamation in place of vague "fake news" offences
   c. Independent regulator, not a government arm
   d. Self-regulation: editors' codes, ombudsmen, fact-check desks
   e. Transparent ownership and advertising
   f. Media literacy in schools
   g. Public-interest broadcasting with real independence
7. Conclusion: freedom and responsibility are not opposites; each makes the other possible

**Evidence to carry.** RSF Index 2025 and 2026; Article 19 and 19-A of the Constitution; PEMRA (2002); PECA (2016, amended 2025); Protection of Journalists and Media Professionals Act (2021).

**If the wording changes.** For "propaganda and the Muslim world", show how images are shaped (terrorism framing, Gaza coverage) and argue for credible media of one's own.
