## 10. Climate justice: those who polluted least are paying most

*Type: argumentative. Past relatives: global warming (2018), fossil-fuel phase-out (2024), reforestation (2025).*

**Decode.** The claim has two parts: a fact (unequal burden) and a moral judgement (injustice). Prove the first, argue the second, then say what justice would require, from the world and from the victims themselves.

**Thesis.** Climate change is an injustice as well as a danger: countries such as Pakistan, which produced a tiny share of the gases that heat the planet, suffer its worst floods and heat; justice demands finance and emission cuts from those responsible, but it does not excuse vulnerable states from putting their own houses in order.

**Outline**

1. The facts of unequal burden
   a. Rich industrial economies produced most historical emissions
   b. Pakistan emits under 1% of the global total
   c. 2022 floods: about 33 million people affected, more than 1,700 dead, roughly $30 billion in damage and losses
   d. 2025 floods: more than 1,000 dead; losses of about Rs 822 billion
   e. Germanwatch ranked Pakistan the most affected country for the year 2022
2. Why this is injustice
   a. Polluter-pays principle
   b. "Common but differentiated responsibilities" (UNFCCC, 1992)
   c. The poor within every country suffer first: farmers, slum dwellers, women, children
   d. Future generations inherit a debt they did not incur
3. What the world has promised
   a. $100 billion a year by 2020: reached late
   b. Loss and Damage Fund agreed at COP27 (2022) and set up at COP28 (2023)
   c. New goal of $300 billion a year by 2035 (COP29, 2024)
   d. COP30 (2025): call to triple adaptation finance by 2035; no agreed fossil-fuel phase-out
4. Why promises fall short
   a. Sums are far below needs; much comes as loans
   b. Major emitters delay; the United States has stepped back from the Paris process
   c. Fossil-fuel interests block firm language
5. The opposing view
   a. Today's large emitters include developing giants; history cannot be undone
   b. Victim states waste aid and build on floodplains
   c. Reply: both points are partly true and neither removes responsibility; justice has an external and an internal side
6. What justice requires of the world
   a. Faster emission cuts by the largest emitters
   b. Grants, not loans; debt relief tied to climate action
   c. Easy access to the Loss and Damage Fund
   d. Technology transfer
7. What justice requires of Pakistan
   a. Adaptation: early warning, drainage, flood-resistant housing, zoning
   b. Protect forests and wetlands; restore the Indus basin
   c. Clean energy at home
   d. Honest, transparent use of climate funds
   e. Local governments able to respond
8. Conclusion: the bill has gone to the wrong address; correcting it is a test of the world's fairness and of our own competence

**Evidence to carry.** PDNA 2022 figures; Economic Survey 2025-26 on the 2025 floods; Germanwatch CRI 2025 and 2026; COP27–COP30 decisions; the ICJ advisory opinion on climate obligations (July 2025).

**If the wording changes.** For "reforestation" or "global warming and remedies", keep sections 1 and 7 and expand mitigation.

## 11. Pakistan's water crisis: scarcity or mismanagement?

*Type: argumentative. Past relatives: water crisis and national unity (2016), the saying on not wasting water (2021).*

**Thesis.** Pakistan's water crisis is real, but it is chiefly a crisis of management: the country stores little, wastes much in its fields and cities, prices water at almost nothing and quarrels over what remains; with India now holding the Indus Waters Treaty in abeyance, managing water well has become a matter of national security.

**Outline**

1. The stakes
   a. An economy and a food system built on one river basin
   b. Water links agriculture, energy, health and inter-provincial peace
2. Evidence of crisis
   a. Per-person availability has fallen from over 5,000 cubic metres at independence to around the scarcity line of 1,000
   b. Storage for roughly 30 days of use
   c. Groundwater falling in Punjab and Balochistan; cities such as Karachi and Quetta short of supply
   d. Floods and droughts in the same decade
3. Natural and external causes
   a. Population has grown about sevenfold since 1947
   b. Climate change: melting glaciers, erratic monsoon
   c. India's upstream projects; the treaty held in abeyance since April 2025
4. Mismanagement: the larger cause
   a. Agriculture uses the great bulk of water, with flood irrigation and unlined canals
   b. Thirsty crops (sugarcane, rice) encouraged in dry zones
   c. Water charges that do not cover even maintenance
   d. Unregulated tube wells
   e. Urban leakage, theft and tanker mafias; untreated sewage
   f. Dams delayed by cost and by disputes between provinces
5. Water and national unity
   a. The 1991 Water Apportionment Accord and distrust over its application
   b. Sindh's fear for the delta; the 2025 dispute over new canals
   c. Shared scarcity needs shared data
6. The opposing view: build big dams and the problem ends
   a. Storage helps
   b. Reply: without saving water in fields and cities, a new dam fills a leaking bucket
7. The way forward
   a. Implement the National Water Policy (2018)
   b. Line canals; high-efficiency irrigation; laser levelling
   c. Price water to reflect scarcity, with protection for the poor
   d. Crop zoning
   e. Build agreed storage, large and small; recharge aquifers
   f. Recycle urban wastewater; fix city networks
   g. Telemetry and open data to end provincial suspicion
   h. Water diplomacy and legal defence of the treaty
   i. Public habit: conservation as a civic and religious duty
8. Conclusion: the river has not failed the country; the country must stop failing the river

**Evidence to carry.** Indus Waters Treaty (1960; World Bank; three eastern and three western rivers); abeyance date; 1991 Accord; National Water Policy 2018; the figures in the Evidence Bank marked [verify].

**If the wording changes.** For "water and national unity", make section 5 the body. For the 2021-style saying, open with the ethic of conservation and treat section 4 as its violation.

## 12. Energy security: the key to Pakistan's economic survival

*Type: expository with a stand. Past relative: fossil-fuel phase-out (2024).*

**Thesis.** Pakistan does not lack generating capacity; it lacks affordable, reliable and home-grown energy, because it built expensive imported-fuel plants, lets a large share of the value leak away in line losses and unpaid bills, and remains exposed to every oil shock; security lies in fixing the system and shifting to domestic clean sources.

**Outline**

1. Why energy is survival
   a. Every factory, farm pump and hospital depends on it
   b. Energy imports are the largest strain on the trade balance
2. The present picture
   a. Installed capacity of about 49,650 MW, well above peak demand
   b. Yet tariffs are among the highest in the region
   c. Circular debt in power and gas running into trillions of rupees
   d. Industry closing or moving off-grid; households turning to rooftop solar
3. How the crisis was built
   a. Shift from hydel to imported oil, gas and coal after the 1990s
   b. Contracts that pay plants for capacity whether or not power is bought
   c. Transmission and distribution losses and theft
   d. Poor recovery of bills; subsidies that are not funded
   e. Declining domestic gas
   f. Weak planning: plants in the south, demand in the north, lines in between inadequate
4. The external shock
   a. The 2026 Gulf war and disruption in the Strait of Hormuz raised fuel prices sharply
   b. Inflation returned to double digits in April 2026
   c. The lesson: imported energy is imported insecurity
5. The quiet revolution
   a. Solar panels imported on a very large scale since 2023; consumers are leading the transition
   b. Risk: the grid is left with fixed costs and fewer paying customers
6. The opposing view: cheap coal and gas first, climate later
   a. Thar coal is local
   b. Reply: sun, wind and water are local too, cheaper over time and free of fuel risk
7. The way forward
   a. Renegotiate or retire expensive contracts
   b. Reform distribution: privatise or professionalise companies; smart meters
   c. Invest in transmission
   d. Scale hydel, solar and wind; storage
   e. A competitive power market
   f. Efficiency: buildings, appliances, industry
   g. Regional energy trade (CASA-1000)
   h. Targeted subsidies for the poor only
8. Conclusion: energy policy is economic policy; get it right and much else follows

**Evidence to carry.** Economic Survey 2025-26 capacity and mix figures; April 2026 inflation (10.9%, PBS); the Prime Minister's statement that the weekly oil bill rose from about $300 million to $800 million; Ember's reporting on solar imports.

**If the wording changes.** For "phasing out fossil fuels", add the global side: COP28's call to transition away from fossil fuels and what it means for oil exporters and importers.

## 13. A large population: Pakistan's greatest asset or its heaviest burden?

*Type: argumentative. Past relatives: youth bulge (2026), brain drain (2025).*

**Thesis.** People are a country's only lasting resource, but only when they are healthy, schooled and employed; Pakistan's population is growing faster than its ability to provide these things, so the asset is turning into a burden, and the answer is to slow the growth and raise the quality at the same time.

**Outline**

1. The numbers
   a. 241.5 million in the 2023 census; about 257 million by mid-2026 (UNFPA)
   b. Growth of 2.55% a year between 2017 and 2023, among the highest in Asia
   c. Fifth most populous country; median age about 20
2. The case for "asset"
   a. A large domestic market and workforce
   b. Young workers while much of the world ages
   c. Overseas workers send more than $38 billion a year
   d. Talent in IT, medicine, engineering
3. The case for "burden" on present trends
   a. About 26 million children out of school
   b. About 40% of young children stunted
   c. Millions enter the job market each year; growth is too slow to absorb them
   d. Pressure on water, land, housing and cities
   e. Every gain in income is divided among more people
   f. Skilled youth leave: hundreds of thousands of registered emigrants a year
4. Why growth stays high
   a. Low use of family planning; weak services in rural areas
   b. Low female education and employment
   c. Early marriage; preference for sons
   d. Reluctance to discuss the subject; mixed messages from pulpits and politicians
   e. Federal funds shared mainly by population, which rewards numbers
5. What others did
   a. Bangladesh: door-to-door health workers and girls' schooling brought fertility down
   b. Iran: clergy support made family planning acceptable
6. The opposing view: development is the best contraceptive
   a. Reply: true, but waiting for development while numbers outrun it is circular; both must move together
7. Turning burden into asset
   a. Family planning in every health facility; Lady Health Workers expanded
   b. Keep girls in school to grade ten at least
   c. Engage religious scholars, as other Muslim countries did
   d. Skills and jobs for the young; women's work
   e. Revise the NFC formula to reward human development
   f. Plan cities
   g. Manage migration: export skills, keep ties with the diaspora
8. Conclusion: numbers are destiny only if nothing is done

**Evidence to carry.** Census 2023; UNFPA 2026 statement; Economic Survey figures on schooling; World Bank on stunting and job entrants; the seventh NFC award's 82% weight for population.

**If the wording changes.** For "brain drain", expand 3(f) and 7(g): people follow opportunity and respect; the cure is at home.
