# Model Outlines 24–31: Abstract, Quotation and Proverb Topics

**The method for every abstract topic** (see `01-Essay-Method.md`, Skeleton 3):

1. Say in plain words what the statement claims.
2. Define its two or three key words.
3. Turn it into a thesis that someone could dispute.
4. Test it at three levels: the individual, society, nations and history.
5. Draw evidence from at least four fields: history, politics, science, literature, religion, daily life, Pakistan.
6. Show where the statement fails or is misused, then answer.
7. End with what it means for us now.

An abstract essay fails when it stays abstract. Every paragraph needs a concrete example.

## 24. "Power tends to corrupt, and absolute power corrupts absolutely."

*Source: Lord Acton, letter to Bishop Creighton, 1887. Past relatives: Milton's line on reigning in Hell (2025), statesmen and politicians (2025).*

**Thesis.** Acton's warning is not a law of nature but a reliable tendency: power removes the checks that keep ordinary people honest, and the more complete the power, the fewer the checks; the lesson is not to fear power, which every society needs, but to divide it, limit it and make it answerable.

**Outline**

1. What Acton meant
   a. Power: the ability to make others do one's will
   b. Corrupt: not only bribery, but the decay of judgement and conscience
   c. "Tends": a tendency, not a certainty; "absolute": power without limit or challenge
2. Why power corrupts: the mechanism
   a. It removes consequences
   b. It surrounds the holder with flatterers; truth stops arriving
   c. It breeds a belief that one is indispensable
   d. It makes the next abuse easier than the last
3. The individual level
   a. The official with discretion and no audit
   b. The head of a family or firm who cannot be questioned
4. The level of rulers: history
   a. Roman emperors after the Republic's checks collapsed
   b. Absolute monarchs; the French Revolution's own leaders turning to terror
   c. Twentieth-century dictators: Hitler, Stalin; the cult of personality
   d. Post-colonial liberators who became rulers for life
5. The level of institutions and nations
   a. Any institution without oversight drifts towards serving itself
   b. Great powers acting without restraint abroad
   c. Corporate monopolies and unaccountable technology platforms
6. The Pakistani experience
   a. Rulers, civil and military, who extended their own terms
   b. Concentration of authority in a few offices; weak local government
7. Exceptions and objections
   a. Some held great power and gave it up: Washington after two terms, Mandela after one
   b. The Rashidun caliphs' insistence on being questioned by ordinary citizens
   c. Objection: it is weak character, not power, that corrupts
   d. Reply: power reveals and enlarges character; wise systems do not bet on saints
   e. Objection: powerlessness also corrupts, through fear and dependence. True, and it supports the same remedy: balance
8. The remedy: power made safe
   a. Separation of powers; independent courts
   b. Fixed terms and peaceful transfer
   c. A free press and the right to information
   d. Devolution: power spread across tiers
   e. Institutions stronger than individuals
9. Conclusion: we cannot do without power; we can refuse to leave it unwatched

**Evidence to carry.** Acton's date; Washington (1797); Mandela (1999); Montesquieu on separation of powers; one or two examples from Pakistan's constitutional history, stated calmly.

## 25. "Imagination is more important than knowledge."

*Source: Albert Einstein, interview, 1929. Asked in CSS 2022.*

**Thesis.** Einstein did not belittle knowledge; he ranked it second: knowledge tells us what is, while imagination asks what could be, and every advance in science, art and social life began as something imagined before it was known; yet imagination without knowledge is daydreaming, so the two are partners in which imagination leads.

**Outline**

1. What the statement means
   a. Knowledge: the stock of what is already understood
   b. Imagination: the power to picture what is not yet present
   c. Einstein's reason: knowledge is limited; imagination reaches beyond it
2. Imagination in science
   a. Einstein's thought experiments came before the mathematics
   b. Newton saw the falling apple and the orbiting moon as one problem
   c. Hypothesis is an act of imagination; experiment then tests it
3. Imagination in technology
   a. Flight, the telephone, the internet were pictured before they were built
   b. Entrepreneurs see a need others overlook
4. Imagination in art and literature
   a. Fiction lets us live other lives; it trains empathy
   b. Iqbal imagined a homeland before there was a map of it
5. Imagination in social and political life
   a. Abolition of slavery, votes for women, the end of apartheid: all were "impossible" until imagined
   b. Jinnah and the idea of Pakistan
   c. Reformers imagine a fairer order; conservatives of every age call it fantasy
6. Imagination in personal life
   a. Hope is imagination about one's own future
   b. Empathy is imagining another's pain
7. The limits
   a. Imagination without knowledge produces superstition, conspiracy theories and bad policy
   b. Imagination can serve evil: propaganda and weapons are inventive too
   c. Knowledge supplies the materials and the test
8. Resolution
   a. Knowledge is the ground; imagination is the leap
   b. In an age when machines store and retrieve all knowledge, the human advantage shifts further to imagination
9. Education: the practical lesson
   a. Rote learning fills the store and starves the faculty
   b. Schools should teach questions, arts, experiments and play
   c. Pakistan's examination culture rewards memory
10. Conclusion: knowledge keeps the lamp; imagination decides where to carry it

**Evidence to carry.** Einstein (Saturday Evening Post, 1929); Newton; Iqbal's Allahabad address (1930); the Wright brothers (1903); one line on AI and stored knowledge.

## 26. "Children must be taught how to think, not what to think."

*Source: Margaret Mead, Coming of Age in Samoa (1928). Asked in the Special CSS 2023 paper.*

**Thesis.** An education that hands children conclusions produces followers who are helpless when the world changes, while one that trains them to question, reason and judge produces citizens; teaching how to think does not mean teaching nothing, for good thinking needs knowledge and values, but it puts the method before the answer.

**Outline**

1. Meaning
   a. "What to think": fixed answers, memorised and repeated
   b. "How to think": observing, questioning, weighing evidence, reasoning to one's own conclusion
2. Why "what" alone fails
   a. Facts change; methods last
   b. Borrowed beliefs break under the first hard question
   c. A mind trained to obey is easily led by demagogues and extremists
3. Why "how" matters for the individual
   a. Problem-solving in work and life
   b. Resistance to rumour, advertising and online falsehood
   c. Confidence and moral independence
4. Why it matters for society
   a. Democracy needs voters who can judge claims
   b. Science and enterprise need people who ask "why" and "what if"
   c. Tolerance grows when people can understand a view they reject
5. Evidence
   a. Socrates taught by questions
   b. School systems admired for results stress understanding over recall
   c. Employers rank analytical thinking among the most needed skills
6. The Pakistani classroom
   a. Rote learning, guidebooks, examinations that reward reproduction
   b. Questioning the teacher seen as disrespect
   c. The CSS examiners' own complaint: crammed answers without original thought
7. Objections
   a. Children need facts and values before they can reason
   b. Unlimited questioning may weaken faith and tradition
   c. Reply: thinking needs content, so teach both, in the right order; and a belief that is understood is held more firmly than one that is only repeated
8. How to teach thinking
   a. Teachers trained to ask open questions
   b. Examinations that test application
   c. Reading beyond the textbook; debate, projects, experiments
   d. Mother-tongue instruction in early years so that children understand
   e. Parents who welcome "why"
   f. Media literacy
9. Conclusion: give a child an answer and she passes a test; give her a method and she can face a life

**Evidence to carry.** Mead (1928); the Socratic method; WEF Future of Jobs 2025 on analytical thinking; FPSC CE-2019 examiners' report; the Quran's repeated appeal to reflect and reason.

## 27. "Surely, with hardship comes ease."

*Source: Quran 94:5–6. Asked in the Special CSS 2023 paper. Related: sweet uses of adversity; Shelley's thorns of life (2021).*

**Thesis.** The verse does not promise that hardship will be brief or that ease will arrive without effort; it teaches that difficulty carries within it the seeds of relief and growth, a truth borne out in individual lives, in the history of nations and in nature, provided that patience is joined to action.

**Outline**

1. Reading the statement
   a. "With", not "after": relief is bound up with the trial
   b. Repeated twice in the chapter for emphasis
   c. Revealed to console the Prophet (peace be upon him) in the hardest Makkan years
2. Why hardship produces ease: the logic
   a. Difficulty forces effort, and effort builds capacity
   b. Loss clarifies what matters
   c. Shared suffering binds people together
3. The individual
   a. Illness, failure and poverty as teachers of resilience
   b. Lives that turned on a setback: Helen Keller; Edison's failed experiments; Mandela's 27 years in prison
   c. Psychology recognises growth after trauma
4. Sacred and national history
   a. The boycott and migration, then the return to Makkah
   b. Europe and Japan rebuilt after 1945
   c. Pakistan's own birth amid the upheaval of Partition, with millions displaced and few resources
5. Society and science
   a. Plagues led to public health; COVID-19 brought vaccines in a year
   b. Necessity as the mother of invention
6. Nature
   a. Night and day; winter and spring; the seed that must break to grow
7. The objection
   a. Some hardships crush: famine, war and injustice do not ennoble their victims
   b. Telling the poor to be patient can become an excuse for those who cause their suffering
   c. Reply: the verse comforts the sufferer; it does not excuse the oppressor. Ease comes through striving, and others have a duty to relieve hardship
8. Lessons
   a. Patience as active perseverance, not passive waiting
   b. Hope as a discipline
   c. For Pakistan: floods, debt and terror are trials that can force long-delayed reform
9. Conclusion: hardship is the price of the ease that follows; whoever pays it with effort does not pay in vain

**Evidence to carry.** Surah Al-Sharh (94:5–6); Quran 13:11 on a people changing their own condition; Mandela; post-war reconstruction; the 2022 floods and the solidarity that followed.

## 28. "I disapprove of what you say, but I will defend to the death your right to say it."

*Source: written by Evelyn Beatrice Hall in 1906 to sum up Voltaire's attitude; often wrongly credited to Voltaire himself. Asked in CSS 2020.*

**Thesis.** The sentence states the central rule of a free society: the right to speak belongs most of all to those with whom we disagree, because truth is found through open contest and power is checked by criticism; the right has limits where speech becomes direct harm, but those limits must be narrow, and the burden of proof lies on the censor.

**Outline**

1. Meaning
   a. Two things kept apart: my opinion of your view, and your right to hold it
   b. Tolerance means bearing what one dislikes
2. Why free speech matters
   a. Truth: no one is infallible; error is corrected only by challenge (J. S. Mill)
   b. Self-government: voters cannot judge what they are forbidden to hear
   c. Dignity: to silence a person is to treat them as less than an adult
   d. Safety valve: grievances spoken are less likely to explode
   e. Progress: every reform began as an unpopular opinion
3. History's verdict
   a. Socrates, Galileo: the silenced were later proved right
   b. Societies that punished dissent stagnated; open ones corrected themselves
   c. Famines and disasters are worst where the press is muzzled (Amartya Sen's observation)
4. The right in law
   a. Article 19 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights
   b. Article 19 of Pakistan's Constitution, with listed restrictions
5. The hard cases: limits
   a. Incitement to violence; defamation; threats
   b. Hate speech against communities
   c. Respect for what others hold sacred
   d. National security in wartime
6. The danger in limits
   a. Vague laws become tools against critics
   b. "Fake news" offences can punish inconvenient truth
   c. Who decides? The powerful rarely censor themselves
7. New challenges
   a. Social media: a lie reaches millions before it is answered
   b. Online mobs and "cancel culture" silence without any law
   c. Reply: more speech, better education and platform responsibility; not blanket bans
8. Pakistan
   a. A lively media and a record of pressure on it
   b. Intolerance in society as well as the state
   c. Need: a culture of disagreement without enmity
9. Conclusion: a right that protects only agreeable speech protects nothing

**Evidence to carry.** Hall (The Friends of Voltaire, 1906); Mill, On Liberty (1859); UDHR Article 19; Constitution Article 19; RSF 2026 rank.

## 29. "Patriotism is supporting your country all the time, and your government when it deserves it."

*Source: attributed to Mark Twain. Asked in CSS 2025.*

**Thesis.** Twain separates two loyalties that rulers like to merge: a country is its people, land and ideals, and deserves constant love, while a government is a temporary manager and deserves support only when it serves them; the true patriot is therefore often the honest critic, and criticism offered in good faith is a service to the nation.

**Outline**

1. Two things distinguished
   a. Country: people, territory, history, constitution, shared future
   b. Government: those who hold office for a time
   c. The state's permanent institutions stand between the two
2. Why the country deserves support "all the time"
   a. Belonging and gratitude; duties of citizenship
   b. Defence in danger, taxes in peace, obedience to law
   c. Support in failure as well as success
3. Why the government deserves only conditional support
   a. Governments err and sometimes abuse
   b. Consent can be withdrawn at the ballot box
   c. Blind support removes the pressure to improve
4. Dissent as patriotism: evidence
   a. Jinnah and Gandhi were "disloyal" to the government of British India
   b. Americans who opposed the Vietnam War; Germans who resisted Hitler
   c. Whistle-blowers and journalists who expose waste
   d. The judge who rules against the executive
5. When the two are confused
   a. Critics branded traitors; debate closes
   b. Mistakes go uncorrected until they become disasters
   c. Nationalism turns into worship of rulers
6. The Pakistani context
   a. A habit of labelling opponents as anti-state
   b. Patriotism shown in floods, earthquakes and war by ordinary citizens
   c. The constitution, not any office-holder, as the object of loyalty
7. Objections
   a. In war or crisis, criticism weakens the nation
   b. Who decides when a government "deserves" support?
   c. Reply: unity in real danger is right, but crisis is every ruler's favourite excuse; the citizen decides, by lawful means: vote, courts, press, peaceful protest
8. The limits of dissent
   a. Criticism must be truthful and lawful
   b. Opposition to a government must not become harm to the country: no violence, no inviting foreign pressure
9. Everyday patriotism
   a. Paying tax, doing one's job honestly, keeping the street clean
10. Conclusion: love the country enough to tell its government the truth

**Evidence to carry.** Twain's line; the oath of office to "preserve, protect and defend the Constitution"; examples in section 4; one recent example of civic solidarity.

## 30. "Universal human equality is a utopian dream."

*Past relatives: universal human equality is utopic (2021), gender equality as myth (2016).*

**Thesis.** Perfect equality of condition has never existed and probably never will, because people differ in talent, effort and luck; but equality of dignity, rights and opportunity is neither utopian nor optional: much of it has already been won, and the idea that it is only a dream has always been the favourite argument of those who profit from inequality.

**Outline**

1. Which equality?
   a. Of outcome: the same income and status for all
   b. Of opportunity: a fair start
   c. Of rights and dignity: one law, one worth
2. Why the statement seems true
   a. Billionaires and the destitute share one planet
   b. Inequality between nations: life expectancy differs by decades
   c. Discrimination by gender, race, caste and creed persists
   d. Attempts to force equal outcomes ended in tyranny (Soviet collectivisation)
   e. Natural differences in ability and ambition
3. Why it is false for dignity and rights
   a. Slavery, legal across the world two centuries ago, is outlawed everywhere
   b. Votes for women went from nowhere to almost everywhere in a century
   c. Colonial empires ended; apartheid fell
   d. The Universal Declaration (1948): "all human beings are born free and equal in dignity and rights"
4. Why it is false for opportunity
   a. Universal schooling and public health have narrowed gaps within many countries
   b. Scandinavian states combine markets with high mobility
   c. Extreme poverty has fallen sharply since 1990
5. The moral and religious ground
   a. The Last Sermon: no superiority of Arab over non-Arab except by piety
   b. Enlightenment and constitutional traditions
6. Why equality matters in practice
   a. Unequal societies are less stable and less trusting
   b. Wasted talent is lost growth
7. The objection restated
   a. "Some inequality is the reward for effort and drives progress"
   b. Agreed: the aim is fair rules, not identical results
8. Unfinished business
   a. Gender gaps; minorities; the digital divide; climate burdens on the poor
   b. Pakistan: elite capture, unequal schooling, Article 25 in letter and practice
9. How to move closer
   a. Education and health for all; progressive taxation; equal justice; affirmative measures with time limits
10. Conclusion: a horizon is never reached, yet it tells the traveller which way to walk

**Evidence to carry.** UDHR Article 1; Constitution Article 25; abolition of slavery (Britain 1833, United States 1865); women's suffrage (New Zealand 1893); the Last Sermon; HDR and Gender Gap rankings.

## 31. "Beware the barrenness of a busy life."

*Attributed to Socrates. Asked in CSS 2018.*

**Thesis.** Being busy is not the same as being useful: a life or a nation can be full of motion and empty of purpose, and the warning matters more than ever in an age of constant notification; the cure is not idleness but direction, choosing fewer things that matter and doing them with attention.

**Outline**

1. Meaning
   a. Busy: occupied, hurried, always doing
   b. Barren: producing nothing of worth
   c. The paradox: activity can hide emptiness
2. Why busy lives become barren
   a. Urgent tasks crowd out important ones
   b. No time to think, so no time to ask whether the work is worth doing
   c. Relationships starved of attention
   d. Health and inner life neglected
   e. Busyness worn as a badge of importance
3. The modern form
   a. Smartphones: every pause filled with a screen
   b. Work that follows people home; the culture of long hours
   c. Students collecting certificates without learning
   d. Rising stress, burnout and loneliness in the most "connected" generation
4. Evidence that less can be more
   a. Scientists and writers whose best ideas came in quiet: Newton in the plague year, Darwin on his walks
   b. Spiritual traditions that set aside time for reflection: prayer, retreat, the Sabbath
   c. Studies linking rest and focus to better work
5. Institutions and nations
   a. Offices judged by files moved, not problems solved
   b. Governments that announce many projects and finish few
   c. Meetings, committees and reports as substitutes for results
   d. Pakistan: activity around crises, little work on causes
6. The objection
   a. Hard work built every great life and nation; idleness is the worse danger
   b. The poor cannot choose leisure
   c. Reply: the target is not effort but aimless effort; purpose makes work fruitful, and the poor suffer most from systems that are busy and barren
7. A fruitful life
   a. Know the purpose: ask "to what end?"
   b. Priorities: the few things that matter
   c. Depth over speed; attention as a discipline
   d. Time for family, service, reading and reflection
   e. Measure by results and by meaning
8. Conclusion: a full calendar is not a full life; what counts is the harvest

**Evidence to carry.** The attribution to Socrates; Newton's year at Woolsthorpe (1665–66); Thoreau's Walden; the Islamic concept of *barakah* in time; one fact on screen time or burnout that you can source.
