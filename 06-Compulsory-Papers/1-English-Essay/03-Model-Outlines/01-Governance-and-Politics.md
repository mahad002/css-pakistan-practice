# Model Outlines 1–5: Governance and Politics

## 1. Democracy in Pakistan has the form but not the substance

*Type: argumentative. Past relatives: democracy's hopes and hurdles (2018), democracy and illiteracy (2019), dynastic politics (2025).*

**Decode.** "Form" means elections, parliaments, a constitution. "Substance" means that power really changes hands by vote, institutions limit rulers, and citizens receive rights and services. The topic asks you to judge the gap.

**Thesis.** Pakistan has kept the ceremonies of democracy for most of its history but has rarely allowed its substance, because elected governments have been weak against unelected power, parties have been run as family firms, and voters have been given little reason to believe that their vote changes their lives; the remedy is more democracy, practised at every tier, not less.

**Outline**

1. What democracy requires
   a. Form: elections, legislature, written constitution
   b. Substance: civilian supremacy, rule of law, accountable parties, local self-government, rights
2. The form exists
   a. The 1973 Constitution has survived two suspensions
   b. Three assemblies in a row completed their terms between 2008 and 2023
   c. The 18th Amendment (2010) restored the parliamentary system and devolved powers
3. The substance is missing: the state
   a. Four military rulers; no prime minister has completed a five-year term
   b. Courts have at times validated takeovers and removed prime ministers
   c. Recent constitutional amendments (26th in 2024, 27th in 2025) were passed in haste and are contested as shifting the balance between institutions
4. The substance is missing: the parties
   a. Dynastic leadership; few real party elections
   b. Candidates chosen for money and local influence ("electables")
   c. Parliament bypassed through ordinances and thin debate
5. The substance is missing: the citizen
   a. Local governments are absent or powerless for long periods
   b. Low literacy and poverty make voters dependent on patrons
   c. Disputed elections reduce trust in the ballot
6. What the gap costs
   a. Policy changes with every government; investors and citizens cannot plan
   b. The EIU Democracy Index 2024 moved Pakistan into its "authoritarian" category
   c. Youth lose faith in politics and look to the street or abroad
7. The opposing view: democracy does not suit Pakistan
   a. The claim: order and growth came under strong rule
   b. Reply: every period of direct rule ended in crisis and left institutions weaker (1971, 1988, 2007)
   c. Democracy's failures here are failures of practice, and are cured by practice
8. Giving democracy its substance
   a. Every institution within its constitutional limits
   b. An election commission and process that losers can accept
   c. Party reform: internal elections, open finances
   d. Elected local governments with protected funds
   e. Civic education and a free press
9. Conclusion: the form has been kept alive; the substance must now be built

**Evidence to carry.** 1973 Constitution; 18th, 26th, 27th Amendments with years; EIU Democracy Index 2024 (rank 124 of 167); Article 140A; examples of dynastic succession in major parties (state it neutrally, name no one abusively).

**If the wording changes.** For "dynastic politics", expand section 4 into the main body. For "democracy and illiteracy", expand 5(b) and argue that literacy improves democracy but is not a precondition for it (India voted before it could read).

## 2. Pakistan's crisis is a crisis of governance, not of resources

*Type: argumentative. Past relatives: governance and institution building (2016), bureaucracy (2021), local government (2026).*

**Decode.** The topic sets two explanations against each other. You must show that the country has resources (people, land, location, minerals) and that weak management, not scarcity, explains the results.

**Thesis.** Pakistan is not a poor country so much as a poorly governed one: with fertile land, a young population and a strategic location, its repeated crises come from institutions that cannot collect, plan, deliver or correct, and they will ease only when the civil service, local government and accountability systems are rebuilt.

**Outline**

1. Terms
   a. Governance: how public decisions are made and carried out
   b. Good governance: participation, rule of law, transparency, responsiveness, accountability
2. The resources are there
   a. The fifth-largest population in the world; median age about 20
   b. One of the largest canal irrigation systems; major producer of wheat, rice, milk
   c. Location between China, Central Asia, the Gulf and South Asia
   d. Remittances above $38 billion a year from workers abroad
3. The results are not
   a. HDI rank 168 of 193 (HDR 2025)
   b. About 26 million children out of school
   c. Repeated balance-of-payments crises and IMF programmes
4. Governance failures that explain the gap
   a. A state that cannot tax: revenue near a tenth of GDP
   b. Loss-making state enterprises and power-sector debt
   c. A civil service built for control, with generalists rotated every few months
   d. Political interference in postings and transfers
   e. Over-centralisation: provinces hold what districts should run
   f. Slow courts: more than two million pending cases
   g. Policy made without data, changed with each government
5. A case that shows the pattern
   a. Floods of 2022 and 2025: warnings existed; encroachments, weak local bodies and poor drainage turned rain into disaster
6. The opposing view: resources really are short
   a. Debt service consumes nearly half the federal budget
   b. Reply: the debt itself is the bill for decades of weak revenue and waste
7. Reform agenda
   a. Civil service: specialised cadres, secure tenure, performance pay, lateral entry
   b. Digital government to cut discretion (e-procurement, online records)
   c. Elected, funded local governments (Article 140A)
   d. Privatise or restructure state enterprises
   e. Court reform and alternative dispute resolution
   f. Policy continuity through cross-party agreement on the economy
8. Conclusion: the shortage is of management, and management can be learned

**Evidence to carry.** HDR 2025 rank; Economic Survey 2025-26 figures; World Bank governance definitions; Article 140A; reform reports (the National Commission for Government Reforms under Ishrat Husain, 2008).

**If the wording changes.** For "bureaucracy", make 4(c), 4(d) and 7(a) the core. For "institution building", define institutions as rules that outlast persons.

## 3. Accountability in Pakistan: a tool of justice or a weapon of politics?

*Type: argumentative. Past relatives: fight against terrorism and corruption (2018).*

**Decode.** The "or" asks you to judge how accountability has actually been used. Corruption is the background; the subject is the accountability process.

**Thesis.** Corruption in Pakistan is real and costly, but accountability has too often been used selectively, against rivals and at chosen moments, and this has damaged both justice and the fight against corruption; only an across-the-board, rule-based system focused on prevention can restore its meaning.

**Outline**

1. Terms and stakes
   a. Corruption: public office used for private gain
   b. Accountability: answering for the use of power, before law and public
2. The problem is real
   a. CPI 2025: score 28 of 100, rank 136 of 182
   b. Everyday bribery in police, land records, utilities
   c. Large losses in procurement, tax evasion and state enterprises
3. A history of selective accountability
   a. 1949 PRODA and 1959 EBDO were used to disqualify politicians
   b. Accountability bodies of the 1990s were run by governments against their opponents
   c. NAB (1999) was created under military rule; critics, including superior courts, have faulted its methods
   d. Cases rise and fall with a politician's fortunes
4. Why selective accountability fails
   a. The public stops believing any verdict
   b. Officials stop signing files: fear replaces initiative
   c. Businesses hold back investment
   d. Real offenders escape through plea bargains and delay
   e. Some institutions remain outside the ordinary process
5. The opposing view
   a. Some accountability is better than none; convictions did recover money
   b. Reply: recoveries are small beside the damage to trust; uneven law is not law
6. What works elsewhere
   a. Independent, well-resourced commissions (Hong Kong's ICAC, Singapore's CPIB)
   b. Prevention through simple rules and digital systems
7. Reform
   a. One law for all holders of public money
   b. Appointment of accountability heads by cross-party process, fixed tenure
   c. Separate investigation from prosecution; time limits for trials
   d. Digitise procurement, land and tax to remove discretion
   e. Right to information and whistle-blower protection in practice
   f. Asset declarations that are published and audited
   g. Decent pay for officials
8. Conclusion: accountability must be blind before it can be believed

**Evidence to carry.** Transparency International CPI 2025; Jinnah's 11 August 1947 speech on bribery and corruption; NAB founding year; Right of Access to Information Act 2017; ICAC (1974).

**If the wording changes.** For "corruption as the mother of all ills", move section 2 forward and add effects on poverty, services and security.

## 4. Will the rule of law remain a distant dream in Pakistan?

*Type: argumentative. Past relative: rule of law as an impracticable myth (2018).*

**Thesis.** The rule of law is weak in Pakistan because law has been applied by status, not by principle, but it is not a myth: where rules have been enforced without exception the public has obeyed them, and a state that reforms its courts, police and its own conduct can make law rule.

**Outline**

1. Meaning
   a. Supremacy of law over persons; equality before law; due process; independent courts
   b. Rule *of* law is different from rule *by* law
2. Why it matters
   a. Security of life and property; the base of investment
   b. The only peaceful way to settle disputes
3. Evidence of weakness
   a. WJP Rule of Law Index 2025: 130th of 143 countries; last on order and security
   b. More than two million cases pending in courts
   c. Constitutional breaks validated under the "doctrine of necessity"
   d. Different treatment for the powerful: protocol, plea bargains, amnesties
   e. Mob violence and misuse of serious charges against the weak
4. Causes
   a. Colonial-era laws and police designed to control, not serve (Police Act 1861 tradition)
   b. Under-resourced lower courts; adjournment culture
   c. Poor investigation and prosecution; low conviction rates
   d. Elite capture: those who make the law are rarely bound by it
   e. Public attitude: breaking small rules is seen as cleverness
5. Signs that it is possible
   a. Motorway police: same law, same people, different enforcement
   b. Automated traffic fines and digital land records in Punjab reduced discretion
   c. Lawyers' movement of 2007–09 showed public demand for an independent judiciary
6. The opposing view: culture cannot be changed
   a. Reply: the same citizens obey rules abroad; behaviour follows enforcement
7. Making law rule
   a. Police reform: operational autonomy, forensic capacity
   b. Judicial reform: case management, more judges, limits on adjournments, ADR
   c. Equal application: end special treatment
   d. Legal aid for the poor
   e. Legislative review of outdated laws
   f. Civic education from school
8. Conclusion: not a myth, but a choice not yet made

**Evidence to carry.** WJP 2025 figures; pending-case figure (Law and Justice Commission); Article 4 and Article 25 of the Constitution; Motorway police example.

**If the wording changes.** For "justice delayed is justice denied", centre on 3(b), 4(b) and 7(b).

## 5. Political polarisation is costing Pakistan its future

*Type: expository with a stand. Past relatives: polarised politics (2020), polarisation, governance and society (2024).*

**Thesis.** Polarisation in Pakistan has moved beyond normal party rivalry into a state where each side denies the other's right to rule; it paralyses parliament, politicises institutions and families alike, and it can be reduced only by agreed rules of the game, not by the victory of one camp.

**Outline**

1. What polarisation is
   a. Disagreement is healthy; polarisation is the belief that the opponent is an enemy
   b. Signs: no dialogue, disputed elections, politics by court case and street
2. How Pakistan reached here
   a. A long history of governments removed before their term
   b. The 1990s cycle of two parties undoing each other
   c. Since 2014: sit-ins, disqualifications, a no-confidence vote (2022), mass arrests after May 2023, a disputed election in 2024
   d. Social media that rewards anger and spreads rumours
   e. Television talk shows built on confrontation
3. Costs to governance
   a. Parliament boycotted; laws passed without debate
   b. Economic policy held hostage: reforms reversed for short-term popularity
   c. Federal–provincial quarrels when different parties rule
   d. Institutions (courts, bureaucracy, election bodies) pulled into party fights
4. Costs to society
   a. Families, campuses and workplaces divided
   b. Trust in all news sources falls
   c. Young people disengage or emigrate
5. Costs to security and foreign policy
   a. A divided house deals weakly with terrorism and with creditors
6. The opposing view: polarisation is a sign of political awakening
   a. True that more citizens are engaged
   b. Reply: engagement without rules turns into permanent conflict
7. Lowering the temperature
   a. A new "charter" between parties on elections, the economy and the limits of institutions
   b. Election reform that losers can trust
   c. Parliament as the place for disputes: stronger committees
   d. Media and platform codes against hate and fabricated content
   e. Local governments to give more actors a stake
   f. Political leaders who model restraint
8. Conclusion: no side can win this contest; the country can only lose it

**Evidence to carry.** Charter of Democracy (2006) as precedent; dates above; Digital 2026 figures on social media use; any one concrete economic cost (for example, reforms delayed under IMF programmes).

**If the wording changes.** For "pragmatism versus passion in politics", use sections 2, 3 and 7 to argue that passion wins elections and pragmatism runs countries.
