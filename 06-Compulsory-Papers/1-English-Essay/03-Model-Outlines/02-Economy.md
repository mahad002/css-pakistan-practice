## 6. From debt to prosperity: what Pakistan's economy needs

*Type: expository with a stand. Past relatives: pathways to prosperity (2024), real development (2018), foreign aid (2016).*

**Decode.** The topic asks for a route, not a list of complaints. Diagnose briefly, then spend most of the essay on what must change and why it has not.

**Thesis.** Pakistan's economy moves in a circle of borrowing, short booms and rescues because it consumes more than it produces and imports more than it exports; prosperity lies in breaking that circle through revenue, exports, productivity and people, and the obstacle is political will more than economic knowledge.

**Outline**

1. The circle
   a. Growth picks up, imports surge, reserves fall, the IMF is called
   b. About two dozen IMF arrangements since 1958
2. Where the economy stands
   a. Growth of 3.7% in FY26; GDP about $452 billion; per capita income $1,901
   b. Public debt Rs 83.3 trillion (March 2026); interest takes close to half the federal budget
   c. National poverty rose from 21.9% (2018-19) to 28.9% (2024-25)
   d. Investment about 14% of GDP, among the lowest in the region
3. Root causes
   a. Narrow tax base: revenue near a tenth of GDP
   b. Exports stuck near $30–32 billion; dependence on textiles
   c. Energy that is costly and unreliable
   d. Loss-making state enterprises
   e. Low savings; elite subsidies and exemptions
   f. Low skills and low female participation
   g. Policy instability; security concerns
4. What stability has achieved since 2023
   a. Default avoided; reserves rebuilt to about $21.8 billion (March 2026)
   b. Primary surplus; inflation brought down from its 2023 peak
   c. But stability is not growth, and the 2026 oil shock showed how thin the cushion is
5. Pathways
   a. Tax everyone who earns: agriculture, retail, property; simplify and digitise
   b. Export-led growth: IT services, value-added textiles, food processing, minerals
   c. Fix energy: competitive market, lower losses, local solar and hydel
   d. Privatise or close loss-makers
   e. Invest in people: schooling, skills, health
   f. Bring women into the workforce
   g. Regional trade and connectivity
   h. Policy continuity: a cross-party economic charter
6. The opposing view: growth first, reform later
   a. Reply: every growth spurt without reform has ended in a crisis within three or four years
7. Why it has not been done
   a. Those who benefit from exemptions sit in the legislatures
   b. Short political horizons
8. Conclusion: prosperity is a matter of choices that have been postponed

**Evidence to carry.** Economic Survey 2025-26 figures above; IMF EFF of $7 billion (September 2024); Vietnam's exports above $400 billion as a comparison; World Bank poverty note.

**If the wording changes.** For "real development is human development", use 2(c), 5(e), 5(f) and the HDI.

## 7. No taxation, no nation: building a tax culture in Pakistan

*Type: expository with a stand. Past relatives: tax culture (2016), informal economy (2020).*

**Thesis.** Pakistan's weak tax culture is less a moral failing of citizens than the result of a bargain in which the state asks little of the powerful and gives little to the rest; revenue will rise only when the system becomes fair, simple and visibly useful to those who pay.

**Outline**

1. Why tax matters
   a. Tax is the price of public services and of independence from lenders
   b. A state that cannot tax must borrow or print
2. The picture
   a. Tax revenue near a tenth of GDP, below regional peers
   b. Only a few million active income-tax filers in a country of over 250 million
   c. Heavy reliance on indirect taxes, which fall hardest on the poor
   d. A large informal economy, often estimated at a third or more of GDP
3. Why people do not pay
   a. Exemptions for powerful sectors: agriculture incomes, real estate, retail
   b. Complex laws and discretionary officials invite bargaining
   c. Low trust: taxpayers see little return in schools, hospitals or security
   d. Cash economy and weak documentation
   e. Amnesties reward evasion
   f. Smuggling and under-invoicing
4. Consequences
   a. Debt and dependence on the IMF
   b. Honest taxpayers and salaried workers carry the burden
   c. Formal firms cannot compete with untaxed rivals
   d. Under-funded schools and hospitals
5. The informal economy: problem and cushion
   a. It employs most workers and softens crises
   b. It also means no protection for workers and no revenue for the state
   c. Aim: make formality cheaper than informality, not to crush small traders
6. The opposing view: taxes are already too high
   a. True for those inside the net
   b. Reply: rates can fall only if the base widens
7. Building the culture
   a. Tax all income alike, whatever its source
   b. Simplify: fewer rates, online filing, faceless assessment
   c. Use data: property, travel, utility and bank records
   d. End amnesties
   e. Digital payments and incentives for documentation
   f. Show the return: local taxes spent on local services
   g. Reform the tax authority: separate policy from collection
8. Conclusion: citizens pay willingly when they believe the system is fair

**Evidence to carry.** FBR revenue trend; provincial agricultural income tax laws passed in 2025 under the IMF programme; composition of direct and indirect taxes; debt-service share.

**If the wording changes.** For "informal economy: the way forward", make section 5 the centre and add measures for small firms: simple registration, micro-credit, social security.

## 8. Food security begins on the farm: reviving Pakistan's agriculture

*Type: expository. Past relatives: world food systems (2022), revitalising agriculture (2026).*

**Thesis.** Agriculture feeds Pakistan, employs a third of its workers and supplies its main exports, yet it is trapped in low yields, wasted water and climate shocks; reviving it requires treating farming as a modern industry that needs science, markets and credit, not as a way of life to be subsidised.

**Outline**

1. Why agriculture matters
   a. 23.4% of GDP and about 33% of employment (Economic Survey 2025-26)
   b. Raw material for textiles; base of rural life
   c. Food security for over 250 million people
2. The paradox
   a. A top producer of wheat, rice, milk and sugarcane
   b. Yet imports of wheat, pulses, edible oil and cotton in bad years
   c. Widespread child stunting: about 40% of under-fives
3. What is wrong on the farm
   a. Yields per acre well below those of comparable countries
   b. Flood irrigation wastes water; agriculture takes the great bulk of withdrawals
   c. Poor seed; little research; weak extension services
   d. Small, fragmented holdings; limited mechanisation
   e. Cotton has fallen to about 7 million bales from nearly twice that a decade ago
4. What is wrong beyond the farm
   a. Middlemen and informal credit capture the farmer's margin
   b. Support prices and subsidies have favoured wheat and sugarcane over higher-value crops
   c. Storage and cold-chain losses
   d. Little processing or branding for export
5. Climate as a new threat
   a. The 2025 floods cost agriculture about Rs 430 billion
   b. Heat, erratic rain, shifting seasons
6. The opposing view: the future is industry and services
   a. Reply: neither can grow without cheap food and raw materials; agro-processing is industry
7. Revival
   a. Certified seed and funded research
   b. Efficient irrigation: laser levelling, drip, canal lining, water pricing
   c. Crop zoning; shift towards oilseeds, pulses, horticulture
   d. Credit, crop insurance, digital land records
   e. Market reform: storage, direct sales, fewer intermediaries
   f. Livestock and dairy modernisation (livestock is over 60% of the sector)
   g. Climate-smart practices and early warning
   h. Women farmers: land rights and extension
8. Conclusion: a country that cannot feed itself cheaply cannot do anything else well

**Evidence to carry.** Survey figures above; wheat 29.6 million tonnes, cotton 7.05 million bales (FY26); stunting (World Bank); China–Pakistan cooperation on seed under CPEC's second phase.

**If the wording changes.** For "food security", add access and affordability: food can be on the market and out of reach.

## 9. CPEC and beyond: can geography make Pakistan prosperous?

*Type: argumentative. Past relatives: CPEC (2018), new war fronts in economic zones (2019), CPEC and the India–Middle East–Europe corridor (2024).*

**Thesis.** Pakistan's location gives it a real chance to earn from trade between China, Central Asia and the sea, and CPEC was the first serious attempt to use it; but corridors reward only countries that have industry to move along them and peace along the route, so geography is an opportunity that domestic reform must convert.

**Outline**

1. The idea of geo-economics
   a. From selling strategic location for aid to earning from trade and transit
   b. The National Security Policy (2022) put economic security at the centre
2. What CPEC is
   a. Launched in 2015 as a flagship of China's Belt and Road
   b. Energy projects, highways, Gwadar port, special economic zones
3. What it has delivered
   a. Power plants that eased the load-shedding of the 2010s
   b. Motorways linking north and south
   c. Gwadar port and airport built
4. Where it has fallen short
   a. Industrial zones slow to start; little relocation of factories
   b. Costly power contracts added to capacity payments and debt
   c. Local grievances in Balochistan; attacks on Chinese workers
   d. Trade with China remains heavily one-sided
5. The wider map
   a. Central Asia: the Uzbekistan–Afghanistan–Pakistan railway agreement (2025)
   b. Energy links: CASA-1000, TAPI on paper
   c. Rival corridors: Chabahar, and the India–Middle East–Europe corridor announced in 2023
   d. Corridors as tools of competition: the "new war fronts"
6. What stands in the way
   a. Insecurity in Balochistan and Khyber Pakhtunkhwa
   b. Tense borders with India and Afghanistan; the Gulf shaken by the 2026 war
   c. Weak export base: a road without goods is only a road
   d. Slow decisions and policy reversals
7. The opposing view: CPEC is a debt trap
   a. Chinese loans are a minority of Pakistan's external debt, though a significant one
   b. Reply: the trap is Pakistan's own low exports; the answer is to make the projects earn
8. Making geography pay
   a. Second phase: industry, agriculture, IT, the ML-1 railway
   b. Security through local inclusion: jobs and services for host communities
   c. Transit trade agreements and modern border management
   d. Balance: ties with China, the Gulf and the West together
   e. Skills so that Pakistanis fill the jobs
9. Conclusion: location is given; prosperity is made

**Evidence to carry.** CPEC launch year and main projects; NSP 2022; IMEC (G20, September 2023); UAP railway framework (July 2025); one figure on the China trade imbalance from the latest Economic Survey.

**If the wording changes.** For "new war fronts lie in economic zones", generalise: sanctions, tariffs, chip controls and corridors are today's battlefields; then apply to Pakistan.
