## 21. Between giants: Pakistan's foreign policy in an age of great-power rivalry

*Type: expository with a stand. Past relatives: global power dynamics and Pakistan's foreign policy (2020), BRICS and Pakistan (2024), multipolar order (2026), permanent interests (2026).*

**Decode.** The "giants" are the United States and China, with India, Russia and the Gulf powers around them. The question is how a middle-sized, indebted state should place itself.

**Thesis.** In a world dividing between Washington and Beijing, Pakistan's interest lies in refusing to choose a camp: it should keep its partnership with China, rebuild a working relationship with the United States, deepen ties with the Gulf and its neighbours, and above all repair its economy, because a country that must borrow has little freedom to manoeuvre.

**Outline**

1. The setting
   a. US–China rivalry in trade, technology and security
   b. A more multipolar world: Russia, India, the Gulf states, Turkey acting on their own
   c. Wars in Europe and the Middle East; weaker international institutions
2. Pakistan's constants
   a. Geography: neighbour to China, India, Afghanistan and Iran; near the Gulf
   b. Security concerns centred on India; the Kashmir dispute
   c. Economic dependence on external finance
   d. Nuclear capability
3. The record: alliances and their cost
   a. Cold War pacts (SEATO, CENTO); the Afghan wars of the 1980s and after 2001
   b. Aid and weapons came; so did militancy and dependence
   c. Lesson: fighting other powers' wars is a poor bargain
4. The China relationship
   a. Strategic and economic partner; CPEC; defence cooperation
   b. Limits: debt, trade imbalance, security of Chinese nationals
5. The United States
   a. Largest export market; influence in the IMF
   b. A relationship that cooled after 2021 and warmed again in 2025
   c. Pakistan's role in hosting and mediating US–Iran talks in 2026
6. The neighbourhood and the Muslim world
   a. India: the May 2025 conflict; the Indus Waters Treaty in abeyance
   b. Afghanistan: militancy, trade, refugees
   c. Iran: a long border and the 2026 war
   d. Saudi Arabia and the Gulf: workers, remittances, the 2025 mutual defence agreement
7. The opposing view: pick the winning side
   a. Reply: dependence on one patron has failed before; interests, not friendships, are permanent
8. Principles for the future
   a. Balance, not bloc politics
   b. Economic diplomacy: markets, investment, labour mobility, technology
   c. Regional connectivity with Central Asia
   d. Peace on the borders where possible; deterrence where necessary
   e. An active voice in the UN, OIC and SCO on Kashmir, Palestine and climate
   f. A professional, well-resourced foreign service
   g. Economic strength at home as the base of everything else
9. Conclusion: a state is as independent abroad as it is solvent at home

**Evidence to carry.** National Security Policy 2022; Pakistan's UN Security Council term (2025–26); the Saudi–Pakistan Strategic Mutual Defence Agreement (September 2025); the Islamabad Talks (April 2026) and the Islamabad Memorandum (June 2026); the May 2025 conflict dates; Palmerston's remark on permanent interests (1848).

**If the wording changes.** For "nations have no permanent friends", use sections 3–6 as proof and section 8 as the lesson.

## 22. The multipolar world: a safer order or a more dangerous one?

*Type: argumentative. Past relatives: UN military engagements and peace (2017), unipolar, bipolar or multipolar (2023 Special), multipolar order and Pakistan (2026).*

**Thesis.** The passing of single-power dominance was bound to come and offers smaller states more room, but a world of several armed and suspicious powers without respected rules is more dangerous, not safer; whether multipolarity brings balance or chaos depends on rebuilding common institutions, beginning with the United Nations.

**Outline**

1. From one pole to many
   a. Bipolar Cold War (1945–91); the unipolar moment after 1991
   b. Today: the United States, China, Russia, the European Union, India and regional powers
   c. Economic weight has shifted towards Asia; BRICS has expanded
2. The case that multipolarity is safer
   a. No single power can impose its will; the excesses of the unipolar years (Iraq, 2003) are harder to repeat
   b. More choices for developing countries in finance, arms and markets
   c. A fairer reflection of the world's population and economy
3. The case that it is more dangerous
   a. History: the multipolar Europe of 1914 slid into world war
   b. More actors mean more miscalculation
   c. Wars since 2022: Ukraine, Gaza, the India–Pakistan clash of 2025, the Gulf war of 2026
   d. World military spending reached a record of nearly $2.9 trillion in 2025
   e. Arms control has frayed; nuclear risks have risen
   f. Trade and technology used as weapons: tariffs, sanctions, export controls
4. The weakness of the referee
   a. The Security Council paralysed by vetoes on Ukraine and Gaza
   b. Its membership reflects 1945, not today
   c. Court rulings and resolutions ignored by the powerful
   d. Funding cuts and withdrawal from agencies and agreements
5. Global problems that no pole can solve alone
   a. Climate change, pandemics, artificial intelligence, debt, migration
6. The opposing view: institutions have always been a mask for power
   a. Partly true
   b. Reply: weak rules still restrained more than no rules; small states are the first to suffer when rules vanish
7. What a safer multipolar order needs
   a. Security Council reform: wider membership, restraint on the veto in mass-atrocity cases
   b. Renewed arms control and crisis hotlines
   c. A working trade system and fairer global finance
   d. Stronger regional organisations
   e. Middle powers acting as bridges and mediators
8. What it means for Pakistan
   a. Opportunity: more partners, a mediating role, relevance of geography
   b. Risk: being squeezed between rivals; conflict in the neighbourhood
9. Conclusion: many poles need a roof; without one, the weather decides

**Evidence to carry.** SIPRI military expenditure 2025; UNHCR displacement figures; vetoes on Gaza and Ukraine; BRICS expansion (2024); New START's expiry in February 2026 [verify status before the exam]; Pakistan's 2025–26 Council term.

**If the wording changes.** For "has the UN failed?", build on section 4 and argue for reform over abandonment.

## 23. Is globalisation in retreat?

*Type: argumentative. Past relatives: global village (2016), Brexit and globalisation (2017), pros and cons (2021), globalisation of markets (2022), globalisation and national economies (2024).*

**Thesis.** Globalisation is not ending, but its confident, market-led phase is over: trade wars, pandemics and conflicts have made governments prize security over efficiency, and the world is moving from one open market towards rival blocs; for developing countries the task is to keep access to markets and technology while protecting their people from shocks.

**Outline**

1. What globalisation is
   a. Growing movement of goods, money, people, data and ideas across borders
   b. Driven by technology, cheaper transport and policy (WTO, 1995)
2. What it delivered
   a. Hundreds of millions lifted from poverty, above all in East Asia
   b. Cheaper goods; spread of technology and knowledge
   c. A connected "global village"
3. What it cost
   a. Lost factory jobs and stagnant wages in parts of the rich world
   b. Widening inequality within countries
   c. Financial crises that spread fast (1997, 2008)
   d. Environmental damage; pressure on local cultures
   e. Fragile supply chains exposed by COVID-19
4. Signs of retreat
   a. Brexit (2016) and the politics of "taking back control"
   b. US–China tariff and technology war since 2018; broad US tariffs in 2025
   c. Sanctions after the Ukraine war; export controls on chips
   d. A paralysed WTO dispute system
   e. Tighter borders against migrants
   f. The 2026 Gulf war: a reminder of how one strait can choke world trade
5. Signs that it continues in new form
   a. Trade in services and data keeps growing
   b. Regional agreements (RCEP, the African free trade area)
   c. "Friend-shoring": supply chains moving, not vanishing
   d. No country can solve climate or pandemics alone
6. The opposing view: de-globalisation is good for the poor nations
   a. Self-reliance has appeal
   b. Reply: no country grew rich in isolation; the losers of a closed world are small exporters
7. What developing countries should do
   a. Diversify markets and products
   b. Join regional value chains
   c. Invest in skills to move into services and technology
   d. Build buffers: reserves, food and energy security
   e. Speak together for fair rules in trade, debt and climate
8. Pakistan
   a. Exports low and narrow; reliance on remittances and a few markets
   b. Opportunity in relocation of supply chains and digital services
9. Conclusion: the tide is not going out; it is changing direction, and those who read it will still sail

**Evidence to carry.** WTO founding (1995); the 2008 crisis; US tariffs of April 2025; the Appellate Body's paralysis since December 2019; World Bank figures on the fall in extreme poverty; Pakistan's export and remittance figures.

**If the wording changes.** For "developing countries and international trade", make sections 6–8 the body. For "global village: learning to live together", stress culture, migration and tolerance.
