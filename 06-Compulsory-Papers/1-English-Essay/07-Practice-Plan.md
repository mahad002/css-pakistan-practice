## 1. Principles

1. **Writing is learned by writing.** Reading model essays feels like progress; only a finished, timed, handwritten essay is progress.
2. **Outlines are cheap practice.** A full essay costs three hours. An outline costs twenty minutes and trains the hardest skills: decoding, thesis, structure. Do many.
3. **Every essay gets marked**, by you, two days later, with the rubric. An unmarked essay is half wasted.
4. **Fix one weakness at a time.** After each essay, name one thing to improve in the next.
5. **By hand, on lined paper, with a timer**, from May 2027 at the latest.
6. **Content comes from daily reading.** Twenty minutes with the Dawn editorial and opinion pages each day feeds the essay, Current Affairs and Pakistan Affairs together.

## 2. The 12-month schedule

Essay time averages 4–5 hours a week: the Saturday slot plus two or three short drills.

### Phase 0: now to 31 January 2027 (GRE first)

- Read one Dawn editorial a day. Write its argument in one sentence.
- No essays. GRE vocabulary and reading work help this paper anyway.

### Phase 2: Core build, May–July 2027 (13 weeks)

### Phase 3: MPT period, August–October 2027

Goal: do not lose the skill while MCQ practice takes over.

- One outline a week (20 minutes).
- One full essay every three weeks (four in the period).
- Keep the daily reading.
- **October 2027:** run the update checklist in `05-Evidence-Bank.md`, section 14. Revise the likely-themes list.

### Phase 4: Written prep, November 2027–January 2028 (13 weeks)

Goal: 20 essays; speed and reliability; current facts.

### February 2028: the last two weeks

## 3. Weekly drills

| Drill | Time | How |
|---|---|---|
| **Decode** | 10 min | Take five past topics. For each: key terms, limiter, tension, question |
| **Thesis** | 15 min | One topic, three different theses (for, against, qualified). Pick the most defensible |
| **Outline** | 20 min | Full outline of 20–30 points from a cold topic |
| **Paragraph** | 15 min | One outline point → one PEEL paragraph of 130–160 words, by hand |
| **Introduction** | 15 min | One topic, two introductions with different hooks |
| **Counter-argument** | 15 min | State, concede, reply, for a thesis you hold |
| **Compression** | 15 min | Summarise a Dawn opinion piece in 80 words (also précis practice) |
| **Handwriting speed** | 20 min | Copy or compose for 20 minutes; count words; divide by 20 |
| **Fact recall** | 10 min | Write from memory ten facts on one theme, with year and source; check |

## 4. Marking your own essay: the procedure

You have no teacher, so replace the teacher with a procedure. It takes 30–40 minutes.

### 4.1 Wait

Mark the essay at least **48 hours** after writing it. You cannot see your own gaps on the same day.

### 4.2 Reverse outline (10 minutes)

Without looking at your original outline, read the essay and write in the margin, in five or six words, what each paragraph says. Then:

- Compare the margin notes with the original outline. Every outline point should appear, in order. Mark any that are missing or extra.
- Read the margin notes alone, top to bottom. Do they form an argument that answers the topic?
- If two notes say the same thing, you repeated yourself.
- If a note does not help answer the topic, the paragraph is irrelevant.

### 4.3 Objective counts (10 minutes)

Count and write down:

| Measure | Target |
|---|---|
| Total words (essay only) | 2,500–3,000 |
| Paragraphs in the body | 12–18 |
| Times the topic's key words appear | In the introduction, at least five topic sentences, and the conclusion |
| Specific facts with year or source | 12–20 |
| Named examples (events, laws, countries, people) | 8 or more |
| Counter-argument paragraphs | 1–3 |
| Grammar and spelling errors on a sample of three pages | 3 or fewer per page |
| Sentences longer than three handwritten lines | 0–3 in the essay |
| Minutes over time | 0 |

### 4.4 Score with the rubric (10 minutes)

Use section 5. Be strict. Write one line of justification for each score.

### 4.5 A second reader

A procedure cannot fully replace a reader. Arrange at least one of these:

- **A CSS-qualified officer or a senior candidate** (try your university's alumni network). Ask for three essays to be read in Phase 2 and three in Phase 4.
- **A study partner** preparing for the same exam: exchange essays monthly and mark each other with the rubric.
- **An AI assistant as a proofreader**, with care. Type or photograph the essay and ask for (a) grammar and usage errors with corrections, (b) a reverse outline, (c) places where the argument does not follow. Do not ask it for a mark: no tool knows how an FPSC examiner marks. Do not let it write for you.

### 4.6 Close the loop

Write three lines in the progress log: what worked, the main weakness, the one thing to change next time. Add new errors to the error log.

## 5. The self-marking rubric

This rubric is this pack's own tool. It is built from FPSC's published words (syllabus, paper instruction, examiners' reports); it is **not** an FPSC marking scheme, which does not exist in public.

### 5.1 The five criteria

**A. Understanding of the topic and thesis (20 marks)**

| Marks | Description |
|---|---|
| 17–20 | Every key word of the topic is addressed. The thesis answers the exact question, takes a position and appears in the outline, the introduction and the conclusion. The essay never drifts |
| 13–16 | Topic understood. Thesis clear but general, or one key word under-treated. Minor drift |
| 9–12 | Essay is on the general subject, not the exact topic. Thesis vague ("has both merits and demerits") |
| 5–8 | Half the topic ignored, or a memorised essay bent to fit |
| 0–4 | Off topic or topic misunderstood |

**B. Outline and organisation (20 marks)**

| Marks | Description |
|---|---|
| 17–20 | Outline of 20–30 points in claims, logically grouped, one format. The essay follows it exactly. Clear introduction, body, conclusion. Each paragraph has one idea and a topic sentence. Smooth transitions |
| 13–16 | Sound outline, mostly followed. A few paragraphs without clear topic sentences or in a weak order |
| 9–12 | Outline is a list of labels. Essay partly departs from it. Repetition. Paragraphs of mixed ideas |
| 5–8 | Outline thin or confused. Essay is a string of points |
| 0–4 | No outline, or no visible structure |

**C. Argument and content (30 marks)**

| Marks | Description |
|---|---|
| 25–30 | A sustained argument. Each claim explained and supported with accurate, specific evidence (facts with year or source, examples, comparisons). More than one dimension covered. A fair counter-argument, answered. Some original insight. Remedies, where relevant, are specific |
| 19–24 | Clear argument with adequate evidence. Some claims asserted without support. Counter-argument present but brief |
| 13–18 | More description than argument. Evidence general or thin ("many reports show"). No real counter-argument |
| 7–12 | Assertions, slogans and repetition. Factual errors |
| 0–6 | Little relevant content |

**D. Language (20 marks)**

| Marks | Description |
|---|---|
| 17–20 | Correct, clear, varied sentences. Precise vocabulary. Formal but natural tone. No more than one or two slips per page |
| 13–16 | Generally correct. Occasional errors in articles, prepositions or agreement. Some clumsy sentences. Meaning always clear |
| 9–12 | Frequent errors (three to five a page). Limited or misused vocabulary. Clichés |
| 5–8 | Errors in most sentences. Meaning sometimes unclear |
| 0–4 | Meaning often lost |

**E. Length, completeness and presentation (10 marks)**

| Marks | Description |
|---|---|
| 9–10 | 2,500–3,000 words. Complete, with a proper conclusion. Legible throughout. Neat corrections, margins, paragraphing |
| 7–8 | 2,200–2,500 words, or small lapses in neatness. Complete |
| 5–6 | 1,800–2,200 words, or a rushed conclusion, or hard to read in places |
| 3–4 | 1,300–1,800 words, or no real conclusion |
| 0–2 | Under 1,300 words, or unfinished, or largely illegible |

### 5.2 Caps

A serious fault limits the total, whatever the other scores. Apply the lowest cap that fits.

| Fault | Maximum total |
|---|---|
| Off topic or topic misread (A is 0–4) | 25 |
| Essay unfinished (stops before the conclusion) | 35 |
| Under 1,500 words | 35 |
| More than five language errors a page throughout | 38 |
| No outline | 38 |
| Outline and essay do not match | 40 |

### 5.3 Reading your score

| Your rubric total | What it probably means |
|---|---|
| Below 50 | Likely fail in the real paper. Find the criterion that is dragging you down and work on that alone |
| 50–59 | Borderline. One bad day fails it |
| 60–69 | A probable pass. This should be your steady level by January 2028 |
| 70 and above | Strong. Keep it reliable; do not chase more |

Why set your own bar at 60 when the pass mark is 40? Because you are a lenient marker of your own work, and FPSC marking is known to be severe: in at least one recent year no candidate reached 60. The gap is your safety margin. This conversion is my judgement, not a measured fact.

## 6. Checklists

### 6.1 Before you start writing (minute 45)

- [ ] I can say the topic's question in my own words.
- [ ] My thesis answers it and uses its key words.
- [ ] The outline has 20–30 points, written as claims, in one format.
- [ ] Every section serves the thesis. Nothing is there only because I know it.
- [ ] There is a counter-argument and a reply.
- [ ] If the topic is a problem, there is a way forward with specific measures.
- [ ] I know my hook and my last line.

### 6.2 While writing

- [ ] One outline point, one paragraph. Tick each point.
- [ ] Topic sentence first.
- [ ] A fact or example in most paragraphs, followed by an explanation.
- [ ] Clock check at the end of each section.
- [ ] Topic key words repeated every page or so.
- [ ] By minute 150, I am starting the conclusion, whatever is left.

### 6.3 Before handing in (last 15 minutes)

- [ ] Topic number and title written exactly.
- [ ] Verbs: agreement and tense.
- [ ] Articles and plurals.
- [ ] No unfinished sentence.
- [ ] Names, dates and figures.
- [ ] Conclusion answers the question and adds nothing new.
- [ ] Pages in order; continuation sheets numbered.

### 6.4 The week before the exam

- [ ] Pens tested (at least four).
- [ ] Evidence sheets updated and reviewed.
- [ ] Error log read.
- [ ] One full timed essay done at the hour of the real paper.
- [ ] Answer-book instructions from the FPSC notice read.

## 7. 40 practice topics

Written for this pack in the style of recent papers. A few in the last group are well-known sayings (34 is Churchill's; 36 is usually credited to Victor Hugo), as on real papers. Types: **A** argumentative, **E** expository, **D** descriptive/analytical, **Q** quotation or abstract.

### Pakistan: state and politics

1. Elections alone do not make a democracy. (A)
2. Institutions, not individuals, build nations. (A)
3. The civil service of Pakistan: steel frame or rusted cage? (A)
4. Federalism in Pakistan: unity through diversity or division through distrust? (A)
5. Judicial reform is the reform on which all other reforms depend. (A)
6. Pakistan's cities: engines of growth or monuments to neglect? (A)
7. Why do good policies fail in Pakistan? (E)
8. A state that fears questions cannot answer its problems. (Q)

### Pakistan: economy and people

9. Can Pakistan make its current IMF programme its last? (A)
10. Exports, not loans, are the road to economic sovereignty. (A)
11. Remittances: a blessing that hides a failure. (A)
12. Poverty in Pakistan is a policy choice, not a fate. (A)
13. Rethinking the purpose of the university in Pakistan. (E)
14. Skills, not degrees, will decide Pakistan's future. (A)
15. The girl child's schooling is the cheapest reform Pakistan has never fully tried. (A)
16. Brain drain or brain gain: what does migration do to Pakistan? (A)
17. Public health in Pakistan: paying for hospitals, neglecting health. (E)

### Climate, water, energy

18. The next war in South Asia may be fought over water. (A)
19. Floods are natural; disasters are man-made. (A)
20. The sun can end Pakistan's energy crisis, if the state lets it. (A)
21. Who should pay for climate change? (A)
22. Food security in an age of climate change. (E)

### Technology and media

23. Artificial intelligence in the classroom: tutor or cheat? (A)
24. Data is the new oil, and privacy the new pollution. (Q/A)
25. Freedom of expression in the age of disinformation. (E)
26. The smartphone has connected the world and isolated the individual. (A)
27. Can technology fix governance? (A)

### World affairs

28. Is the United Nations still relevant? (A)
29. The global South in a divided world: opportunity or new dependence? (A)
30. The Muslim world: rich in resources, poor in unity. (D)
31. Economic interdependence no longer guarantees peace. (A)
32. Pakistan as a mediator: can a middle power shape events? (A)
33. International law: a shield for the weak or a tool of the strong? (A)

### Abstract and quotation topics

34. "The price of greatness is responsibility." (Q)
35. It is easier to fight for one's principles than to live up to them. (Q)
36. He who opens a school door closes a prison. (Q)
37. Change is the only constant. (Q)
38. Courage is not the absence of fear but the mastery of it. (Q)
39. The means are the seeds; the end is the tree. (Q)
40. A society is judged by how it treats its weakest members. (Q)

**Using the list.** Do not work through it in order. For each Saturday, roll for a number or ask someone to choose three, then pick one of the three in ten minutes, as you will in the hall. Also practise on the real papers of 2016–2026 (copy the exact wording from the sources in `02-Past-Paper-Analysis.md`).
