# VIII. Punitive and Reformative Treatment of Criminals

Asked 2020 (corporal vs capital punishment; recidivism and reintegration; institutional and community corrections), 2024 (rehabilitation centres and reintegration), 2025 (types of justice). Section II back-up topic.

---

## 1. Philosophies of punishment

| Philosophy | Aim | Thinkers | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Retribution** | Punish because the offender deserves it ("just deserts"); proportionality; society's moral outrage | Kant; Hegel; von Hirsch (*Doing Justice*, 1976) | Sentences scaled to seriousness |
| **Deterrence** — general | Discourage others | Beccaria, Bentham | Publicised punishment |
| **Deterrence** — specific | Discourage the offender | | Fines, imprisonment |
| **Incapacitation** | Prevent offending by removing ability (prison, death, curfews) | | Long sentences for repeat offenders |
| **Rehabilitation** | Change the offender | Positivists; medical model | Education, therapy, vocational training |
| **Restoration** | Repair harm to victim and community | Howard Zehr (*Changing Lenses*, 1990); Braithwaite | Mediation, compensation, conferencing |
| **Denunciation/expressive** | Express society's condemnation | Durkheim; Feinberg | Public trials |

**Martinson's "nothing works" (1974)** shook faith in rehabilitation; later research (meta-analyses; "what works" — risk–need–responsivity model, Andrews and Bonta) showed well-designed programmes reduce recidivism.

## 2. Types of justice (2025)

| Type | Meaning | Criminal-justice example | Criticism |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Retributive justice** | Punishment proportionate to wrongdoing; offender "pays" to the state | Prison terms scaled to offence seriousness; qisas as retribution in kind | Ignores victim's needs and causes of crime |
| **Procedural justice** | Fairness of the process: voice, neutrality, respect, trustworthy authorities (Tom Tyler, 1990) | Fair trial (Art. 10A), legal aid, impartial judges, due process | Fair process may still give unjust outcomes |
| **Distributive justice** | Fair distribution of benefits and burdens in society (Aristotle; Rawls) | Equal access to justice and policing; fair allocation of resources; bail system that does not punish the poor | Contested criteria (need, merit, equality) |
| **Restorative justice** | Crime is harm to people and relationships; repair through dialogue and amends | Victim–offender mediation, family group conferencing, community service, diyat and compounding in Pakistani law; JJSA diversion | Risk of pressure on victims (especially women in honour cases); unsuitable for serious violence without safeguards |

(Also: transitional justice, social justice, corrective justice.) A 2025 MCQ classed Parliament and the Constitution as instruments of political justice.

## 3. Corporal punishment

- **Meaning:** punishment inflicted on the body — whipping/flogging, caning, amputation, branding, stocks.
- **History:** common in all pre-modern systems; abolished in most Western countries in the 19th–20th centuries.
- **Pakistan:** whipping was widely used under martial law (1977–85) and Hudood laws; **Abolition of the Punishment of Whipping Act 1996** abolished whipping except as **hadd**; hadd amputations have never been carried out by the state **[verify]**. Corporal punishment of children in schools banned in ICT (2021) and in some provinces **[verify]**.
- **For:** quick, cheap, visible deterrence; avoids prison contamination; religious basis for hudud.
- **Against:** cruel, inhuman and degrading (UN Convention against Torture — Pakistan ratified 2010; ICCPR Art. 7); brutalises society; no evidence it deters more than other punishment; irreversible; disproportionately used on the poor.
- **Capital punishment (2020 contrast):** death penalty. Pakistan: about 30 offences carry death **[verify current count]**; moratorium 2008–2014, lifted after the Army Public School attack (December 2014); hundreds executed in 2015–16, very few since 2020 **[verify]**; large death-row population. Debate: deterrence evidence weak; wrongful convictions; Islamic law permits qisas but encourages forgiveness; EU GSP+ pressure. The **death penalty for drug trafficking** under the CNSA was abolished by amendment **[verify year — reported 2023]**.

## 4. Imprisonment

- **History:** prisons as punishment are modern (18th–19th centuries). John Howard, *The State of the Prisons* (1777) — reform movement. **Pennsylvania (separate) system** — Eastern State Penitentiary (1829): solitary confinement and reflection. **Auburn (silent/congregate) system**, New York (1820s): work together in silence, sleep alone — became the US model. Bentham's **Panopticon** (1791); Michel Foucault, *Discipline and Punish* (1975) — prison as disciplinary power.
- **Functions:** incapacitation, deterrence, retribution, rehabilitation.
- **Effects:** **prisonisation** (Donald Clemmer, 1940) — inmates absorb prison culture; Gresham Sykes, *The Society of Captives* (1958) — **pains of imprisonment** (loss of liberty, goods and services, heterosexual relations, autonomy, security); Goffman's "total institution" (*Asylums*, 1961); stigma; family breakdown; loss of employment; criminal networks.
- **Types of institutions:** maximum, medium, minimum security; open prisons; juvenile institutions; women's prisons; high-security prisons for terrorists (Sahiwal high-security prison in Punjab **[verify]**).
- **Pakistan's prisons:** see file 06 (160.6% occupancy, 75% under-trial, Oct 2026).

## 5. Rehabilitation of criminals (2020, 2024)

**Recidivism:** re-offending, re-arrest, re-conviction or re-imprisonment after a sanction. Measured over 2–5 years. Causes: lack of jobs and housing after release, stigma, addiction, mental illness, criminal peers, institutionalisation, no aftercare.

**Rehabilitation:** planned interventions to change the offender's attitudes, skills and circumstances so he stops offending.

**Risk–Need–Responsivity (RNR) model (Andrews and Bonta):** match intensity to **risk**; target **criminogenic needs** (antisocial attitudes, peers, substance abuse, employment, family); deliver in a way the offender responds to (**responsivity**: cognitive-behavioural, tailored).

**Institutional programmes:** education (literacy; AIOU degrees in prisons); vocational training (tailoring, carpentry, IT) with certificates; work with wages; religious and moral education; cognitive-behavioural therapy (anger management, thinking skills); drug treatment; mental-health care; family contact; pre-release planning.

**Rehabilitation centres' role (2024):** structured programmes, counselling, skill-building, health care, de-addiction, and links to employers and families. Examples: Sabaoon (Swat) and Mishal centres for de-radicalisation of youths and adults (Pakistan Army with civil partners, from 2009); Juvenile Rehabilitation Centres (JJSA 2018); drug rehabilitation centres (ANF Model Addiction Treatment and Rehabilitation Centres **[verify]**).

**Reintegration (re-entry):** aftercare and parole supervision; halfway houses; job placement (employer incentives; removal of criminal-record barriers for minor offences); microfinance (Akhuwat for ex-prisoners **[verify]**); family counselling; community mentors; faith-based support; expungement of records for juveniles.

**Modern institutional strategies for Pakistan (2020):**
1. Classification and separation (first-time vs hardened, juveniles, women, terrorists).
2. Individual risk–need assessment at entry.
3. Education and certified vocational training linked to market needs (with TEVTA/NAVTTC).
4. Prison industries with fair wages and savings.
5. Drug treatment inside prisons.
6. Psychological counselling; de-radicalisation programmes.
7. Digital records and case management.
8. Probation and parole expansion; open prisons for low-risk inmates.
9. Aftercare: through a probation and parole service, NGOs and employers.
10. Restorative justice programmes; victim awareness.
11. Monitoring of outcomes (recidivism data).

## 6. Institutional and community corrections (2020)

| Institutional corrections | Community (alternative) corrections |
|---|---|
| Jails/prisons (maximum, medium, minimum security) | **Probation** |
| Juvenile institutions, borstals | **Parole** |
| Boot camps | **Community service orders** |
| Open prisons/prison farms | **Fines, restitution, compensation** (diyat, arsh, daman under PPC) |
| Halfway houses (transitional) | **Electronic monitoring and house arrest** |
| | **Day-reporting centres, intensive supervision** |
| | **Restorative justice** (mediation, conferencing) |
| | **Drug courts/treatment orders** |
| | **Suspended sentences, conditional discharge** (CrPC s.562) |

**Advantages of community corrections:** cheaper; avoid prison contamination; keep family and work ties; reduce overcrowding; better for low-risk and juvenile offenders. **Risks:** public perception as "soft"; need for strong supervision capacity.

**Pakistan:** community corrections underused — probation officers few; electronic monitoring not in law **[verify]**; courts default to custody; bail difficult for the poor. Reform: use of non-custodial measures in the UN **Tokyo Rules (1990)** spirit; more probation officers; sentencing guidelines; judicial training.
