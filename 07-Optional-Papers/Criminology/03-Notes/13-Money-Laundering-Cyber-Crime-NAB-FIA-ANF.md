## 1. Money laundering

**Definition:** the process of disguising the illegal origin of criminal proceeds so they appear legitimate. FATF: processing criminal proceeds to disguise their illegal origin.

**Stages:**
1. **Placement** — introducing cash into the financial system (deposits below reporting thresholds — "smurfing", buying assets, currency exchange, casinos).
2. **Layering** — complex transactions to obscure the trail (wire transfers through many accounts and jurisdictions, shell companies, trade invoices, crypto mixing).
3. **Integration** — returning funds as apparently legitimate wealth (real estate, businesses, luxury goods, loans).

**Methods:** hawala/hundi (informal value transfer); trade-based laundering; shell and front companies; real estate (especially with cash and undervaluation); benami (proxy) accounts and properties; fake bank accounts (Pakistan's 2018 fake accounts case); prize bonds (high-denomination bonds withdrawn in 2021 **[verify]**); cash couriers; crypto-assets; professional enablers (lawyers, accountants, real estate agents).

**Effects:** fuels crime, terrorism and corruption; distorts the economy and asset prices; erodes tax base; damages financial integrity and reputation (grey-listing raises costs of trade and finance); capital flight.

### Trade-Based Money Laundering (TBML) (2021)
- Moving value through trade transactions to disguise illicit origin.
- **Techniques:** over-invoicing and under-invoicing of goods; multiple invoicing; over- or under-shipment; falsely described goods; phantom shipments; black-market peso exchange-type schemes.
- **Pakistan:** misdeclaration of imports and exports; Afghan Transit Trade abuse (goods diverted into Pakistan); undervalued imports; links with hawala. Responses: FBR/Customs' WeBOC and Pakistan Single Window (data), FATF guidance, trade-data matching with partner countries, SBP's monitoring of trade finance.

### Do cryptocurrencies carry high money-laundering risk? (2021)
- **Risk factors:** pseudonymity; cross-border speed; unregulated exchanges; mixers and privacy coins; DeFi; peer-to-peer trading; ransomware payments; scams.
- **Counter-points:** blockchains are transparent and traceable (blockchain analytics firms trace flows); most laundering still uses cash and trade; regulation of exchanges (FATF's 2019 standard on virtual assets and VASPs, the "travel rule") reduces risk.
- **Balanced answer:** high *potential* risk where unregulated; manageable with VASP licensing, KYC, travel rule, analytics and international cooperation.
- **Pakistan:** SBP long barred banks from dealing in crypto (2018 circular); in 2025 the government created a **Pakistan Crypto Council** and a **Pakistan Virtual Assets Regulatory Authority (PVARA)** to license and regulate virtual-asset service providers **[verify legal instrument and status]**.

### FATF and Pakistan
- **FATF** (G7, Paris, 1989): 40 Recommendations; mutual evaluations; "grey list" (increased monitoring) and "black list".
- **Pakistan:** grey-listed 2008–2010 **[verify]**, 2012–2015, and **June 2018 – October 2022**. Completed a 27-point action plan (terror financing) and a 7-point plan (AML, from APG's 2019 evaluation); removed in October 2022 after an on-site visit. Next APG mutual evaluation expected **[verify date]**.
- **Effects of reforms:** stronger laws, more STRs, prosecutions for terror financing (including of proscribed groups' leaders), beneficial ownership transparency, regulation of DNFBPs.

### Challenges and way forward
Large cash and informal economy; hawala; weak prosecution and convictions; real estate opacity; political interference; capacity of FMU and LEAs; need for digital payments (Raast), property tax and registration reform, international asset recovery, and continued FATF compliance.

## 2. Cyber crime

**Definition:** crime in which a computer, network or digital device is the target, tool or storage of evidence. Two broad types: **cyber-dependent** (hacking, malware, DDoS) and **cyber-enabled** (fraud, harassment, child exploitation, extremism online).

**Forms:**
| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Hacking | Unauthorised access (PECA s.3–5) |
| **Virus** | Malicious code that **inserts itself into other programs** and spreads when they run; damages data |
| **Worm** | Self-replicating malware that spreads without a host program |
| Trojan | Malware disguised as legitimate software |
| **Spyware** | Secretly collects private information |
| Ransomware | Encrypts data and demands payment |
| **Phishing** | Deceptive messages to obtain credentials — a form of identity theft/fraud |
| Vishing/smishing | Phone/SMS phishing — common banking scams in Pakistan |
| **Spoofing** | Disguising identity (email, caller ID, website) |
| **Spamming** | Sending unwanted bulk email |
| **Skimming** | Copying card data at ATMs/POS |
| Identity theft | Using another's personal data |
| Cyber stalking/harassment | PECA s.24 |
| Child sexual abuse material | PECA s.22 |
| Cyber terrorism | PECA s.10 |
| Deepfakes | AI-generated fake audio/video for fraud, blackmail, disinformation |
| Crypto scams | Fake investment platforms |

**Explanations:** routine activity (motivated offenders, suitable targets online, absent guardians); rational choice (low risk, high reward); social learning (hacker subcultures); space transition theory (Jaishankar, 2008); neutralisation.

**Pakistan's framework:**
- **Prevention of Electronic Crimes Act (PECA) 2016** — offences (unauthorised access, data damage, cyber terrorism, electronic fraud and forgery, identity theft, cyber stalking, offences against dignity and modesty, child pornography, spoofing, spamming), procedural powers, PTA powers to block content (s.37).
- **PECA (Amendment) Act 2025** (passed by Parliament in January 2025): new offence of spreading false or fake information likely to cause fear, panic or unrest (up to three years and/or fine); a **Social Media Protection and Regulatory Authority**; social media complaint council and tribunals; and a **National Cyber Crime Investigation Agency (NCCIA)** replacing the FIA's Cyber Crime Wing ([Amnesty](https://www.amnesty.org/en/latest/news/2025/01/pakistan-authorities-pass-bill-with-sweeping-controls-on-social-media/); [ARY summary](https://arynews.tv/peca-amendment-act-2025-the-key-points/)). Critics (journalists' unions, NCHR, rights groups) say the "fake news" offence is vague and threatens free expression **[verify current litigation]**.
- Electronic Transactions Ordinance 2002; Pakistan Telecommunication (Re-organization) Act 1996; National Cyber Security Policy 2021; **Digital Nation Pakistan Act 2025**; **National AI Policy 2025**; Personal Data Protection Bill still pending **[verify]**.
- **Institutions:** NCCIA (formerly FIA Cyber Crime Wing/NR3C), PTA, provincial CTDs (online extremism), Punjab Safe Cities Authority, National CERT (PKCERT, 2023 **[verify]**).

**Challenges:** under-reporting; skills shortage; low conviction rates; cross-border evidence (MLA); encryption; anonymity; slow courts; misuse of cyber laws against journalists and critics; digital illiteracy; women's vulnerability to online harassment (Digital Rights Foundation helpline data).

**Prevention:** awareness and digital literacy; strong authentication; bank and telecom fraud controls (SIM biometric verification); data protection law; capacity of NCCIA; international cooperation (Budapest Convention — Pakistan not a party **[verify]**; UN Cybercrime Convention adopted 2024 **[verify]**); public–private partnerships with platforms and banks.

## 3. Emerging technologies and future crime (2026)

**Deepfakes:** fraud (voice cloning to impersonate executives or relatives), sextortion and non-consensual intimate images, political disinformation, evidence tampering ("liar's dividend" — real evidence dismissed as fake). Responses: detection tools, watermarking, criminal offences for malicious deepfakes, digital forensics capacity, media literacy.

**Quantum computing:** could eventually break current public-key encryption ("harvest now, decrypt later"), threatening banking and state secrets; also helps defenders (quantum-safe cryptography — NIST post-quantum standards 2024 **[verify]**). Timeline uncertain.

**AI surveillance:** facial recognition and predictive policing can improve detection but risk mass surveillance, bias against minorities and the poor, false matches, chilling effects, and misuse by authoritarian or corrupt actors; data breaches. Needs legal limits, oversight and audits.

**Other:** AI-assisted cyber attacks and phishing; autonomous drones (smuggling, attacks — drone drops on borders); crypto and DeFi crime; IoT hacking; synthetic identity fraud.

**Criminological lens:** routine activity (new targets, weak guardianship), rational choice (lower costs, higher rewards), techno-social inequality; conflict theory (who controls surveillance); "crime harvest" (Pease) — new products create new crime opportunities, so "design out crime" early.

### AI's effects on employment, surveillance and inequality in Pakistan (2026)
- **Employment:** automation threatens routine jobs (call centres, data entry, some textile and banking work) → unemployment → strain and possible rise in property and cyber crime; but also new jobs (IT exports, freelancing — Pakistan among top freelancing countries).
- **Surveillance:** Safe City facial recognition, NADRA biometrics, social-media monitoring — crime control benefits vs privacy and political misuse.
- **Inequality:** digital divide (rural, women, poor) → AI benefits concentrated among elites → relative deprivation (conflict and strain theories); algorithmic bias in credit and policing.
- **Policy:** reskilling (NAVTTC, DigiSkills), social protection, data protection and AI regulation (National AI Policy 2025 implementation), oversight of police AI, inclusive digital access.

## 4. Role of NAB, FIA and ANF

### National Accountability Bureau (NAB)
- **Law:** National Accountability Ordinance 1999 (Musharraf), replacing the Ehtesab Bureau (1997).
- **Mandate:** eradicate corruption and corrupt practices by holders of public office and others; awareness, prevention, enforcement (investigation, prosecution in Accountability Courts); plea bargain and voluntary return.
- **Structure:** Chairman (appointed after consultation between PM and opposition leader); Prosecutor-General; regional bureaus.
- **Record:** large recoveries claimed through plea bargains; high-profile cases against politicians.
- **Criticism:** selective and politically timed accountability; long pre-trial detention; low conviction rates; harassment of businessmen and bureaucrats causing "policy paralysis"; plea bargain legitimises corruption.
- **Reforms:** 2022 and 2023 amendments — cases involving less than **Rs 500 million** excluded; limits on powers to arrest and remand; restricted jurisdiction over tax, regulatory and cabinet decisions; Supreme Court struck down parts in September 2023 but a larger bench **restored** the amendments in **September 2024** on the federal government's intra-court appeal **[verify]**.

### Federal Investigation Agency (FIA)
- **Law:** FIA Act 1974 (successor to the Special Police Establishment, 1942/1947). Under the Ministry of Interior.
- **Mandate (Schedule offences):** corruption by federal employees; economic crimes (banking fraud, hawala/hundi, money laundering, counterfeit currency); immigration and passport offences; human trafficking and migrant smuggling (TIP and SOM Acts 2018); terrorism and terror financing (Counter-Terrorism Wing); intellectual property; cyber crime (until the NCCIA took over in 2025); INTERPOL's NCB in Pakistan.
- **Role in organised crime (2022, 2023):** dismantling human smuggling networks (after boat tragedies); action against hawala and illegal currency trade (2023 crackdown supporting the rupee **[verify]**); terror financing cases; banking fraud; trafficking rings; coordination with INTERPOL and foreign agencies.
- **Challenges:** political use against opponents; limited capacity and forensic resources; overlapping jurisdiction with NAB, ANF, police; corruption within; slow prosecution; brain drain of skilled staff.
- **Reforms:** professional investigators and prosecutors; independence from political control; forensic and financial investigation capacity; data sharing with FMU, NADRA, banks; performance audits.

### Anti-Narcotics Force (ANF) (2025 note)
- **Origins:** Pakistan Narcotics Control Board (1973) → ANF created in **1995**; **ANF Act 1997**. Under the Ministry of Narcotics Control; headed by a serving Major General; officers seconded from the army and police.
- **Mandate:** enforcement of the **Control of Narcotic Substances Act (CNSA) 1997** — investigation, interdiction, asset freezing and forfeiture of drug proceeds; demand reduction (awareness, treatment — Model Addiction Treatment and Rehabilitation Centres); international cooperation (UNODC, Triangular Initiative with Iran and Afghanistan, liaison with foreign agencies); maintaining the Pakistan Narcotics policy.
- **Context:** Pakistan is a major transit route for Afghan opiates, hashish and methamphetamine; domestic drug use estimated at **6.7 million** users in the 2013 UNODC survey **[verify; a new survey has been planned]**; over **28,000 prisoners** held under the CNSA (NCHR/JPP, Oct 2026). Death penalty for drug trafficking under CNSA was abolished by amendment **[verify year]**.
- **Achievements:** large seizures (Pakistan regularly among the top seizing countries for opiates and hashish **[verify]**); airport and seaport interdiction; awareness campaigns in schools.
- **Challenges:** porous Afghan and Iranian borders; small force relative to task; corruption; growing synthetic drugs ("ice") among youth; weak treatment capacity; low conviction rates; prison overcrowding from petty possession cases.
- **Way forward:** shift petty users from prison to treatment; intelligence-led targeting of trafficking networks and finances; border management; regional cooperation; national drug-use survey; school prevention programmes.
