# IV. Crime and Criminality: Theoretical Perspectives

Theory questions appeared in eight of ten papers: perspectives (2017), Islamic perspective (2018), nature vs nurture and realism (2020), biological theories (2021), Merton's anomie (2022), labelling (2024), strain (2025), conflict theory and symbolic interactionism (2026). The 2026 paper asked theories **applied to new problems** (polarisation, hate crime, misinformation, AI). For each theory learn: author and year, core idea, key concepts, strengths, criticisms, Pakistani application.

---

## 1. Early explanations

- **Demonology/spiritual explanations:** crime as sin, the devil's influence; trial by ordeal or battle. Islamic and Christian traditions linked crime to moral failing but also to accountability before God.
- **Classical school:**
  - **Cesare Beccaria, *Dei delitti e delle pene* (On Crimes and Punishments), 1764:** people have free will and act rationally; punishment should deter, be proportionate, certain and prompt (certainty matters more than severity); laws should be clear and public; opposed torture and the death penalty; the social contract justifies punishment.
  - **Jeremy Bentham:** utilitarianism; hedonistic (felicific) calculus — people weigh pleasure against pain; the Panopticon prison design (1791).
  - **Influence:** French Penal Code 1791; modern deterrence theory; rational choice theory.
  - **Criticism:** ignores mental illness, age, social conditions; equal punishment can be unjust.
- **Neo-classical school:** free will limited by age, insanity, circumstances → mitigating factors; French Code of 1819.
- **Positivist school (determinism, scientific method):**
  - **Cesare Lombroso, *L'uomo delinquente* (1876):** the "born criminal" is an evolutionary throwback (**atavism**) identifiable by physical stigmata (sloping forehead, large jaw, long arms). Later accepted social causes. Discredited by **Charles Goring, *The English Convict* (1913)**, which found no physical criminal type.
  - **Enrico Ferri:** social, physical and anthropological factors; law of criminal saturation.
  - **Raffaele Garofalo:** "natural crime" offends basic sentiments of pity and probity.
  - **Phrenology** (Franz Joseph Gall, Spurzheim, early 1800s): skull shape reveals mental faculties. **Physiognomy** (Johann Kaspar Lavater, 1770s): facial features reveal character. Both pseudo-scientific (2020 question).

## 2. Biological theories (critically analysed in 2021)

| Theory | Author | Claim | Criticism |
|---|---|---|---|
| Atavism | Lombroso (1876) | Born criminals are throwbacks | Goring (1913) found no difference; no control groups |
| Body type (somatotypes) | William Sheldon (1949) | Mesomorphs (muscular) more delinquent than endomorphs or ectomorphs; also Glueck and Glueck (1950) | Correlation may reflect social reaction or selection |
| Heredity / family studies | Dugdale's *The Jukes* (1877); Goddard's *Kallikak Family* (1912) | Crime runs in families | Ignored environment; Goddard's data manipulated; misused for eugenics |
| Twin studies | Lange (1929); Christiansen (1977) | Identical twins more concordant than fraternal | Shared environment confounds |
| Adoption studies | Mednick, Gabrielli and Hutchings (1984, Denmark) | Adoptees resemble biological parents in convictions | Modest effect; selective placement |
| XYY chromosome | Jacobs et al. (1965) | Extra Y linked to aggression | Later studies found link to low intelligence, not violence |
| Biochemical | Hormones (testosterone), diet, hypoglycaemia, lead exposure | Imbalances affect aggression | Effects small, interact with environment |
| Neurological | Brain injury, frontal lobe dysfunction, low arousal (Raine) | Impulse control deficits | Not deterministic |
| Neurotransmitters / genetics | Low MAOA activity ("warrior gene") with childhood maltreatment (Caspi et al., 2002) | Gene–environment interaction | Ethical risks; determinism |

**Biosocial criminology:** modern view — biology creates predispositions that interact with environment (gene × environment). **Criticisms of biological theories overall:** determinism; ethical misuse (eugenics, racism); ignore social definitions of crime (who makes laws); cannot explain white-collar crime or crime rate changes; policy implications (medicalisation, preventive detention).

**Nature vs nurture (2020):** nature = biology and genetics; nurture = family, peers, environment, culture. **Balanced position:** interaction — biology loads the gun, environment pulls the trigger. Evidence: twin/adoption studies show some heritability but strong environmental effects; Moffitt's life-course-persistent offenders combine neuropsychological deficits with adverse environments. Policy: early childhood programmes (nurse home-visiting, Perry Preschool) reduce later offending — nurture can change outcomes.

## 3. Psychological theories

- **Psychoanalytic (Sigmund Freud):** personality = id (instincts), ego (reality), superego (conscience). Crime results from an overactive id or weak superego (guilt-driven crime). Aichhorn applied it to delinquents. Criticism: untestable.
- **Attachment/maternal deprivation (John Bowlby, 1944 "Forty-four juvenile thieves"):** early separation from mother → "affectionless psychopathy". Criticism: overstated; father and other carers matter.
- **Personality theory (Hans Eysenck, 1964):** extraversion, neuroticism, psychoticism + poor conditionability → crime.
- **Moral development (Piaget; Lawrence Kohlberg, 1958):** offenders stuck at pre-conventional reasoning.
- **Behavioural/social learning (B.F. Skinner; Albert Bandura, Bobo doll 1961; *Aggression*, 1973):** aggression learned by observing and imitating models, especially when rewarded.
- **Cognitive theories:** criminal thinking patterns (Yochelson and Samenow), hostile attribution bias.
- **Personality disorders:** antisocial personality disorder; psychopathy (Hare's Psychopathy Checklist, PCL-R).
- **Intelligence:** Hirschi and Hindelang (1977) — low IQ indirect link via school failure; contested.
- **Frustration–aggression hypothesis** (Dollard et al., 1939).
- **Use:** offender assessment, treatment (cognitive-behavioural therapy), profiling.

## 4. Sociological theories — overview

| Family | Theories | Core question |
|---|---|---|
| Social structure | Social disorganisation, strain/anomie, subcultural | Why are crime rates higher in some places/groups? |
| Social process | Learning (differential association, social learning), control (bonds, self-control), neutralisation, labelling | How do individuals become offenders? |
| Social conflict | Marxist, conflict, critical, feminist, left realism | Who makes laws and whom do they serve? |
| Choice/opportunity | Rational choice, routine activity, situational prevention, right realism | Why does crime happen at particular times and places? |
| Integrated/life-course | Sampson and Laub; Moffitt; Agnew | How does offending change over life? |

**Criminological perspectives (2017):** classical, positivist (biological/psychological), sociological, critical/conflict — describe three with authors.

## 5. Social disorganisation theory

- **Chicago School:** Robert Park and Ernest Burgess — **concentric zone model** (1925): city grows in rings; the **zone in transition** (Zone II) near the centre has highest crime.
- **Clifford Shaw and Henry McKay, *Juvenile Delinquency and Urban Areas* (1942):** delinquency rates stayed high in the same inner-city areas even as ethnic groups moved through — so place, not people, explained crime. Causes: poverty, residential mobility, ethnic heterogeneity → weak community institutions and informal social control.
- **Collective efficacy (Robert Sampson et al., 1997):** social cohesion + willingness to intervene reduces crime.
- **Broken windows (James Q. Wilson and George Kelling, *Atlantic Monthly*, 1982):** visible disorder signals no one cares and invites more serious crime → order-maintenance policing.
- **Pakistan:** Karachi's katchi abadis and Lyari; rapid urbanisation, migration and weak municipal services; low collective efficacy where communities are transient.
- **Criticisms:** ecological fallacy; ignores white-collar crime; tautology (disorganisation defined by crime).
- **Note:** the 2024 MCQ asked who proposed social disorganisation theory — the options did not include Shaw and McKay, so the answer was "None of these".

## 6. Strain theory (asked 2022 and 2025)

### Durkheim's anomie
- **Émile Durkheim** introduced *anomie* into sociology: *The Division of Labour in Society* (1893) and *Suicide* (1897).
- Anomie = normlessness — a breakdown of social norms during rapid change, when aspirations are not regulated.
- Crime is normal in every society and has functions (boundary maintenance, social change).

### Robert K. Merton, "Social Structure and Anomie" (1938)
- In the US, culture stresses **success goals** (wealth — the "American Dream") but society limits **legitimate means** (education, jobs) for many. The gap produces **strain**.
- **Five modes of adaptation:**

| Mode | Cultural goals | Institutional means | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Conformity | + | + | Most people |
| **Innovation** | + | − | Theft, fraud, drug dealing, white-collar crime — accepts goals, uses illegitimate means |
| Ritualism | − | + | Bureaucrat who follows rules without hope of success |
| Retreatism | − | − | Drug addicts, vagrants |
| Rebellion | ± (new goals) | ± (new means) | Revolutionaries, militants |

- **Effects on youth (2025):** blocked opportunities (unemployment, poor schools, inequality) push youth to innovation (street crime, cyber fraud), retreatism (drug use), or rebellion (radicalisation). Social media raises aspirations (consumerism) while means stay blocked.
- **Remedies:** education and skills (TEVTA), youth employment schemes (Prime Minister's Youth Programme), microfinance (Akhuwat), reducing inequality, sports and mentoring, social protection (BISP), merit-based opportunities, counselling.
- **Later strain theories:**
  - **Albert Cohen, *Delinquent Boys* (1955):** status frustration → delinquent subculture (non-utilitarian, malicious).
  - **Cloward and Ohlin, *Delinquency and Opportunity* (1960):** differential opportunity — criminal, conflict and retreatist subcultures.
  - **Robert Agnew, General Strain Theory (1992):** strain from failure to achieve goals, removal of positive stimuli, presentation of negative stimuli → negative emotions (anger) → crime.
  - **Messner and Rosenfeld, *Crime and the American Dream* (1994):** institutional anomie — economy dominates family, education, polity.
- **Criticisms:** overpredicts lower-class crime; ignores white-collar crime (though innovation can include it); assumes universal goals; ignores gender; little explanation of why one adaptation is chosen.

## 7. Social control theory

- Asks why people **do not** commit crime.
- **Walter Reckless, containment theory (1961):** inner containment (self-concept) and outer containment (family, community).
- **Sykes and Matza, techniques of neutralisation (1957):** denial of responsibility, denial of injury, denial of the victim, condemnation of the condemners, appeal to higher loyalties. Delinquents "drift" between conventional and deviant behaviour (Matza, 1964).
- **Travis Hirschi, *Causes of Delinquency* (1969) — social bond theory:** four elements: **attachment** (to parents, school, peers), **commitment** (stake in conformity — education, career), **involvement** (busy with conventional activities), **belief** (in moral validity of rules). Weak bonds → delinquency.
- **Gottfredson and Hirschi, *A General Theory of Crime* (1990):** low self-control formed by poor parenting by age 8–10; plus opportunity.
- **Pakistan:** family breakdown in migrant urban settings; school dropout; unemployment lowers commitment; madrassas and sports clubs as bonding institutions.
- **Criticisms:** ignores motivation; bonds with deviant peers can increase crime; self-control theory accused of tautology.

## 8. Learning theory

- **Gabriel Tarde, laws of imitation (1890).**
- **Edwin Sutherland, differential association (1939; final 1947, nine propositions):**
  1. Criminal behaviour is learned.
  2. Learned through interaction with others by communication.
  3. Learned mainly within intimate personal groups.
  4. Includes techniques and motives, drives, rationalisations, attitudes.
  5. Direction of motives learned from definitions of legal codes as favourable or unfavourable.
  6. A person becomes delinquent because of an **excess of definitions favourable to law violation** over unfavourable ones.
  7. Associations vary in frequency, duration, priority and intensity.
  8. Learning criminal behaviour involves the same mechanisms as any learning.
  9. Criminal behaviour expresses general needs and values but is not explained by them.
- **Burgess and Akers, differential reinforcement (1966); Ronald Akers' social learning theory:** differential association, definitions, differential reinforcement (rewards/punishments), imitation.
- **Application — terrorism and the internet (2020):** online echo chambers provide definitions favourable to violence; videos and propaganda offer models to imitate; online status and belonging act as reinforcement; recruiters build intimate personal ties. Response: counter-narratives, de-radicalisation, platform moderation, digital literacy, family and peer engagement (Paigham-e-Pakistan narrative, 2018).
- **Criticisms:** hard to measure definitions; does not explain the first offender; ignores individual differences; impulsive crimes.

## 9. Labelling theory (asked 2024)

- **Frank Tannenbaum, *Crime and the Community* (1938):** "dramatisation of evil" — tagging a youth as bad makes him what he is described as.
- **Edwin Lemert (1951):** **primary deviance** (initial rule-breaking, unlabelled, no change in self-image) vs **secondary deviance** (deviance that follows from being labelled and adopting the deviant identity).
- **Howard Becker, *Outsiders* (1963):** "deviance is not a quality of the act… but a consequence of the application by others of rules and sanctions"; **moral entrepreneurs** create rules; master status.
- **Erving Goffman, *Stigma* (1963)** — spoiled identity.
- **John Braithwaite, *Crime, Shame and Reintegration* (1989):** **reintegrative shaming** (disapprove the act, accept the person) reduces crime; **disintegrative (stigmatising) shaming** increases it.
- **Effects on youth:** stigma → exclusion from school and jobs → deviant peer groups → self-fulfilling prophecy; police targeting of "known" youths; criminal records; media naming of juveniles.
- **Remedies:** diversion from formal processing (JJSA 2018 — juvenile justice committees for minor offences); confidentiality of juvenile identity (JJSA prohibits disclosure **[verify section]**); restorative justice; expunging records; community service; avoid jailing juveniles with adults; reintegrative programmes.
- **Criticisms:** does not explain primary deviance; ignores the victim and real harm; some labelling deters; overstated determinism.

## 10. Conflict and critical theories (asked 2026)

- **Karl Marx:** did not write a theory of crime but saw law as an instrument of the ruling class; crime as a product of capitalism (alienation, inequality).
- **Willem Bonger, *Criminality and Economic Conditions* (1916):** capitalism encourages egoism.
- **George Vold, *Theoretical Criminology* (1958):** group conflict — law reflects interests of groups with power.
- **Austin Turk (1969)**, **Richard Quinney (*The Social Reality of Crime*, 1970)**, **William Chambliss** (law of vagrancy; "The Saints and the Roughnecks", 1973 — class bias in labelling).
- **Core claims:** laws are made by the powerful; enforcement targets the poor; white-collar and state crimes are under-punished; inequality causes crime.
- **Relevance to Pakistan (2026):** high inequality and elite capture; VIP culture and selective enforcement ("thana culture"); land grabbing and feudal power over police; poor fill prisons (75% under-trial, many unable to pay bail); elite evasion of tax and accountability; ethnic and regional grievances. **Limits:** much crime (domestic violence, honour killing, terrorism) cuts across class; religious and tribal norms, not just economics; Pakistan's crime patterns also shaped by conflict spill-over and weak institutions.
- **Left realism (Lea and Young, *What Is to Be Done About Law and Order?*, 1984):** takes crime seriously, especially its harm to the poor; **relative deprivation**, subculture and marginalisation cause crime; "square of crime" (offender, victim, state, public); favours democratic, community-accountable policing.
- **Right realism (James Q. Wilson, *Thinking About Crime*, 1975; Charles Murray's underclass):** crime from individual choice, weak morality and family; favours deterrence, incapacitation, broken-windows policing.
- **Feminist criminology:** Carol Smart (1976); Meda Chesney-Lind; gender gap; women as victims; chivalry hypothesis; patriarchy.

## 11. Symbolic interactionism (asked 2026)

- **George Herbert Mead; Herbert Blumer (1969):** people act on meanings; meanings arise from interaction and are modified through interpretation; the self develops through role-taking ("looking-glass self" — Cooley).
- **Link to crime:** labelling, differential association and neutralisation all draw on it.
- **Polarisation and hate crimes among youth (2026):** in-group/out-group meanings built through interaction (online and offline); dehumanising labels ("traitor", "infidel", "agent") turn opponents into legitimate targets; repeated interaction in echo chambers reinforces hostile definitions; youth seek identity and status by enacting group norms; symbols (slogans, flags) carry meaning. Example contexts: political polarisation after 2022; sectarian and religious violence (mob attacks on alleged blasphemers, e.g. Jaranwala, August 2023); ethnic tensions. **Responses:** counter-narratives, contact between groups, media literacy, responsible political rhetoric, legal action against hate speech (PPC s.153A, s.505; PECA), school curricula on tolerance.

## 12. Choice and opportunity theories

- **Rational choice (Cornish and Clarke, 1986):** offenders weigh costs and benefits; bounded rationality.
- **Routine activity theory (Lawrence Cohen and Marcus Felson, 1979):** crime occurs when a **motivated offender**, a **suitable target** and the **absence of a capable guardian** converge in time and space.
- **Situational crime prevention (Ronald Clarke):** increase effort, increase risk, reduce rewards, reduce provocations, remove excuses (25 techniques).
- **Crime pattern theory (Brantingham and Brantingham):** environmental criminology — crime locations and patterns (2026 MCQ).
- **Deterrence:** certainty, severity, celerity.

## 13. Life-course theories

- **Sampson and Laub, *Crime in the Making* (1993):** turning points (marriage, employment, military) promote desistance.
- **Terrie Moffitt (1993):** adolescence-limited vs life-course-persistent offenders.

## 14. Islamic perspective on deviance and crime (asked 2018)

**Basic ideas:**
- Human beings have a natural disposition (*fitrah*) toward good but are tempted by *nafs* and Shaytan; free will and accountability.
- Crime violates the rights of God (*huquq Allah*) and/or the rights of people (*huquq al-ibad*).
- **Maqasid al-Shariah** (objectives of Islamic law, al-Ghazali, al-Shatibi): protection of religion (*din*), life (*nafs*), intellect (*'aql*), lineage (*nasl*) and property (*mal*). Crimes are attacks on these.
- Prevention first: moral education, family, prayer, zakat (reducing poverty), prohibition of intoxicants, promoting good and forbidding evil (*amr bil-ma'ruf*), *hisbah*.

**Categories of punishment:**
| Category | Meaning | Offences |
|---|---|---|
| **Hudud** | Fixed punishments prescribed by Qur'an/Sunnah | Zina (illicit sex), qazf (false accusation of zina), sariqa (theft), hirabah (highway robbery/armed disorder), shurb (drinking); apostasy and rebellion are debated |
| **Qisas** | Retaliation in kind | Murder and bodily injury |
| **Diyat** | Compensation/blood money in lieu of qisas | Same; victim's heirs may forgive |
| **Ta'zir** | Discretionary punishments by the state | All other offences |

**Features:** very strict standards of proof for hudud (four witnesses for zina; confession can be withdrawn); "avert hudud in cases of doubt" (*idra'u al-hudud bil-shubuhat*); repentance (*tawbah*) and forgiveness; restorative elements (diyat, victim's role); justice equal for all ("if Fatima daughter of Muhammad stole…" — hadith on equality before law); Caliph Umar suspended the hadd for theft during the famine (Year of Ashes) — context and necessity matter.

**Examples to cite:** the Prophet's handling of the Makhzumi woman's theft (equality); Umar's famine suspension; Qur'anic verse 5:32 on the sanctity of life; 4:135 on justice even against oneself.

**Pakistan:** Hudood Ordinances 1979 (Zia); Qisas and Diyat Ordinance 1990 (made permanent 1997) — criticised because heirs can forgive killers in honour crimes; 2016 honour-killing amendment limited forgiveness for honour killings to reduce impunity; Protection of Women (Criminal Laws Amendment) Act 2006 moved rape out of the Hudood regime into the PPC; Federal Shariat Court hears hudood appeals; Abolition of the Punishment of Whipping Act 1996 abolished whipping except for hadd.

**Comparative note:** Islamic criminal law shares with classical criminology the idea of deterrence and responsibility; with restorative justice the victim's role and forgiveness; and with social-structure theories the emphasis on removing causes (poverty, injustice).

## 15. Applying theories to new questions (2026 practice)

| Problem | Theories to use |
|---|---|
| Misinformation/disinformation in political conflict | Moral panic (Cohen), labelling, social learning (echo chambers), routine activity (motivated actors, suitable targets — credulous audiences, absent guardians — weak platform moderation), conflict theory (power interests), neutralisation |
| Hate crimes and polarisation | Symbolic interactionism, social identity theory (Tajfel), strain (status frustration), differential association |
| AI and future crime | Routine activity (new targets, weaker guardians), rational choice (low cost, high reward), techno-crime; strain from AI job loss; conflict (surveillance by powerful) |
| Declining trust in institutions → deviance | Legitimacy and procedural justice (Tom Tyler, *Why People Obey the Law*, 1990), anomie, conflict, control (weak belief bond) |
| Urban crime | Social disorganisation, broken windows, routine activity, situational prevention, CPTED |
