## 1. Basic concepts

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Crime** | An act or omission prohibited by law and punishable by the state. Legal definition (Tappan, 1947): an intentional act in violation of criminal law, committed without defence or excuse, and penalised by the state |
| **Criminality** | The tendency or quality of committing crime; also the overall level of crime in a society |
| **Criminal behaviour** | Conduct that violates criminal law; studied for its causes, patterns and control |
| **Criminal** | A person convicted of a crime (strict legal sense); loosely, a person who commits crime |
| **Actus reus** | The guilty act (physical element) |
| **Mens rea** | The guilty mind (intent, knowledge, recklessness, negligence) |
| **Strict liability** | Liability without proof of mens rea (e.g. food adulteration, traffic offences) |
| **Mala in se** | Acts wrong in themselves (murder, rape, theft) |
| **Mala prohibita** | Acts wrong because prohibited (traffic rules, licensing) |
| **Victim / victimology** | The person harmed; study of victims (Hans von Hentig, Benjamin Mendelsohn — "father of victimology") |
| **Penology** | Study of punishment, prisons and treatment of offenders |
| **Criminal etiology** | Study of the causes of crime |
| **Sociology of law** | Study of law as a social institution and of how law creates and controls crime (Sutherland's three divisions of criminology) |
| **Recidivism** | Re-offending after punishment or release |
| **Felony / misdemeanour** | Serious vs minor offences (US terms); in Pakistan: cognizable vs non-cognizable, bailable vs non-bailable, compoundable vs non-compoundable (CrPC) |
| **Index crimes** | The serious offences reported in the US FBI Uniform Crime Reports (Part I: murder, rape, robbery, aggravated assault, burglary, larceny-theft, motor vehicle theft, arson). Fraud is not an index crime |
| **Dark figure of crime** | Crime that is never reported or recorded |

**Measuring crime:** official statistics (police records — in the US the UCR since 1930 and NIBRS), victimisation surveys (US NCVS; Crime Survey for England and Wales), self-report studies. Each has bias: police data reflect reporting and recording practices; surveys miss victimless crime. **Pakistan:** provincial police publish FIR-based statistics; under-reporting is severe (refusal to register FIRs, fear, informal settlement); no national victimisation survey.

## 2. Definition and meaning of criminology

- The word comes from Latin *crimen* (accusation, crime) and Greek *logos* (study). French anthropologist **Paul Topinard** used it first (1879); Italian jurist **Raffaele Garofalo** popularised it in the title of his book *Criminologia* (1885).
- **Edwin Sutherland:** "the body of knowledge regarding crime as a social phenomenon. It includes within its scope the processes of making laws, of breaking laws, and of reacting toward the breaking of laws." Three divisions: sociology of law, criminal etiology, penology.
- **Larry Siegel:** the scientific approach to studying criminal behaviour.
- Criminology is interdisciplinary: sociology, psychology, biology, law, economics, political science, forensic science, statistics.

**"Fathers" (common MCQ):**
- **Cesare Beccaria** — father of classical criminology, *On Crimes and Punishments* (1764).
- **Cesare Lombroso** — father of modern (positivist) criminology, *L'uomo delinquente* (1876). FPSC treats Lombroso as "father of criminology/modern criminology" in its MCQs; Beccaria is the father of the classical school.
- **Edwin Sutherland** — leading American criminologist; coined "white-collar crime" (1939).

## 3. Nature and scope of criminology

**Nature:**
1. A social science that uses scientific method (observation, hypothesis, data).
2. Interdisciplinary.
3. Both theoretical (explains causes) and applied (policy, policing, corrections).
4. Dynamic — new crimes (cyber, crypto, AI) change its content.
5. Value-laden questions (what should be crime?) sit beside empirical ones.

**Scope (areas):**
1. **Criminal statistics** — measuring crime.
2. **Sociology of law** — how laws are made and enforced.
3. **Theory construction / etiology** — causes of crime.
4. **Criminal behaviour systems** — types of crime (violent, property, white-collar, organised, cyber).
5. **Penology** — punishment and corrections.
6. **Victimology.**
7. **Juvenile delinquency.**
8. **Policing and criminal justice studies.**
9. **Forensic science and criminal investigation** (2023 question framed criminology as covering criminal law, forensics and investigation).
10. **Crime prevention** — situational, social, community.
11. **Comparative and transnational criminology** — terrorism, trafficking, money laundering.

**Branches:** criminal anthropology, criminal sociology, criminal psychology, criminal psychiatry, penology, victimology, criminalistics/forensics, criminal policy, cyber criminology, green criminology, feminist criminology.

## 4. Criminology and criminal law

| Criminal law | Criminology |
|---|---|
| Normative: defines offences and punishments | Empirical: studies why crime occurs and how society reacts |
| Concerned with guilt of an individual | Concerned with patterns, causes, prevention |
| Sources: PPC 1860, CrPC 1898, Qanun-e-Shahadat 1984, special laws | Sources: research, data, theories |
| Practitioners: judges, lawyers | Practitioners: researchers, policy-makers, police, correctional staff |

**Links:** criminal law defines what criminology studies; criminology informs criminal law reform (e.g. evidence on deterrence, juvenile justice, restorative justice). Principles of criminal law: legality (*nullum crimen sine lege*; Art. 12 of the Constitution — protection against retrospective punishment), mens rea, proportionality, presumption of innocence, fair trial (Art. 10A).

**Pakistan's criminal law framework:** Pakistan Penal Code 1860 (amended — Qisas and Diyat 1990/1997, Hudood 1979, Protection of Women Act 2006, honour killing 2016, Anti-Rape 2021); Code of Criminal Procedure 1898; Qanun-e-Shahadat Order 1984; Anti-Terrorism Act 1997; Control of Narcotic Substances Act 1997; Prevention of Electronic Crimes Act 2016; Anti-Money Laundering Act 2010; Juvenile Justice System Act 2018.

## 5. Crime as a social problem (2022, 2024)

**Social problem:** a condition that harms many people and that society believes can and should be changed (objective harm + subjective definition).

**Why crime is a social problem:**
- Harms victims (physical, financial, psychological).
- Fear of crime restricts freedom, especially of women.
- Economic cost: lost investment, security spending, insurance; Karachi's street crime and kidnapping deter business.
- Erodes trust and social cohesion.
- Linked to other problems: poverty, unemployment, drugs, illiteracy, corruption.
- Overloads courts and prisons (Pakistan prisons at about 161% occupancy, Oct 2026).

**Perspectives:**
- *Functionalist* (Durkheim): some crime is normal and even functional — it defines moral boundaries and can trigger change; too much indicates anomie.
- *Conflict:* law reflects the interests of the powerful; crime is a product of inequality.
- *Symbolic interactionist:* crime is a label applied through interaction.
- *Constructionist:* what counts as a crime problem depends on claims-makers, media and politics (moral panics — Stanley Cohen, 1972).

**How perceptions vary across societies (2024):**
- *Legal-cultural differences:* alcohol is criminal in Pakistan, legal in the West; blasphemy laws; adultery criminal in some states; drug policies (Portugal decriminalised personal use in 2001).
- *Religion and values:* sin and crime overlap in religious societies.
- *Development level:* property and street crime salient in cities; honour crimes and land disputes in rural Pakistan.
- *Media and politics:* crime waves can be constructed.
- *Trust in police:* low trust → under-reporting → the problem seems smaller in statistics but larger in public perception.

**Crime as a social problem in Pakistan:** street crime in Karachi and Lahore; terrorism in KP and Balochistan; violence against women (rape, honour killing, domestic violence); child abuse (Kasur 2015, Zainab 2018); drug abuse; kidnapping for ransom in the katcha areas of Sindh and Punjab; cyber crime and harassment; corruption; overcrowded prisons; low conviction rates. Contributing factors: poverty, unemployment, urbanisation, weak policing, slow justice, illiteracy, easy access to weapons, drugs, Afghan war spill-over, political patronage of criminals.

## 6. Deviance, sin, vice, evil, norms, values (2022)

| Term | Meaning | Example | Relation to crime |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Norms** | Rules of expected behaviour. Folkways (customs, mild sanction), mores (moral norms, strong sanction), laws (formal norms enforced by the state) — W.G. Sumner, 1906 | Greeting elders; not lying; not stealing | Crime = violation of laws |
| **Values** | Shared ideas of what is good and desirable | Honesty, family, religion | Norms put values into practice |
| **Deviance** | Behaviour that violates social norms (formal or informal) | Tattoos in conservative circles, eccentric dress, suicide attempts | Broader than crime; not all deviance is crime; most crime is deviance |
| **Sin** | Violation of religious or divine law | Missing prayers; lying | Some sins are crimes (theft, zina in Pakistani law), most are not |
| **Vice** | Immoral habit or practice, often harming oneself | Gambling, drinking, prostitution | Many vices are "victimless crimes" criminalised in some societies |
| **Evil** | Extreme moral wrong causing great harm | Genocide, torture | Philosophical/religious concept; may or may not be crime |
| **Crime** | Violation of criminal law, punished by the state | Murder, robbery | Legal concept |

**Key point:** crime is a legal category, deviance a social one, sin a religious one, vice a moral one. They overlap but are not the same. Example: alcohol use is a sin in Islam, a vice morally, a crime in Pakistan, and merely deviant (or normal) elsewhere.

**Theories of deviance:** Durkheim (deviance is normal); Merton (strain); Becker ("deviant behaviour is behaviour that people so label"); Erikson (boundary maintenance).

## 7. Security: physical, social, economic

- **Physical security:** protection of persons and property from harm — policing, target hardening, CCTV, safe cities.
- **Social security:** protection against social risks — illness, old age, unemployment; and a sense of belonging and order in community. Social safety nets (BISP) reduce strain that can lead to crime.
- **Economic security:** stable income and resources; poverty and inequality are linked to property crime (strain and conflict theories).
- **Human security** (UNDP Human Development Report 1994): freedom from fear and freedom from want — seven dimensions: economic, food, health, environmental, personal, community, political.
- **Criminological link:** insecurity in one domain feeds crime in others; crime prevention must address social and economic security, not only policing.

## 8. Importance of criminology in Pakistan (2017, 2018, 2025)

1. **High and changing crime:** terrorism, street crime, cyber crime, gender-based violence, child abuse.
2. **Evidence-based policy:** understanding causes enables prevention (e.g. youth programmes, urban design).
3. **Police reform:** training in investigation, community policing, human rights; reducing custodial torture (Torture and Custodial Death (Prevention and Punishment) Act 2022).
4. **Criminal justice efficiency:** low conviction rates and delays; prosecution and forensic capacity.
5. **Corrections:** overcrowded prisons, three-quarters under-trial (NCHR/JPP, Oct 2026); need for probation, parole, rehabilitation.
6. **Juvenile justice:** implementation of JJSA 2018.
7. **Counter-terrorism and de-radicalisation:** understanding radicalisation pathways (Sabaoon centre in Swat).
8. **Victim support:** gender-based violence centres, child protection.
9. **Cyber and financial crime:** FATF compliance, PECA, money laundering.
10. **Academic growth:** criminology departments (University of Karachi; University of Peshawar; others) and the Pakistan Society of Criminology (founded 2009 **[verify]**).

**History of criminology as a discipline (2017):**
1. *Demonological era* — crime as sin or possession; trial by ordeal.
2. *Classical school* (18th c.): Beccaria (1764), Bentham (hedonistic calculus, panopticon) — free will, rational choice, proportionate and certain punishment; influenced the French Penal Code (1791).
3. *Neo-classical school*: allowed mitigating factors (age, insanity) — French Code of 1810/1819.
4. *Cartographic/statistical school*: Adolphe Quetelet and André-Michel Guerry (1830s) — crime rates and social conditions.
5. *Positivist school* (late 19th c.): Lombroso (born criminal, atavism), Enrico Ferri (social factors), Garofalo (natural crime) — determinism, scientific method, treatment.
6. *Sociological era* (20th c.): Durkheim; Chicago School (1920s–40s); Merton (1938); Sutherland (1939).
7. *Critical era* (1960s–70s): labelling, conflict, Marxist, feminist criminology.
8. *Contemporary*: realism (left and right), rational choice, routine activity, life-course, environmental criminology, cyber and green criminology, evidence-based policing.
