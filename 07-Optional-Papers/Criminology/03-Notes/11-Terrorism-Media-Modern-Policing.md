## 1. Terrorism

**Definitions:**
- No agreed UN definition. Common elements: violence or threat of violence; against civilians/non-combatants; to intimidate a population or coerce a government; for political, religious or ideological aims.
- **UNSC Resolution 1566 (2004)** gives a working description.
- **Pakistan's Anti-Terrorism Act 1997, s.6:** action or threat designed to coerce or intimidate the government or public, or create a sense of fear or insecurity, or advance a religious, sectarian or ethnic cause, involving serious violence, damage, kidnapping, attacks on officials, etc. The Supreme Court in ***Ghulam Hussain v. State* (2019/2020)** clarified that terrorism requires the design/purpose of the act, not merely its effect of creating fear **[verify citation]**.

**Types:** religious/sectarian; ethno-nationalist/separatist (Baloch militant groups); left/right-wing; state-sponsored; lone-actor; cyber-terrorism; narco-terrorism.

**Explanations:** relative deprivation and grievance; identity and humiliation; group dynamics; rational strategic choice (Pape on suicide terrorism); social learning and networks (Sageman's "bunch of guys"); religious and ideological framing; state failure and ungoverned spaces; foreign interference and proxy wars.

### Radicalisation (2024, 2025)
- **Meaning:** a process by which a person comes to adopt extremist beliefs and may come to support or use violence.
- **Models:** Moghaddam's "staircase to terrorism" (2005) — perceived injustice → displaced aggression → moral engagement with terrorist group → categorical us-vs-them thinking → violent act; McCauley and Moskalenko's two pyramids (opinion and action); NYPD model (pre-radicalisation, self-identification, indoctrination, jihadisation).
- **Push factors:** poverty, unemployment, marginalisation, injustice, state repression, identity crisis, sectarian discrimination, trauma.
- **Pull factors:** ideology, belonging, status, adventure, money, charismatic recruiters, online propaganda.
- **Enablers:** madrassas without oversight (a minority), online echo chambers, weapons, conflict zones (Afghanistan), prisons.

**Is radicalism present in Pakistani society and how does it escalate to terrorism? (2025)**
- *Evidence:* mob violence over blasphemy allegations (Mashal Khan, 2017; Sialkot lynching of a Sri Lankan manager, December 2021; Jaranwala church attacks, August 2023); TLP-led violent protests (2017, 2021); sectarian killings (Kurram clashes 2024 **[verify]**); TTP resurgence after 2021; Baloch militant attacks (Jaffar Express hijacking, March 2025 **[verify]**).
- *Escalation path:* exposure to extremist narratives → grievance framing and dehumanisation of "others" → group membership (online/offline) → justification of violence by religious or ethnic framing → training and opportunity → attacks. Political patronage and state ambivalence toward some groups in the past enabled escalation.
- *Data:* Pakistan ranked **2nd** in the Global Terrorism Index 2025 (Institute for Economics and Peace) **[check GTI 2026 rank]**.

### Role of law enforcement agencies (2017)
- *Police and CTDs:* first responders; intelligence-based operations; investigations; prosecutions in ATCs.
- *Civil armed forces (FC, Rangers):* operations under Article 245 / aid of civil power; Karachi operation (2013–) reduced target killings and extortion.
- *Intelligence agencies (ISI, IB, MI):* intelligence-based operations.
- *FIA:* terror financing, cyber, cross-border.
- *Military:* large-scale operations.
- **Problems:** coordination (NACTA weak), CTD capacity, low conviction rates in ATCs, witness and judge intimidation, police casualties, human-rights concerns (enforced disappearances), political ambivalence, Afghan sanctuary.

### Reducing radicalisation (2017, 2024)
1. Education reform: critical thinking, tolerance, regulated madrassa curricula.
2. Economic inclusion: youth jobs and skills in vulnerable regions (ex-FATA, Balochistan).
3. Good governance and justice: address grievances; end enforced disappearances; fair trials.
4. Counter-narratives through scholars, media, social media (Paigham-e-Pakistan).
5. Online regulation with rights safeguards; digital literacy.
6. De-radicalisation and reintegration programmes; prison de-radicalisation.
7. Community engagement: families, religious leaders, community policing.
8. Strengthen CTDs, prosecution and witness protection; terror-financing enforcement.
9. Regional cooperation (Afghanistan; SCO RATS).
10. Inclusion of women and youth in prevention (UNSC Res. 1325 and 2250).

### War on Terror
- After 9/11 (2001), Pakistan joined the US-led coalition; operations in tribal areas; drone strikes (2004–2018); blowback — TTP formed 2007; Pakistan suffered tens of thousands of civilian and security deaths and large economic losses (Economic Survey estimates over $120bn **[verify]**). US withdrawal from Afghanistan (August 2021) and Taliban takeover were followed by a rise in TTP attacks in Pakistan; tensions with Kabul over TTP sanctuaries.

## 2. Media's representation of crime and the criminal justice system

- **Newsworthiness:** "if it bleeds, it leads"; violent, unusual and sexual crimes over-reported; property and white-collar crime under-reported.
- **Effects:**
  - Fear of crime out of proportion to risk (Gerbner's **cultivation theory** — "mean world syndrome").
  - **Moral panics** (Stanley Cohen, *Folk Devils and Moral Panics*, 1972): media exaggeration → public alarm → harsh laws.
  - Stereotypes of offenders and victims (ethnic, class).
  - **CSI effect** — juries/public expect forensic evidence.
  - Media trials — prejudice fair trial (Art. 10A); naming juveniles and rape victims violates law.
  - Copycat crime and terrorism's "oxygen of publicity" (Margaret Thatcher's phrase).
  - Positive roles: exposing injustice and police abuse (CCTV/mobile videos), mobilising reform (Zainab case 2018; Motorway case 2020), helping find suspects.
- **Pakistan:** sensational crime shows and reenactments; PEMRA codes on coverage of terrorism and victims; social media spreads rumours (child-kidnapping rumours causing lynchings); journalists themselves at risk.

### Misinformation and disinformation (2026)
- **Misinformation:** false information spread without intent to deceive. **Disinformation:** deliberately false, spread to deceive. **Malinformation:** true information used to harm.
- **Criminological reasons for their rise during political conflict:**
  1. *Rational choice/routine activity:* low cost, high reach, anonymity, weak guardianship (platform moderation), suitable targets (polarised, low-literacy audiences).
  2. *Strain and anomie:* distrust and frustration create appetite for alternative "truths".
  3. *Social learning and differential association:* echo chambers reinforce and reward sharing.
  4. *Labelling and moral panic:* opponents labelled as traitors or enemies.
  5. *Conflict theory:* powerful actors (states, parties) use information as a weapon.
  6. *Neutralisation:* "everyone does it", "they lie too".
  7. *Profit motive:* click-bait monetisation; troll farms.
- **Pakistan responses:** PECA 2016 and its **January 2025 amendment** — criminalised spreading "fake or false" information likely to cause fear or unrest (up to three years), set up a social media regulatory authority and a National Cyber Crime Investigation Agency; criticised by journalists and rights groups as a threat to free expression ([Amnesty, Jan 2025](https://www.amnesty.org/en/latest/news/2025/01/pakistan-authorities-pass-bill-with-sweeping-controls-on-social-media/); NCHR report 2026). Balanced response: fact-checking, media literacy, platform accountability, transparency by the state, narrow and clear laws.

## 3. Modern law enforcement and crime prevention

### (a) Intelligence-led policing (ILP) (2018, 2021)
- **Background:** UK — Audit Commission's *Helping with Enquiries* (1993) and Kent Constabulary under Sir David Phillips (1990s); **National Intelligence Model (NIM)** (2000); US adoption after 9/11 (fusion centres).
- **Definition (Jerry Ratcliffe):** a business model and managerial philosophy where data analysis and crime intelligence are pivotal to objective decision-making that facilitates crime and problem reduction, disruption and prevention through strategic management and effective enforcement targeting prolific and serious offenders.
- **Key principles:** intelligence drives operations (not reacting to calls); focus on prolific offenders and organised groups; analysis (crime pattern, network, risk); tasking and coordination meetings; information sharing across agencies; prevention and disruption as well as arrest; evaluation.
- **Ratcliffe's 3-i model:** **interpret** the criminal environment → **influence** decision-makers → **impact** the criminal environment.
- **Against organised crime:** target networks, finances, supply chains; asset seizure.
- **Pakistan:** CTDs' intelligence-based operations; Punjab's crime analytics and Safe City; FIA–FMU financial intelligence; the need for databases and analysts.
- **Criticisms:** privacy and civil liberties; data quality; over-focus on enforcement; analysts' influence limited; bias in data.

### (b) Community policing (2022, 2024)
- **Meaning:** a philosophy that promotes partnerships between police and community and proactive problem-solving to address the conditions that give rise to crime, disorder and fear of crime.
- **Origins:** Robert Peel's principles (Metropolitan Police, **1829** — "the police are the public and the public are the police"; first modern police force in England); US community policing movement of the 1980s–90s; Herman Goldstein's **problem-oriented policing** (1979, 1990) and the **SARA** model — **S**canning, **A**nalysis (a working definition of the problem is formed), **R**esponse, **A**ssessment.
- **Core elements:** community partnership; problem solving; organisational transformation (decentralisation, geographic beat responsibility, foot patrol, less reliance on patrol cars — prevention emphasis and geographic focus, 2024 MCQ); trust and legitimacy.
- **Benefits:** better intelligence; reduced fear of crime; legitimacy and cooperation (procedural justice); early identification of problems; prevention of radicalisation; improved police image.
- **Challenges in Pakistan:** colonial, control-oriented culture; low trust; politicisation; staff shortages and transfers; corruption; lack of training and incentives; elite capture of community bodies; security threats; women's exclusion.
- **Pakistani examples:** KP's **Dispute Resolution Councils** (2014) and police–community liaison; Citizen-Police Liaison Committee (**CPLC**, Karachi, 1989) — citizen body that tracks kidnapping and vehicle theft; Punjab's community policing and Khidmat Markaz; Islamabad's community policing initiatives; Motorway Police's citizen-friendly image.
- **Addressing challenges:** legal backing (Public Safety Commissions under Police Order 2002); training and performance indicators for community engagement; beat officers with stable tenure; women officers and desks; transparent complaint systems; use of technology (apps, social media); inclusive community forums.

### (c) Private–public partnership in crime prevention
- **Meaning:** cooperation between police and private actors — private security companies, businesses, banks, telecoms, tech firms, NGOs.
- **Examples:** CPLC Karachi; Safe City projects with tech vendors; banks and FMU for suspicious transactions; telecom data for investigations; business improvement districts; private security guards (licensed by provincial home departments); joint traffic and road-safety campaigns.
- **Benefits:** resources, technology, expertise, coverage. **Risks:** accountability gaps, unequal protection (rich areas better protected), data privacy, profit motive, unregulated private security.

### (d) Other modern approaches
- **Hot-spot policing** (Sherman and Weisburd, Minneapolis 1995) — focus on small high-crime places.
- **CompStat** (New York, 1994) — data-driven management accountability.
- **Broken-windows/order-maintenance policing** — reduce disorder.
- **Predictive policing** — algorithms forecast crime (bias concerns).
- **CPTED (Crime Prevention Through Environmental Design)** — C. Ray Jeffery (1971); Oscar Newman's *Defensible Space* (1972): natural surveillance, access control, territorial reinforcement, maintenance.
- **Situational crime prevention** (Clarke) — 25 techniques.
- **Evidence-based policing** (Sherman, 1998).

## 4. Crime prevention in densely populated urban areas of Pakistan (2023, 2026)

**Problem:** street crime (mobile snatching, motorcycle theft, robbery) in Karachi, Lahore, Rawalpindi, Faisalabad; dacoities; drug peddling; youth gangs. Drivers: urban poverty and unemployment, migration, slums, drug use, weak policing, easy access to weapons, poor street lighting, CCTV gaps, slow justice.

**Strategies and feasibility:**
| Strategy | Feasibility | Effectiveness |
|---|---|---|
| Hot-spot patrolling with crime mapping | High — data exist (Safe City, 15 calls) | Strong evidence of reductions without displacement |
| Safe City CCTV, ANPR, e-tagging of motorcycles | Medium — costly; maintenance issues | Good for detection; deterrence when paired with response |
| Rapid-response units (e.g. Lahore's Dolphin Force, Karachi's Madadgar 15) | Medium | Improves response; risk of excessive force |
| CPTED: lighting, clearing encroachments, design of markets and transit | Medium — needs municipal capacity | Good long-term prevention |
| Community policing, mohalla committees, CPLC-type partnerships | High | Improves intelligence and trust |
| Youth programmes, skills, sports, drug rehabilitation | Medium — needs funding | Addresses root causes (strain, control) |
| Tackling stolen-phone markets (PTA's DIRBS blocking of IMEIs) | High | Reduces rewards (situational prevention) |
| Swift prosecution and trial for street crime; repeat-offender tracking | Medium | Increases certainty of punishment |
| Weapons control (licence audits, recovery drives) | Medium | Reduces lethality |
| Inter-agency coordination (police, Rangers, municipal bodies) | Medium | Essential in Karachi |
| Avoid extrajudicial "encounters" as a strategy | — | Undermine legitimacy; human-rights violations (Punjab CCD 2025–26 controversy) |
