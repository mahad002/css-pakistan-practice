# III. Crime and Criminals

## 1. Why classify criminals and crimes? (typology)

- **Typology:** grouping offenders or offences by shared features to explain causes and design responses.
- **Focus of typology (2021):** offender characteristics (age, career, motivation, psychology), offence characteristics (violent, property, public order), the criminal career (onset, frequency, duration, desistance), the social reaction, and the opportunity structure.
- **Uses:** theory building; sentencing (habitual offender laws); correctional classification (risk levels); policing priorities; prevention design.
- **Limits:** people commit mixed offences (versatility); labels stigmatise; categories overlap.
- **Classic typologies:**
  - Lombroso: born criminal, insane criminal, criminaloid, criminal by passion, occasional criminal.
  - Ferri: born, insane, habitual, occasional, passionate.
  - Clinard and Quinney, *Criminal Behavior Systems* (1967): violent personal, occasional property, public order, conventional, political, occupational, corporate, organised, professional.
  - Moffitt (1993): adolescence-limited vs life-course-persistent offenders.
  - Legal classification: offences against the person, property, the state, public tranquillity, religion, etc. (PPC chapters).

## 2. Occasional criminals

- Commit crime rarely, in response to a situational opportunity or pressure; not committed to a criminal lifestyle.
- Examples: shoplifting on impulse, a fight leading to injury, a one-time fraud under financial pressure, crimes of passion.
- Features: little skill; self-image as non-criminal; often first offenders; low recidivism if handled well.
- Explanations: situational/rational choice, strain, neutralisation techniques (Sykes and Matza).
- Response: diversion, probation, restorative justice; avoid labelling (prison may turn them into habitual offenders).

## 3. Habitual criminals

- Repeatedly commit crimes and are repeatedly convicted; crime has become a habit.
- Often petty property offenders, drug users, vagrants; poor skills; frequently imprisoned ("revolving door").
- Causes: early delinquency, addiction, labelling and stigma, lack of employment after release, prison socialisation ("schools of crime").
- Legal responses: enhanced punishment for repeat offenders (habitual offender laws; US "three strikes" laws), security for good behaviour (CrPC s.110 — habitual offenders can be bound over), registers of history-sheeters (Police Rules).
- Rehabilitation: treatment for addiction, vocational training, aftercare.

## 4. Professional criminals

- Treat crime as an occupation; use specialised skills; earn a living from crime; have status in the criminal world.
- **Sutherland, *The Professional Thief* (1937):** based on the account of a thief ("Chic Conwell"): tutelage (learning from experienced criminals), skills, status, consensus (code of conduct), differential association, organisation (contacts with "fixers", fences, corrupt officials).
- Examples: pickpockets, con-artists, safe-crackers, car-theft rings, card skimmers, professional hackers.
- Differences from habitual criminals: skill, planning, low arrest rates, avoidance of violence.
- Response: specialised investigation units, intelligence-led policing, asset confiscation.

## 5. White-collar crime

- **Edwin Sutherland**, presidential address to the American Sociological Society (1939); book *White Collar Crime* (1949): "a crime committed by a person of respectability and high social status in the course of his occupation".
- **Features:** non-violent; committed through trust and position; complex; low visibility — hidden within normal business transactions; victims diffuse or unaware; offenders have resources to defend themselves; lenient treatment (administrative fines rather than prison).
- **Types:** occupational crime (employee theft, embezzlement), professional misconduct (medical fraud), corporate crime, state-corporate crime, tax evasion, insider trading, bribery and corruption, procurement fraud, environmental crime.
- **Explanations:** differential association (learned in business culture); opportunity; strain ("anomie of affluence"); rational choice; organisational culture; low self-control (Gottfredson and Hirschi, contested).
- **Pakistan examples:** sugar and wheat crises inquiries (2020); fake bank accounts case (2018); housing society frauds; tax evasion; medicine price manipulation; NAB plea bargains. Institutions: NAB, FIA (economic crime wing), SECP, FBR, Competition Commission, State Bank.
- **Why hard to control:** complex evidence; powerful offenders; political links; slow courts; weak regulators; public tolerance.

## 6. Organized crime

- **UN Convention against Transnational Organized Crime (Palermo, 2000):** an organised criminal group is a structured group of three or more persons, existing for a period of time and acting in concert to commit serious crimes for financial or material benefit. Pakistan ratified UNTOC in 2010.
- **Features:** continuity; hierarchy or network; use of violence and corruption; division of labour; monopoly of illicit markets; secrecy; money laundering.
- **Models:** bureaucratic/hierarchical (Cressey's Cosa Nostra model, 1969), patron–client (local "godfathers"), network/enterprise model (flexible cells — modern view).
- **Activities:** drug trafficking, human trafficking and migrant smuggling, arms smuggling, extortion, kidnapping for ransom, cyber fraud, counterfeiting, smuggling of goods (fuel, cigarettes), land grabbing (*qabza* mafia).
- **Pakistan:** drug trafficking from Afghanistan; human smuggling to Europe (the June 2023 boat disaster off Greece killed hundreds of Pakistanis **[verify number]**); Karachi gangs (Lyari gang war); katcha-area dacoits (Sindh–Punjab riverine belt); fuel smuggling from Iran; land mafias. Laws: Prevention of Trafficking in Persons Act 2018; Prevention of Smuggling of Migrants Act 2018; CNSA 1997; AML Act 2010; ATA 1997. Agencies: FIA, ANF, CTDs, Customs, Rangers.
- **Crime–terror nexus:** militants fund themselves through drugs, extortion and kidnapping.

## 7. Corporate crime

- Crimes committed by a corporation (or by officials for the corporation) to benefit the company.
- **Clinard and Yeager, *Corporate Crime* (1980).**
- **Types:** financial (accounting fraud — Enron 2001; Wirecard 2020), environmental (Bhopal gas disaster 1984), consumer safety (Ford Pinto; Volkswagen emissions 2015), labour violations (Baldia factory fire, Karachi 2012 — arson case; Rana Plaza, Bangladesh 2013), price-fixing and cartels (CCP fines on cement and fertiliser cartels), bribery (Siemens).
- **Problems of control:** "too big to jail"; corporate liability doctrines; diffused responsibility; regulatory capture; lobbying.
- **Responses:** corporate criminal liability; deferred prosecution agreements; whistle-blower protection; independent regulators; compliance programmes; consumer courts.
