# V. Juvenile Delinquency

The single most-asked topic: 2017 (causes, note), 2018 (nature, extent, causes, remedies), 2020 (risk and protective factors), 2022 (definition), 2023 (prevention through institutions, counselling, care), 2024 and 2025 (socio-economic, demographic and environmental factors — identical wording). ## 1. Meaning and definitions

- **Juvenile:** a person below the age of criminal majority. In Pakistan, the **Juvenile Justice System Act 2018 (JJSA)** defines a "juvenile" as a child who at the time of the offence has not reached **18 years**.
- **Age of criminal responsibility (PPC):** s.82 — nothing is an offence by a child under **10** (raised from 7 by amendment in 2016 **[verify year]**); s.83 — a child above 10 and under **14** is not liable if he had not attained sufficient maturity to understand the nature and consequences of the act **[verify ages]**.
- **Juvenile delinquency:** law-breaking or seriously anti-social behaviour by a juvenile.

### Legal vs behavioural definitions
| Legal definition | Behavioural (sociological) definition |
|---|---|
| A juvenile who has committed an act that would be a crime if done by an adult, or a status offence, and has been adjudicated by a court | Any anti-social or norm-violating behaviour by a young person, whether or not detected or processed |
| Narrow, precise, depends on law and enforcement | Broad, includes hidden delinquency, truancy, defiance |
| Basis for official statistics | Basis for self-report studies and prevention |
| Criticism: misses most delinquency (dark figure) | Criticism: vague; risks labelling normal adolescent behaviour |

### Juvenile delinquent vs status offender
- **Delinquent act:** an act that would be a crime for an adult (theft, assault).
- **Status offence:** an act illegal only because of the actor's age — truancy, running away, curfew violation, underage drinking/smoking, incorrigibility.
- **Chronic status offender:** a child who keeps committing status offences despite repeated intervention by family, school, social services and police (2024 MCQ).
- Modern policy (US Juvenile Justice and Delinquency Prevention Act 1974): **deinstitutionalise** status offenders — handle them through social services, not detention.
- Pakistan law does not use the status-offence category in the US sense; truancy and running away are handled informally, though vagrancy laws and child protection laws (e.g. Punjab Destitute and Neglected Children Act 2004) apply **[verify title]**.

**Other terms:** *parens patriae* — the state as ultimate guardian of every child (basis of juvenile courts); *dependent/neglected child*; *youthful offender*; *life-course-persistent vs adolescence-limited* (Moffitt).

## 2. Official statistics of juvenile delinquency

- **Sources:** police arrest data, court records, prison/juvenile institution data. In the US: FBI UCR/NIBRS juvenile arrests; OJJDP reports.
- **Problems:** reflect police discretion and reporting, not actual behaviour; the "dark figure"; class and race bias in arrests; changes in law change numbers.
- **Alternatives:** self-report surveys (Short and Nye, 1958, showed delinquency spread across classes), victimisation surveys, cohort studies (Wolfgang's Philadelphia cohort, 1972 — about 6% of boys committed over half of offences: "chronic offenders").
- **Pakistan:** no reliable national juvenile crime statistics. Data come from provincial prison departments (juvenile prisoners), Sahil's annual "Cruel Numbers" (child abuse, children as victims), SPARC's *State of Pakistan's Children* reports, NCHR/JPP prison data reports. Juveniles in prisons number in the low thousands, many under trial **[cite the exact latest figure only after checking the NCHR/JPP Oct 2026 report]**. Age is often disputed because of missing birth registration.
- **Patterns (international):** age–crime curve peaks in late teens; boys far outnumber girls; most juvenile offending is minor and stops by early adulthood; a small chronic group commits most serious offences.

## 3. Nature and extent in Pakistan

- **Nature:** theft and snatching, drug use and peddling, street fights, vehicle theft, cyber offences (harassment, fraud), sexual offences; children used by gangs and militants; child labourers drawn into petty crime; runaway and street children (estimated over a million street children **[verify]**).
- **Extent:** under-recorded. Youth bulge — about **two-thirds of the population under 30** (2023 census basis **[verify]**) — means large absolute numbers at risk. Around **25 million children out of school** (UNICEF/Pakistan Education Statistics **[verify latest]**).

## 4. Causes: socio-economic, demographic and environmental factors (2024, 2025)

### Socio-economic
1. **Poverty and deprivation** — inability to meet needs; strain (Merton, Agnew).
2. **Unemployment and child labour** — exposure to exploitation and adult criminals.
3. **Inequality and relative deprivation** — visible consumerism vs blocked means.
4. **Lack of education / school dropout** — weak commitment (Hirschi); out-of-school children.
5. **Family factors:** broken homes, parental neglect, domestic violence, harsh or inconsistent discipline, parental criminality and addiction, large families with little supervision, absent fathers (labour migration to the Gulf).
6. **Peer pressure and gangs** — differential association.
7. **Drug availability** — ice (crystal meth) spreading among students **[verify]**; heroin and hashish.
8. **Media and internet** — violent content, online gaming addiction, cyber exploitation.

### Demographic
1. **Age:** peak offending at 15–19.
2. **Gender:** boys more involved; girls more often victims or status offenders.
3. **Youth bulge and rapid population growth** (2.55% a year, 2023 census).
4. **Migration and urbanisation** — rural–urban migration weakens family and community control.
5. **Displacement** — Afghan refugees, internally displaced persons from conflict areas (FATA operations, floods 2022).

### Environmental
1. **Urban slums and katchi abadis** — social disorganisation (Shaw and McKay).
2. **Lack of recreation spaces** — no parks or sports facilities; idle time.
3. **Neighbourhood crime and disorder** — broken windows; gang presence.
4. **Conflict and terrorism** — exposure to violence; militant recruitment of children (suicide bombers trained in KP — Sabaoon de-radicalisation centre in Swat from 2009).
5. **Weak institutions** — police corruption, absence of social services.
6. **Climate disasters** — floods displace families, increasing child labour and trafficking risk.

### Individual factors (complete the picture)
Low self-control, learning disabilities, mental health problems, early aggressive behaviour, trauma and abuse.

## 5. Risk and protective factors (2020)

| Domain | Risk factors | Protective factors |
|---|---|---|
| Individual | Impulsivity, early aggression, substance use, low IQ/school failure, trauma | Self-control, social skills, religiosity/moral beliefs, positive self-image |
| Family | Poverty, neglect, abuse, parental criminality, conflict, poor supervision | Warm parent–child bond, consistent discipline, parental monitoring, family stability |
| School | Dropout, truancy, low achievement, bullying, corporal punishment | School attachment, good teachers, extracurricular activities, counselling |
| Peers | Delinquent friends, gang membership | Pro-social peers, mentors |
| Community | Slums, crime, drugs, guns, unemployment, disorder | Collective efficacy, youth centres, mosques/community organisations, sports facilities, jobs |

**Prevention science (Hawkins and Catalano's Communities That Care):** reduce risk factors and strengthen protective factors early.

## 6. Prevention and reduction (2018, 2023)

**UN Guidelines for the Prevention of Juvenile Delinquency (Riyadh Guidelines, 1990):** prevention through family, education, community, media; avoid criminalising minor behaviour; child-centred approach.

**Institutional role:**
- *Family:* parenting education; support for single mothers; cash transfers conditional on schooling (BISP's Waseela-e-Taleem).
- *School:* universal enrolment (Art. 25A); stop corporal punishment (ICT Corporal Punishment Act 2021 **[verify]**); school counsellors; anti-bullying; vocational streams.
- *Community:* sports and youth centres; mentoring (big brother/sister); mosque and NGO programmes; community policing.
- *State:* child protection units (Punjab Child Protection and Welfare Bureau; Sindh Child Protection Authority; KP Child Protection Commission); Zainab Alert Act 2020 for missing children; drug prevention and rehabilitation; skills (TEVTA, National Vocational and Technical Training Commission).
- *Juvenile justice:* diversion, probation, rehabilitation centres (see file 05).

**Professional counselling:** cognitive-behavioural therapy; family therapy (Functional Family Therapy, Multisystemic Therapy — strong evidence); anger management; substance-abuse counselling; trauma-informed care.

**Increased care:** foster care and kinship care rather than institutions; aftercare after release; mental-health services; safe spaces for street children (e.g. Edhi homes; Punjab's shelters).

**Situational prevention:** supervision of public places, safe routes to school, regulating sale of drugs and weapons.

**Evidence-based programmes:** early childhood (Perry Preschool, Nurse–Family Partnership); school-based social-emotional learning; mentoring; youth employment. Scared Straight programmes **increase** offending — avoid.
