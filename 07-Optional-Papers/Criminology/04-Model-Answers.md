## Answer 1 (Section I). Discuss the scope of criminology and its significance in Pakistani society. *(CSS 2025; similar 2017, 2018, 2023)*

**Outline**
1. Introduction and thesis
2. Meaning and nature of criminology
3. Scope: main areas
4. Significance for Pakistan
5. Limits of criminology in Pakistan today
6. Way forward
7. Conclusion

**Introduction**
Edwin Sutherland defined criminology as the study of crime as a social phenomenon, covering the making of laws, the breaking of laws and society's reaction to law-breaking. From Beccaria's *On Crimes and Punishments* (1764) and Lombroso's positivism to today's cyber and environmental criminology, the discipline has widened with the forms of crime. **For Pakistan — facing terrorism, street crime, gender-based violence, cyber fraud and overcrowded prisons — criminology is not an academic luxury but a practical tool for safer communities and fairer justice.**

**1. Meaning and nature**
The term comes from Latin *crimen* and Greek *logos*; Topinard used it first and Garofalo popularised it in 1885. Criminology is:
- a **social science** that uses observation, data and theory;
- **interdisciplinary**, drawing on sociology, psychology, biology, law, economics and forensic science;
- both **theoretical** (explaining causes) and **applied** (guiding policing, courts and corrections);
- **dynamic**, adapting to new crimes such as deepfake fraud.

**2. Scope**
Sutherland divided the field into the sociology of law, criminal etiology and penology. Today the scope includes:
1. **Criminal statistics** — measuring crime, the dark figure, victim surveys.
2. **Sociology of law** — how laws are made and enforced, and whose interests they serve.
3. **Etiology** — biological, psychological and sociological causes (strain, control, learning, labelling, conflict).
4. **Typologies** — occasional, habitual, professional, white-collar, organised and corporate crime.
5. **Penology and corrections** — punishment, prisons, probation, parole, rehabilitation.
6. **Victimology** — victims' needs and rights.
7. **Juvenile delinquency and juvenile justice.**
8. **Policing and criminal investigation**, including forensics.
9. **Crime prevention** — situational, social, community-based.
10. **Transnational crime** — terrorism, trafficking, money laundering, cyber crime.

**3. Significance in Pakistani society**
- *Understanding and preventing terrorism.* Pakistan ranked second in the Global Terrorism Index 2025. Criminological research on radicalisation (push and pull factors, social learning online) informs de-radicalisation programmes such as Sabaoon in Swat and counter-narratives such as Paigham-e-Pakistan.
- *Urban crime.* Rapid urbanisation in Karachi and Lahore has produced street crime and robberies. Social disorganisation theory and routine activity theory explain hot spots; situational prevention, crime mapping and Safe City systems apply these insights.
- *Gender-based violence.* Honour killings, rape and domestic violence require victim-centred investigation and specialised courts — reflected in the Anti-Rape (Investigation and Trial) Act 2021.
- *Juvenile justice.* With a large youth population and millions of children out of school, prevention and diversion under the Juvenile Justice System Act 2018 depend on criminological knowledge of risk and protective factors.
- *Police reform.* Research on procedural justice and community policing shows that legitimacy, not force, wins cooperation — relevant as encounter killings by Punjab's Crime Control Department (2025–26) draw criticism from the HRCP.
- *Corrections.* In October 2026 the NCHR and Justice Project Pakistan reported 110,513 prisoners against capacity of 68,832 (about 161% occupancy), with three-quarters awaiting trial. Penology points to bail reform, probation and parole, and rehabilitation.
- *Financial and cyber crime.* FATF grey-listing (2018–2022) showed the cost of weak anti-money-laundering systems. Cyber criminology guides the PECA framework and the new National Cyber Crime Investigation Agency.
- *Evidence-based policy.* Criminology supplies data and evaluation so that laws are judged by results, not slogans.

**4. Limits today**
Criminology in Pakistan is still young. Crime data are unreliable because many crimes are not registered; few universities have strong criminology departments; police and policy-makers rarely use research; and imported theories need adaptation to tribal, religious and feudal contexts. The Islamic perspective on crime and punishment — with its emphasis on prevention, strict proof for hudud and forgiveness through diyat — is under-researched as a living framework.

**5. Way forward**
1. A national crime and victimisation survey.
2. Criminology departments and research centres linked to police and prosecution academies.
3. Evidence-based policing units in provincial police.
4. Evaluation of major programmes (Safe City, CTDs, juvenile diversion).
5. Training of judges, prosecutors and investigators in criminology and forensics.
6. Indigenous research on honour crime, jirgas, sectarian violence and drug use.

**Where criminological knowledge has already shaped policy in Pakistan**
Several recent reforms reflect criminological thinking, even if not always acknowledged. The Juvenile Justice System Act 2018 builds on labelling theory and the evidence that diversion reduces re-offending: minor cases go to Juvenile Justice Committees instead of courts. The Anti-Rape (Investigation and Trial) Act 2021 reflects victimology — special investigation units, in-camera trials and the ban on the two-finger test protect victims from secondary victimisation. Safe City projects in Lahore and Islamabad apply situational crime prevention and routine activity theory by increasing guardianship through cameras. The Punjab Probation and Parole Service Act 2019 applies penological research showing that supervised community sentences are cheaper and often more effective than short prison terms. De-radicalisation centres such as Sabaoon draw on social learning and psychological research.

**Criminology and the Islamic tradition**
Criminology also helps Pakistan connect its constitutional commitment to Islamic principles with modern justice. The Islamic emphasis on preventing crime through social justice (zakat, care for orphans), strict standards of proof, and forgiveness and compensation through diyat parallels modern ideas of social crime prevention, due process and restorative justice. Studying these links can produce policies that are both effective and culturally legitimate.

**A note of caution**
Criminology does not provide ready-made answers. Theories developed in Chicago or London may not fit a society where tribe, caste, religion and feudal power shape behaviour. Pakistani criminology must therefore test imported ideas locally before turning them into policy.

**Conclusion**
Criminology studies crime from its causes to society's response. Its wide scope — from theory to forensic science and corrections — matches the breadth of Pakistan's crime problems. Used well, it can help Pakistan move from reactive, force-based crime control to prevention, fair investigation and rehabilitation.

## Answer 2 (Section I). Explain R.K. Merton's strain theory. Critically elaborate its effects on youth and suggest remedies. *(CSS 2025; anomie 2022)*

**Outline**
1. Introduction and thesis
2. Durkheim's anomie: the background
3. Merton's theory and the five adaptations
4. Effects on youth (with Pakistani evidence)
5. Later developments
6. Critical evaluation
7. Remedies
8. Conclusion

**Introduction**
Why do some young people turn to crime while most conform? Robert K. Merton's "Social Structure and Anomie" (1938) answered that crime is produced not by abnormal individuals but by a society that preaches success to all while denying many the means to achieve it. **Merton's strain theory helps explain youth crime, drug use and even radicalisation in Pakistan, where aspirations rise faster than opportunities; but it needs to be combined with other theories, and its remedies lie in widening legitimate opportunity.**

**1. Background: Durkheim's anomie**
Émile Durkheim introduced anomie — normlessness — in *The Division of Labour in Society* (1893) and *Suicide* (1897). In periods of rapid change, social norms fail to regulate human desires, leading to deviance.

**2. Merton's theory**
Merton adapted anomie to American society. Culture sets **goals** (wealth and success, the "American Dream") and prescribes **legitimate means** (education, hard work). When the social structure blocks access to the means, people experience **strain** and adapt in five ways:

| Adaptation | Goals | Means | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Conformity | Accept | Accept | Student working hard for a job |
| **Innovation** | Accept | Reject | Mobile snatching, cyber fraud, drug dealing |
| Ritualism | Reject | Accept | Clerk going through the motions |
| Retreatism | Reject | Reject | Drug addicts, dropouts |
| Rebellion | Replace | Replace | Revolutionary or militant groups |

**3. Effects on youth in Pakistan**
- *Innovation.* Unemployment and inflation, together with consumerism promoted by social media, push some young men into street crime — mobile phone and motorcycle snatching in Karachi and Lahore — and online scams.
- *Retreatism.* Blocked opportunities and family breakdown contribute to drug use, including crystal meth among students and heroin addiction in urban slums.
- *Rebellion.* Some youths reject the system's goals altogether; militant groups offer new goals (religious or ethnic) and new means (violence). Strain from marginalisation in ex-FATA and Balochistan is often cited as a push factor.
- *Ritualism.* Educated youth stuck in low-paid or insecure jobs disengage — not crime, but a waste of potential and a source of frustration.
- *Structural roots.* About two-thirds of Pakistanis are under 30; millions of children are out of school; jobs for graduates are scarce; inequality is visible. The gap between aspiration and opportunity is exactly the condition Merton described.

**4. Later developments**
- **Albert Cohen (1955):** lower-class boys facing status frustration form delinquent subcultures with their own values.
- **Cloward and Ohlin (1960):** differential opportunity — illegitimate means are also unequally distributed, producing criminal, conflict or retreatist subcultures.
- **Robert Agnew's General Strain Theory (1992):** strain also arises from the loss of positive stimuli (death, divorce) and the presence of negative stimuli (abuse, bullying); negative emotions like anger lead to crime. This fits youth exposed to domestic violence or police humiliation.
- **Messner and Rosenfeld (1994):** institutional anomie — when the economy dominates family and education.

**5. Critical evaluation**
*Strengths:* links individual crime to social structure; explains property crime and drug use; policy-relevant (opportunity programmes); adaptable to many societies.
*Weaknesses:*
- Over-predicts lower-class crime; most poor youths conform.
- Ignores white-collar crime by the privileged (though "innovation" can cover it).
- Assumes everyone shares the same goals; in Pakistan, goals also include family honour and religious duty.
- Does not explain why a person chooses one adaptation over another.
- Neglects gender — girls under strain rarely turn to crime.
- Does not address the role of peers and learning (Sutherland) or weak bonds (Hirschi).
So strain theory works best when combined with learning and control theories.

**6. Remedies**
1. **Education** — universal schooling under Article 25A; quality and market-relevant curricula; technical and vocational training (TEVTA, NAVTTC).
2. **Employment** — youth employment and apprenticeship schemes; support for freelancing and IT exports; microfinance (Akhuwat).
3. **Reducing inequality** — progressive taxation, social protection (BISP), merit-based recruitment that ends *sifarish*.
4. **Realistic aspirations and values** — media and schools promoting respect for honest work; mentoring.
5. **Recreation and community** — sports, youth centres, scouts, civic volunteering.
6. **Counselling and anger management** for youth under strain (Agnew).
7. **Drug prevention and treatment** for retreatists.
8. **De-radicalisation and inclusion** — political participation and grievance redress in marginalised regions.
9. **Juvenile justice that avoids labelling** — diversion under JJSA 2018.

**Applying strain to two Pakistani patterns**
*Cyber fraud by educated youth.* Graduates with digital skills but no jobs are recruited into online scam operations and fake call centres. This is Merton's innovation in a modern form: the goal (income, status) is accepted, the legitimate means (employment) are blocked, and technology offers an illegitimate path with low perceived risk.
*Female youth.* Young women in Pakistan face strain too — restrictions on education and work, forced marriage — but they rarely respond with crime. Agnew's General Strain Theory helps here: the response to strain depends on coping resources, social support and social control. Women's tighter supervision and different socialisation channel strain into depression or withdrawal rather than offending. This also shows the limits of Merton's original theory, which assumed one response pattern.
*Policy implication.* Remedies must therefore address both blocked opportunities (jobs, education) and coping (counselling, social support), as Agnew suggests.

**Strain and radicalisation**
Merton's "rebellion" also helps explain why some marginalised youths join militant or separatist groups: rejecting both the goals and the means of the existing order, they adopt new ones offered by a movement. Strain alone does not make a militant — ideology, recruiters and opportunity matter — but it creates the frustration recruiters exploit.

**Conclusion**
Merton showed that crime can be a normal response to an abnormal gap between goals and means. In Pakistan, that gap is wide for millions of young people. Strain theory does not explain every crime, but it points to the most important remedy: open legitimate doors — education, jobs, fairness — so that young people do not seek illegitimate ones.

## Answer 3 (Section II). Explain juvenile delinquency. What socio-economic, demographic and environmental factors lead to it? *(CSS 2024, 2025; similar 2018, 2020)*

**Outline**
1. Introduction and thesis
2. Meaning: legal and behavioural definitions; status offences
3. Nature and extent in Pakistan
4. Socio-economic factors
5. Demographic factors
6. Environmental factors
7. Risk and protective factors (summary)
8. Way forward
9. Conclusion

**Introduction**
Most adult offenders begin offending in adolescence, and the age–crime curve peaks in the late teens. Juvenile delinquency is therefore the starting point of crime prevention. **In Pakistan, delinquency grows out of an interaction of poverty, family breakdown, a youth bulge and rapid, unplanned urbanisation; tackling these early is cheaper and more humane than punishing later.**

**1. Meaning**
Under the Juvenile Justice System Act 2018, a juvenile is a child under 18 at the time of the offence. The **legal definition** of delinquency is an act by a juvenile that would be a crime if done by an adult, as determined by a court. The **behavioural definition** is wider: any anti-social or norm-violating conduct, detected or not. **Status offences** — truancy, running away, underage smoking — are offences only because of age. The state acts as *parens patriae*, the ultimate guardian of children, which is why juvenile justice aims at welfare and rehabilitation.

**2. Nature and extent in Pakistan**
Official statistics are weak: many cases are settled informally, ages are disputed because births are unregistered, and police data reflect recording practices. Common offences include theft and snatching, drug use and peddling, fights, vehicle theft and online harassment. Children are also drawn into gangs and militancy. Data from prison departments, the NCHR and NGOs such as Sahil and SPARC show juveniles held in prisons and many children as victims of abuse.

**3. Socio-economic factors**
- **Poverty:** unmet needs and relative deprivation create strain (Merton, Agnew).
- **Child labour:** working children meet adult offenders and suffer exploitation.
- **Lack of education:** millions of children are out of school; dropping out weakens commitment to conventional goals (Hirschi's social bond).
- **Family problems:** broken homes, neglect, harsh or inconsistent discipline, domestic violence, parental addiction or criminality, and fathers absent as labour migrants reduce supervision and attachment.
- **Peer influence:** delinquent friends teach techniques and justifications (Sutherland's differential association).
- **Drugs:** availability of hashish, heroin and crystal meth.
- **Media and internet:** violent content, gaming addiction and online exploitation.

**4. Demographic factors**
- **Age and gender:** offending peaks between 15 and 19; boys are far more involved.
- **Youth bulge:** with population growth of 2.55% a year (2023 census) and a very young population, the number of adolescents at risk is large.
- **Migration:** rural–urban migration loosens family and community control.
- **Displacement:** families displaced by military operations, the 2022 floods and Afghan conflict lose homes, schooling and community ties.

**5. Environmental factors**
- **Slums and katchi abadis:** Shaw and McKay showed that delinquency concentrates in disorganised inner-city areas regardless of who lives there. Karachi's informal settlements fit this pattern.
- **No recreation:** lack of parks, playgrounds and youth centres leaves idle time.
- **Neighbourhood disorder and crime:** the broken-windows thesis — visible disorder signals no control.
- **Violence and conflict:** exposure to terrorism and militancy normalises violence; militants recruited children in the tribal areas.
- **Weak institutions:** corrupt policing and absent social services fail to intervene early.

**6. Risk and protective factors**

| Domain | Risk | Protection |
|---|---|---|
| Individual | Impulsivity, trauma, substance use | Self-control, moral beliefs |
| Family | Neglect, abuse, conflict | Warm bonds, supervision |
| School | Dropout, bullying | Attachment, counselling |
| Peers | Delinquent friends | Pro-social mentors |
| Community | Slums, drugs, guns | Collective efficacy, youth centres |

Delinquency rises when risks accumulate and protections are absent.

**7. Way forward**
1. **Family support:** parenting programmes; cash transfers tied to schooling (BISP's education stipends).
2. **Education:** enforce Article 25A; school counsellors; vocational streams; end corporal punishment.
3. **Community:** sports and youth centres, mentoring, community policing.
4. **Child protection:** functional child protection units, helplines, the Zainab Alert system for missing children.
5. **Drug prevention and treatment.**
6. **Juvenile justice:** diversion through Juvenile Justice Committees, observation homes instead of police lock-ups, probation, rehabilitation centres (JJSA 2018).
7. **Urban planning:** improve slums, lighting and public spaces.
8. **Data:** birth registration and a juvenile justice database.
9. **Partnerships:** UNICEF and NGOs for child protection systems.

**Explaining the factors through theory**
The factors are best understood through several theories together. **Strain theory** (Merton, Agnew) explains how poverty and blocked opportunities push adolescents toward theft, snatching and drug dealing. **Social control theory** (Hirschi) explains why weak attachment to parents, low commitment to school and idle time free young people to offend. **Differential association** (Sutherland) explains how delinquent peers and gangs teach techniques and justifications. **Social disorganisation** (Shaw and McKay) explains why delinquency clusters in slums with transient populations and weak community institutions. **Labelling theory** warns that harsh responses — jailing children with adults, naming them in the media — can turn occasional delinquents into persistent offenders. Moffitt's distinction between adolescence-limited and life-course-persistent offenders suggests that most juveniles grow out of delinquency, so the justice system should avoid doing lasting harm, while focusing intensive help on the small group with early, serious problems.

**A counter-view**
Some argue that poverty alone causes delinquency. The evidence does not support so simple a view: most poor children never offend, and delinquency also occurs among affluent youth — in drug use, cyber offences and dangerous driving. What matters is the combination of risks and the absence of protection, especially family support and schooling. This is why prevention must work through families and schools, not only through poverty reduction.

**Conclusion**
Juvenile delinquency in Pakistan is not caused by a single factor but by the meeting of social, demographic and environmental pressures on children with too few protections. The evidence from prevention science is clear: strengthening families, schools and communities reduces delinquency more effectively than punishment. Investing in children is the cheapest crime policy Pakistan has.

## Answer 4 (Section II). What challenges do police face in controlling crime and maintaining law and order in Pakistan? *(CSS 2023, 2025)*

**Outline**
1. Introduction and thesis
2. Role of police
3. Historical roots of today's problems
4. Challenges (structural, political, operational, social)
5. Recent developments
6. Reforms
7. Conclusion

**Introduction**
Sir Robert Peel's principle that "the police are the public and the public are the police" defines modern policing as service by consent. Pakistan's police, by contrast, were built under the Police Act 1861 to control a colonial population after 1857. **The police in Pakistan face severe challenges — terrorism, urban crime, scarce resources — but their deepest problem is structural: a colonial, politicised and under-resourced organisation that lacks public trust.**

**1. Role of police**
Prevention and detection of crime, maintenance of public order, investigation, counter-terrorism, traffic, protection of life and property, and service to citizens.

**2. Historical roots**
The 1861 Act created a paramilitary force under the district magistrate. After 1947 the system continued. The Police Order 2002 tried to modernise it with operational autonomy, public safety commissions, complaints authorities and separation of investigation from watch and ward. But it was diluted in 2004, and after the 18th Amendment provinces went different ways: Sindh and Balochistan returned to the 1861 Act in 2011 (Sindh revived a modified Police Order in 2019), Khyber Pakhtunkhwa passed its own Police Act in 2017, and Punjab kept an amended Police Order.

**3. Challenges**
*Structural and political*
1. **Political interference:** transfers and postings of SHOs, DPOs and IGPs at politicians' wishes; police used against opponents.
2. **Colonial culture:** control-oriented "thana culture", custodial torture, distance from citizens.
3. **Weak oversight:** public safety commissions and complaints authorities exist on paper in many places.

*Resources and capacity*
4. **Shortage of staff and equipment:** low police-to-population ratio; old vehicles and weapons; little forensic support.
5. **Poor pay and working conditions:** long hours without shifts, poor housing → low morale and corruption.
6. **Weak investigation:** reliance on confessions, poor crime-scene management, limited forensics → low conviction rates; courts acquit, and criminals return.
7. **Training gaps:** little training in scientific investigation, human rights or cyber crime.

*Operational threats*
8. **Terrorism and militancy:** police in KP and Balochistan are frontline targets of the TTP and Baloch militants.
9. **Urban crime:** street crime in Karachi and Lahore; heavily armed dacoit gangs in the katcha areas of Sindh and south Punjab.
10. **Cyber and financial crime:** new skills needed.
11. **Coordination:** with Rangers, FC, CTDs, intelligence agencies, prosecution and courts.

*Social and legitimacy*
12. **Low public trust:** refusal to register FIRs, bribery, and fear of police discourage reporting; Transparency International Pakistan's surveys repeatedly place police among the most corrupt sectors.
13. **Human-rights concerns:** alleged extrajudicial "encounters" — the HRCP documented hundreds of deaths in encounters by Punjab's Crime Control Department after its creation in 2025 — and custodial deaths, despite the Torture and Custodial Death (Prevention and Punishment) Act 2022.
14. **Gender gap:** very few women officers; women victims avoid police stations.
15. **Justice system bottlenecks:** overcrowded prisons (about 161% occupancy in 2026) and court delays undermine deterrence.

**4. Recent developments**
Safe City projects with CCTV and command centres; provincial Counter-Terrorism Departments; KP's Dispute Resolution Councils; police service centres (Khidmat Markaz); the August 2025 amendment to the Anti-Terrorism Act restoring preventive detention for up to three months; the Crime Control Department in Punjab, praised by some for falling crime figures and criticised by rights groups for encounter killings.

**5. Reforms**
1. **Operational autonomy with accountability:** fixed tenures for police chiefs; postings by merit; functional public safety commissions and independent complaints authorities.
2. **Investigation wing:** separate, trained and funded; forensic labs in every province; prosecutor–police cooperation.
3. **Resources and welfare:** pay reform, shift systems, housing, insurance for martyrs' families.
4. **Training:** human rights, PEACE interviewing, cyber crime, community policing.
5. **Technology:** crime mapping, digital FIRs, body cameras, data-driven deployment.
6. **Community policing:** beat officers, liaison committees, women's desks.
7. **Legal modernisation:** replace 1861-era laws and the 1934 Police Rules with modern, rights-based statutes.
8. **Accountability for abuses:** judicial inquiries into encounters and custodial deaths; enforcement of the 2022 Torture Act.
9. **Women in police:** recruitment targets and facilities.
10. **Criminal justice coordination:** district committees of police, prosecution, courts and prisons.

**Lessons from other countries**
Other countries with colonial or corrupt police forces have reformed them. **Georgia** dismissed its notoriously corrupt traffic police in 2004 and recruited a new, better-paid patrol police with modern equipment and visible accountability; public trust rose sharply. **India's** Supreme Court, in *Prakash Singh v. Union of India* (2006), directed states to set up security commissions, give police chiefs a minimum fixed tenure, separate investigation from law-and-order duties, create police establishment boards for postings, and establish police complaints authorities — measures similar to Pakistan's Police Order 2002, and similarly resisted by state governments. The **United Kingdom** relies on policing by consent, independent inspection (HM Inspectorate) and elected Police and Crime Commissioners (since 2012) for local accountability. The common lesson is that operational independence must come with strong, transparent oversight — neither political control nor unchecked police power works.

**The debate over force**
Some argue that tough tactics — encounters, mass arrests, preventive detention — are necessary when criminals are heavily armed and courts are slow. Supporters of Punjab's Crime Control Department point to falling crime figures. Critics, including the HRCP, reply that extrajudicial killings violate Article 9, kill innocent people, destroy evidence that could expose wider networks, and further erode trust. Long-term evidence from other countries shows that legitimacy, not fear, produces lasting cooperation and lower crime. A balanced position is that firm action against violent gangs must remain within the law and be subject to independent inquiry.

**Conclusion**
Pakistan's police face a dangerous and changing crime environment with colonial structures, political interference and thin resources. More force alone — encounters or emergency laws — cannot solve these problems and may deepen distrust. A professional, accountable, community-oriented police service, backed by modern investigation and fair courts, is the only durable path to law and order.

## Answer 5 (Section III). Enumerate and explain the key principles of effective criminal investigation. How do they contribute to success? *(CSS 2024; similar 2016, 2017, 2018, 2020, 2025)*

**Outline**
1. Introduction and thesis
2. Meaning and objectives of investigation
3. Key principles (twelve)
4. How the principles contribute to success
5. Pakistani practice: gaps and examples
6. Way forward
7. Conclusion

**Introduction**
A trial can only be as good as the investigation behind it. In Pakistan many acquittals result not from innocence but from weak investigation — late FIRs, contaminated crime scenes, coerced confessions and hostile witnesses. **Effective investigation rests on a set of legal, scientific and ethical principles; when followed, they identify offenders accurately, protect the innocent and secure convictions that the public trusts.**

**1. Meaning and objectives**
Criminal investigation is the lawful collection, preservation, analysis and presentation of information and evidence to establish whether a crime occurred, who committed it, and to support prosecution. Its objectives are to prove the *corpus delicti*, identify and arrest the offender, recover property, and present admissible evidence in court. O'Hara's "three I's" — **information, interrogation and instrumentation** — summarise its tools.

**2. Key principles**
1. **Legality.** Act within the Constitution (Arts. 9, 10, 10A, 13, 14) and the CrPC and Qanun-e-Shahadat. Illegal methods produce inadmissible or unreliable evidence.
2. **Objectivity and impartiality.** Follow evidence, not assumptions, pressure or bribes; avoid tunnel vision.
3. **Promptness.** The first hours matter: evidence decays, suspects flee, memories fade.
4. **Preservation of the scene.** Cordon, log entries, prevent contamination.
5. **Locard's exchange principle.** Every contact leaves a trace; search for fingerprints, DNA, fibres, digital traces.
6. **Chain of custody.** Document every transfer of evidence so its integrity cannot be challenged.
7. **Thoroughness.** Examine all leads, including those pointing away from the main suspect.
8. **Documentation.** Notes, photographs, sketches, and the case diary under CrPC s.172.
9. **Scientific methods.** Forensics, databases (NADRA, call records), digital forensics, crime analysis.
10. **Ethical interviewing.** Rapport-based, non-coercive methods such as the PEACE model; confessions to police are inadmissible under Articles 38–39 of the Qanun-e-Shahadat, and torture is a crime under the 2022 Act.
11. **Teamwork and coordination.** With forensic experts, prosecutors, other agencies, and INTERPOL where needed.
12. **Victim and witness care.** Trauma-informed handling, protection from intimidation, privacy for women and children.
13. **Confidentiality.** Protect informants and the integrity of the case.
14. **Supervision and review.** Senior officers check progress and correct errors.

**3. How the principles contribute to success**
- **Accuracy:** objectivity and thoroughness reduce wrongful arrests and identify the real offender.
- **Admissibility:** legality, documentation and chain of custody make evidence acceptable in court.
- **Strength of proof:** forensic evidence corroborates or replaces unreliable eyewitness testimony.
- **Speed:** prompt action prevents escape and further crime.
- **Cooperation:** respectful treatment of victims and witnesses makes them willing to testify.
- **Legitimacy:** ethical investigation builds public trust, which brings more information.
- **Efficiency:** teamwork and supervision avoid duplication and missed leads.

**4. Pakistani practice**
*Successes:* the Zainab murder case (Kasur, 2018) was solved through DNA matching by the Punjab Forensic Science Agency after hundreds of samples were tested; the Noor Mukadam murder case (Islamabad, 2021) relied on CCTV and forensic evidence; Safe City cameras help trace street criminals.
*Gaps:* poor crime-scene handling by first responders; few forensic labs outside Punjab; reliance on confessions and "recoveries" without independent witnesses (CrPC s.103); weak police–prosecution coordination; witness intimidation and lack of protection; investigation officers burdened with other duties and paying expenses themselves; political pressure and corruption.

**5. Way forward**
1. A separate investigation cadre with training and budgets.
2. Crime-scene units and forensic labs in every province, with accreditation.
3. Standard operating procedures for scenes, evidence and interviews; video recording of interviews.
4. Early involvement of prosecutors.
5. Witness protection programmes that actually function.
6. Digital case management and evidence tracking.
7. A DNA database law with privacy safeguards.
8. Accountability for fabrication and torture.

**The principles in action: a street robbery case**
Consider a mobile-phone robbery at gunpoint in a Lahore market. *Promptness:* the first officer responds quickly, aids the victim and circulates a description. *Scene preservation:* the spot is cordoned; a dropped cap and a discarded cartridge are protected for forensic examination. *Locard's principle:* the cap may carry DNA; the cartridge links to a weapon. *Information:* Safe City cameras show the getaway motorcycle; the stolen phone's IMEI is flagged; witnesses are interviewed separately using open questions. *Database use:* the motorcycle registration and the suspect's CNIC are checked with NADRA; call records place the suspect at the scene. *Legality:* the suspect is arrested, informed of the grounds and produced before a magistrate within 24 hours; the house search is made in the presence of two respectable witnesses (CrPC s.103). *Ethical interviewing:* the suspect is questioned without coercion; any confession is recorded before a magistrate under s.164. *Documentation and chain of custody:* every item is logged. *Coordination:* the prosecutor reviews the file before the challan. The result is a case resting on CCTV, forensic and digital evidence rather than a disputed confession — far more likely to end in conviction.

**Ethics as a principle, not an add-on**
Torture and fabricated evidence do not only violate rights; they also produce unreliable information, wrongful convictions and acquittals on appeal when the defects come to light. Ethical investigation is therefore also effective investigation. The 2022 Torture Act and the shift toward rapport-based interviewing (PEACE, the Méndez Principles) reflect this.

**Supervision and review**
Finally, senior officers should review every serious case at fixed points — after the first 48 hours, before the challan, and when new evidence emerges. Structured review catches tunnel vision early and ensures that the principles are actually applied, not just listed in manuals.

**Conclusion**
The principles of criminal investigation — legality, objectivity, promptness, scientific method, documentation and ethics — are not technicalities. They are what turns suspicion into proof. Pakistan's low conviction rates show the cost of ignoring them; its forensic successes show what is possible when they are followed.

## Answer 6 (Section III). To what extent has INTERPOL been successful in reducing organised crime at the international level? *(CSS 2018; INTERPOL also 2020, 2021)*

**Outline**
1. Introduction and thesis
2. INTERPOL: history, structure, principles
3. Tools against organised crime
4. Evidence of success
5. Limitations
6. Pakistan and INTERPOL
7. Way forward
8. Conclusion

**Introduction**
Organised crime crosses borders faster than police can. Drug cartels, human-smuggling networks and cyber-fraud rings exploit differences between national laws. INTERPOL, the world's largest police organisation, exists to close these gaps. **INTERPOL has been successful as a facilitator of information-sharing and joint operations, but it cannot by itself reduce organised crime, because it has no executive powers and depends on the capacity and will of its members.**

**1. History, structure, principles**
The idea began at the First International Criminal Police Congress in Monaco (1914); the International Criminal Police Commission was founded in Vienna in 1923 and took the name INTERPOL in 1956. Its General Secretariat is in Lyon, France, with an innovation centre in Singapore. It has 196 member countries, each with a National Central Bureau (in Pakistan, located in the FIA). Article 3 of its constitution forbids involvement in political, military, religious or racial matters.

**2. Tools against organised crime**
- **I-24/7**, a secure global police communication network.
- **Databases:** nominal data, fingerprints, DNA, stolen and lost travel documents, stolen vehicles, firearms, child sexual exploitation images.
- **Notices:** Red (locate and arrest for extradition), Blue, Green, Yellow (missing persons), Black, Orange, Purple, the INTERPOL–UN Security Council Special Notice, and a Silver Notice pilot (2025) for tracing criminal assets.
- **Operations:** coordinated global actions against drugs, trafficking, illicit medicines and cyber-enabled financial crime.
- **Capacity building:** training, forensic support, Incident Response Teams.
- **Financial crime tools:** rapid payment-stopping mechanisms and asset tracing.

**3. Evidence of success**
- **Arrests and extraditions:** thousands of Red Notices issued every year help locate fugitives worldwide.
- **Global operations:** operations against illicit pharmaceuticals (Pangea), drug trafficking and online fraud have led to large seizures and arrests across dozens of countries at once.
- **Victim rescue:** anti-trafficking operations have rescued victims of forced labour and sexual exploitation.
- **Stopping travel of criminals:** border checks against the SLTD database detect stolen passports used by traffickers and terrorists.
- **Networking:** INTERPOL links police where no bilateral agreements exist, including between rival states.

**4. Limitations**
1. **No executive power.** INTERPOL officers cannot arrest; national police must act.
2. **Uneven member capacity.** Weak NCBs in developing countries limit data entry and response.
3. **Political abuse of notices.** Some states have used Red Notices against dissidents; reforms since 2015 have added review, but concerns remain.
4. **Legal barriers.** Extradition depends on treaties, dual criminality and courts.
5. **Adaptive criminals.** Encryption, cryptocurrency and decentralised networks evade traditional tools.
6. **Corruption** in member police forces leaks information or protects criminals.
7. **Funding and data gaps.**
8. **Measurement problem.** It is hard to show that organised crime has declined overall; drug production and trafficking volumes have often grown despite record seizures (UNODC World Drug Reports).

**5. Pakistan and INTERPOL**
The FIA's NCB uses Red Notices to seek fugitives, including in financial and terrorism cases, and checks travel documents at airports. Cooperation has helped in human-smuggling and trafficking cases after migrant-boat disasters. But Pakistan's extradition arrangements are limited, and politically sensitive requests have been refused by other countries' courts, showing that INTERPOL cannot override national legal processes.

**6. Way forward**
1. Stronger national capacity — trained NCB staff, real-time database access at all borders.
2. Integrating INTERPOL tools with financial intelligence (FMU) to target criminal money.
3. Regional cooperation (SCO RATS, UNODC programmes).
4. Safeguards against political misuse.
5. Investment in cyber and crypto capabilities.
6. More extradition and mutual legal assistance agreements.

**Regional and complementary mechanisms**
INTERPOL works alongside other bodies. EUROPOL provides deeper analysis and joint investigation teams within the EU; ASEANAPOL (1981) links Southeast Asian police; the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation's Regional Anti-Terrorist Structure (RATS) in Tashkent shares intelligence on terrorism among member states including Pakistan. UNODC supports legal frameworks under the Palermo Convention, and FATF tackles the money behind organised crime. These overlapping mechanisms show that INTERPOL is one part of a wider architecture: it provides global reach and common databases, while regional bodies provide depth and trust.

**What would make INTERPOL more effective?**
Greater use of its databases at every border crossing, more funding for weak National Central Bureaus, faster review of politically sensitive notices, and closer links between INTERPOL's financial-crime tools and national financial intelligence units would raise its impact on organised crime.

**A balanced verdict**
Critics who say INTERPOL has "failed" because drug trafficking and human smuggling persist expect too much from an information-sharing body. Defenders who credit it with every arrest overstate its role. The fairest judgement is that INTERPOL has raised the cost and risk of crossing borders for criminals, made fugitives easier to find and helped coordinate operations that no single country could run alone. Its success is real but partial; the decisive factor remains the strength and integrity of national police forces.

**INTERPOL and terrorism**
The same tools serve counter-terrorism. Databases of foreign terrorist fighters, travel-document checks and Orange Notices on imminent threats have helped member states track militants returning from conflict zones. For Pakistan, which faces cross-border militancy, these channels supplement bilateral intelligence sharing. Yet political disagreements between states — over who counts as a terrorist — limit cooperation, a reminder of Article 3's neutrality rule and its constraints.

**Conclusion**
INTERPOL has made international policing possible by giving 196 countries a common language, network and set of tools. Its successes are real — arrests, rescues, seizures — but limited. Organised crime is reduced when strong national agencies use INTERPOL's tools, follow the money and cooperate across borders. INTERPOL is a force multiplier, not a substitute for capable domestic policing.

## Answer 7 (Section IV). Is radicalism present in Pakistani society? How does it escalate into terrorism, and how can society respond? *(CSS 2025; similar 2017, 2024)*

**Outline**
1. Introduction and thesis
2. Concepts: radicalism, radicalisation, extremism, terrorism
3. Evidence of radicalism in Pakistan
4. Causes: push and pull factors
5. The escalation process (models)
6. State response so far
7. Critical assessment
8. Way forward
9. Conclusion

**Introduction**
Not every radical becomes a terrorist, but every terrorist was first radicalised. Pakistan has suffered tens of thousands of deaths from terrorism since 2001 and ranked second in the Global Terrorism Index 2025. **Radicalism exists in parts of Pakistani society — religious, sectarian and ethno-nationalist — and it escalates into terrorism when grievances, ideology, group dynamics and opportunity combine. The response must address all four, not just the last.**

**1. Concepts**
- **Radicalism:** advocacy of fundamental change in society or politics; not necessarily violent.
- **Radicalisation:** the process by which a person adopts extremist beliefs and may come to support or use violence.
- **Extremism:** rejection of pluralism and the rule of law.
- **Terrorism:** violence or threat against civilians to intimidate a population or coerce a government for political, religious or ideological ends (ATA 1997, s.6).

**2. Evidence of radicalism in Pakistan**
- **Mob violence over blasphemy allegations:** the lynching of Mashal Khan (2017), the killing of a Sri Lankan factory manager in Sialkot (2021), and attacks on churches and homes in Jaranwala (2023).
- **Violent religious-political protests**, such as those led by the TLP in 2017 and 2021.
- **Sectarian violence**, including clashes in Kurram.
- **Militant groups:** the TTP's resurgence after the Taliban takeover of Afghanistan in 2021, and Baloch separatist attacks.
- **Online extremism:** propaganda and recruitment on social media and encrypted apps.
At the same time, most Pakistanis reject terrorism — over a thousand scholars endorsed the Paigham-e-Pakistan fatwa (2018) against suicide attacks. Radicalism is present but not dominant.

**3. Causes**
*Push factors:* poverty and unemployment (Merton's strain); marginalisation of regions such as ex-FATA and Balochistan; injustice, enforced disappearances and slow courts; sectarian discrimination; identity crises among youth; trauma from conflict.
*Pull factors:* ideology offering meaning and certainty; belonging and brotherhood; status and adventure; money; charismatic recruiters.
*Enablers:* unregulated seminaries (a minority), easy availability of weapons, sanctuaries across the border, online echo chambers, prisons, and past state tolerance of some militant groups.

**4. The escalation process**
- **Moghaddam's staircase (2005):** perceived injustice → search for options → displacement of aggression onto an enemy → moral engagement with an extremist group → us-versus-them thinking → the terrorist act. Few climb to the top.
- **Social learning (Sutherland, Akers):** definitions favouring violence are learned in intimate groups and online; rewards (status, belonging) reinforce them.
- **Symbolic interactionism and labelling:** dehumanising labels ("infidel", "traitor") make violence seem legitimate.
- **Group dynamics:** small groups radicalise each other; "a bunch of guys" (Sageman).
- **Opportunity:** training, weapons and a target turn intention into action.

**5. State response so far**
The Anti-Terrorism Act 1997 (amended many times, most recently in August 2025 to restore preventive detention of suspects for up to three months with a three-year sunset clause); the National Action Plan (2014, revised 2021); NACTA; provincial Counter-Terrorism Departments; military operations Zarb-e-Azb (2014), Radd-ul-Fasaad (2017) and Azm-e-Istehkam (2024); de-radicalisation centres such as Sabaoon in Swat; madrassa registration; FATF-driven action on terror financing; the Paigham-e-Pakistan narrative.

**6. Critical assessment**
Military operations reduced violence sharply after 2014, but militancy returned after 2021, showing that kinetic action without political and social measures cannot last. Prosecution remains weak; CTDs lack resources; counter-narratives reach few; enforced disappearances and selective action feed grievances; and mob violence over blasphemy often goes unpunished, encouraging repetition.

**7. Way forward**
1. **Rule of law:** prosecute incitement and mob violence consistently; protect witnesses and judges; fair trials.
2. **Education:** curricula that teach critical thinking and tolerance; regulated madrassa curricula.
3. **Economic inclusion:** jobs and skills in vulnerable regions.
4. **Grievance redress:** political dialogue in Balochistan and the merged districts; an end to enforced disappearances.
5. **Counter-narratives:** religious scholars, influencers, families and teachers; digital literacy.
6. **Online measures:** targeted removal of terrorist content with rights safeguards.
7. **De-radicalisation and reintegration** inside and outside prisons.
8. **Community policing** to detect early signs.
9. **Regional diplomacy** to deny sanctuaries.
10. **Youth participation** in politics and civic life.

**Online radicalisation and the role of families**
The internet has changed the escalation process. Militant groups use social media, encrypted messaging and videos to reach young people directly, bypassing local religious and community gatekeepers. Algorithms can push users toward more extreme content, creating echo chambers where violent views seem normal — a modern setting for Sutherland's differential association. The CSS 2020 paper asked exactly this. Responses include monitoring and removing terrorist content under PECA and through cooperation with platforms, but also positive alternatives: credible counter-messages by scholars and former militants, digital literacy in schools, and helplines for worried parents.
Families are often the first to notice changes — withdrawal, new peer groups, hostility toward family members' beliefs. Programmes that train mothers and teachers to recognise warning signs and seek help, as some de-radicalisation initiatives have done, can intervene before a young person reaches the top of Moghaddam's staircase. Women and families are therefore not only victims of terrorism but partners in preventing it.

**Avoiding counter-productive responses**
Heavy-handed measures can backfire. Enforced disappearances, collective punishment and the use of broad laws against peaceful critics create new grievances that recruiters exploit — the very push factors that drive radicalisation. Counter-terrorism is most effective when it is lawful, targeted and seen as fair.

**Measuring progress**
Success should be measured not only by the number of militants killed or arrested, but by fewer recruits, more convictions in anti-terrorism courts, fewer incidents of mob violence, and greater public confidence in the state's fairness.

**Conclusion**
Radicalism exists in Pakistan's society and politics, and in some cases it has escalated into terrorism through a recognisable process of grievance, ideology, group belonging and opportunity. Force can stop attacks, but only justice, education, inclusion and credible counter-narratives can stop people from climbing the staircase in the first place.

## Answer 8 (Section IV). Discuss the laws and institutional arrangements for controlling money laundering in Pakistan. *(CSS 2018; ML also 2016, 2017, 2020, 2021, 2025)*

**Outline**
1. Introduction and thesis
2. Meaning and stages of money laundering
3. Methods and risks in Pakistan
4. Legal framework
5. Institutional framework
6. FATF journey
7. Weaknesses
8. Way forward
9. Conclusion

**Introduction**
Money laundering is the lifeblood of organised crime, corruption and terrorism: it lets criminals enjoy their profits and fund further crime. Pakistan's place on the FATF grey list from June 2018 to October 2022 showed how weak controls damage a country's economy and reputation. **Pakistan now has a reasonably strong legal and institutional framework against money laundering, built largely under FATF pressure; the challenge is effective, non-political enforcement in a cash-based, informal economy.**

**1. Meaning and stages**
Money laundering is disguising the illegal origin of criminal proceeds. It has three stages: **placement** (cash enters the financial system), **layering** (complex transfers hide the trail) and **integration** (money returns as apparently legitimate wealth).

**2. Methods and risks in Pakistan**
Hawala and hundi; trade-based money laundering through over- and under-invoicing and misuse of Afghan transit trade; real estate bought in cash at undervalued prices; benami (proxy) accounts and properties, as in the fake bank accounts case (2018); cash smuggling; and, increasingly, crypto-assets. Predicate crimes include corruption, tax evasion, drug trafficking, smuggling and terrorism financing.

**3. Legal framework**
- **Anti-Money Laundering Act 2010** (replacing a 2007 ordinance), substantially **amended in 2020**: defines the offence and predicate offences; raises penalties; creates reporting duties for banks and designated non-financial businesses and professions; provides for freezing, seizure and confiscation; establishes the Financial Monitoring Unit.
- **Anti-Terrorism Act 1997:** terrorism financing offences and freezing of terrorist assets; proscription.
- **Control of Narcotic Substances Act 1997:** forfeiture of drug assets.
- **National Accountability Ordinance 1999:** corruption-related assets.
- **Benami Transactions (Prohibition) Act 2017.**
- **Foreign Exchange Regulation Act 1947** and SBP rules on exchange companies.
- **Mutual Legal Assistance (Criminal Matters) Act 2020.**
- **Companies Act 2017:** beneficial ownership disclosure.
- **UN commitments:** UNTOC and UNCAC.

**4. Institutional framework**
- **Financial Monitoring Unit (FMU):** the financial intelligence unit — receives and analyses suspicious and currency transaction reports and passes intelligence to law enforcement.
- **State Bank of Pakistan:** AML/CFT regulations for banks and exchange companies; customer due diligence; biometric verification.
- **SECP:** supervision of securities, insurance and non-bank finance; beneficial ownership registry.
- **FBR:** customs controls against trade-based laundering; supervision of certain designated businesses.
- **FIA:** investigates money laundering, hawala and terror financing.
- **NAB:** corruption-linked laundering by public office holders.
- **ANF:** drug money.
- **Provincial CTDs:** terror financing.
- **National coordination:** a national AML/CFT authority and periodic national risk assessments.

**5. The FATF journey**
Pakistan was grey-listed in June 2018. It completed a 27-point action plan on terror financing and a 7-point plan on money laundering derived from the Asia/Pacific Group's 2019 mutual evaluation. Reforms included the 2020 amendments, prosecutions and convictions for terror financing, better supervision of designated businesses, beneficial-ownership transparency and stronger inter-agency coordination. FATF removed Pakistan from the grey list in October 2022.

**6. Weaknesses**
1. A large cash and informal economy and widespread hawala.
2. Low conviction rates for money laundering itself.
3. Real estate sector opacity and undervaluation.
4. Political interference and selective accountability.
5. Limited capacity of FMU and investigators in financial analysis.
6. Trade-based laundering hard to detect.
7. Crypto-assets — the 2025 move to create a Pakistan Virtual Assets Regulatory Authority must be matched with strong supervision.
8. Limited international asset recovery.

**7. Way forward**
1. Expand digital payments (Raast) and reduce cash use.
2. Property registration at market values; digital land records.
3. Specialised financial-crime prosecutors and courts.
4. Better data sharing among FMU, FBR, NADRA, SBP and law enforcement.
5. Trade-data matching with partner countries against TBML.
6. Licensing and supervision of virtual-asset service providers, with the FATF travel rule.
7. Non-partisan enforcement to build credibility.
8. Preparation for the next APG mutual evaluation.

**Case illustration: trade-based laundering and crypto-assets**
CSS 2021 asked about trade-based money laundering and the risk of cryptocurrencies. In TBML, a trader over-invoices imports from a related company abroad, moving money out of Pakistan under cover of trade, or under-invoices exports to leave value abroad; misuse of Afghan transit trade adds another channel. Detection depends on comparing declared prices with international benchmarks, matching trade data with partner countries and sharing customs data with the FMU. Crypto-assets carry high potential risk because transfers are fast, cross-border and pseudonymous, but blockchains are traceable; the risk falls when exchanges are licensed, apply know-your-customer checks and follow FATF's travel rule.

**International comparison**
The UK's Criminal Finances Act 2017 introduced Unexplained Wealth Orders, requiring people to explain the source of assets disproportionate to their known income. Pakistan's own experience with plea bargains under the NAB law and benami legislation shows that asset-focused tools can work if they are applied fairly and not used selectively against political opponents.

**Enforcement and credibility**
Laws on paper do not deter if convictions are rare. Specialised financial-crime investigators, prosecutors trained in forensic accounting, and courts that decide cases within set time limits are needed. Equally important is credibility: if anti-money-laundering tools are seen as weapons against political opponents, public and international confidence declines. Even-handed enforcement against the powerful is the real test of the system.

**Role of the private sector**
Banks, exchange companies, real-estate agents, jewellers, lawyers and accountants are the gatekeepers of the financial system. Their customer due diligence, record-keeping and suspicious transaction reporting are the first line of defence; training them and sanctioning non-compliance turns them from potential enablers into partners.

**Conclusion**
Pakistan's anti-money-laundering framework has moved from weak to reasonably robust on paper, as the 2022 exit from the FATF grey list showed. Durable success, however, depends on shrinking the informal economy, enforcing the law even-handedly and keeping pace with new methods such as trade-based laundering and crypto-assets.

## Answer 9 (Section IV). Discuss community policing and the benefits and challenges of implementing it in Pakistan. How can the challenges be addressed? *(CSS 2024; 2022)*

**Outline**
1. Introduction and thesis
2. Meaning and origins
3. Core elements and models
4. Benefits for Pakistan
5. Pakistani experience
6. Challenges
7. Addressing the challenges
8. Conclusion

**Introduction**
Pakistan's police were designed to control people, not to work with them. Community policing reverses that logic: it treats citizens as partners in preventing crime. **Community policing can raise trust, intelligence and crime prevention in Pakistan, but it will remain a slogan unless the police organisation itself changes — in its culture, incentives and accountability.**

**1. Meaning and origins**
Community policing is a philosophy that promotes partnerships between the police and the community and proactive problem-solving to address the conditions that cause crime, disorder and fear of crime. Its roots go back to Sir Robert Peel's principles for the London Metropolitan Police (1829). In the 1980s and 1990s it spread in the US and UK, alongside Herman Goldstein's problem-oriented policing.

**2. Core elements**
- **Partnership** with residents, businesses, schools, religious leaders and NGOs.
- **Problem-solving** through the **SARA** model: scanning, analysis, response, assessment.
- **Organisational change:** decentralisation, geographic beat responsibility, foot patrol, less reliance on patrol cars, more face-to-face contact.
- **Prevention emphasis** rather than only reaction to calls.
- **Legitimacy** based on procedural justice: fair, respectful and transparent treatment (Tom Tyler).

**3. Benefits for Pakistan**
1. **Trust and legitimacy:** respectful contact can repair the "thana culture" image.
2. **Better intelligence:** residents share information on criminals and militants.
3. **Crime prevention:** solving local problems (dark streets, drug dens, encroachments) before crimes occur.
4. **Reduced fear of crime,** especially for women.
5. **Counter-radicalisation:** early warning through community and religious leaders.
6. **Efficient use of scarce resources:** communities help with low-level problems and dispute resolution.
7. **Accountability:** community forums give feedback on police performance.

**4. Pakistani experience**
- **Citizen-Police Liaison Committee (CPLC), Karachi (1989):** a citizen-run body that helped with kidnapping cases, vehicle theft and crime data.
- **Dispute Resolution Councils in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (2014):** panels of respected citizens resolve minor disputes at police stations, reducing FIRs and litigation.
- **Punjab's police service centres (Khidmat Markaz)** and complaint lines; women's help desks.
- **Police Order 2002** envisaged public safety commissions and citizen oversight.
- The Motorway Police's citizen-friendly image shows that a different police culture is possible.

**5. Challenges**
1. **Colonial and militarised culture:** officers trained for control resist partnership.
2. **Low public trust:** corruption, torture and refusal to register FIRs make citizens wary; recent encounter killings deepen distrust.
3. **Political interference:** local politicians want police loyalty, not community accountability.
4. **Staff shortages and frequent transfers:** beat officers cannot build relationships if moved every few months.
5. **Lack of training and incentives:** promotion rewards arrests and recoveries, not problem-solving.
6. **Elite capture:** committees dominated by influential people (landlords, traders) exclude the poor and women.
7. **Security threats:** in militancy-hit areas, community contacts put officers and informants at risk.
8. **Gender barriers:** few women officers; women cannot easily attend police forums.
9. **Weak legal framework:** public safety commissions were never fully established in many provinces.
10. **Resources:** community programmes lack budgets.

**6. Addressing the challenges**
1. **Legal backing:** functional public safety commissions and complaints authorities; community policing written into police law and rules.
2. **Stable tenure** for beat officers and SHOs.
3. **Training** in communication, problem-solving, human rights and gender sensitivity.
4. **Incentives:** performance indicators for community satisfaction and problems solved.
5. **Inclusive forums:** representation of women, minorities and youth; rotating membership to avoid capture.
6. **Women officers and desks** at every station.
7. **Technology:** apps and social media for complaints and feedback; public crime data.
8. **Accountability for abuse:** body cameras and independent inquiries.
9. **Pilot and scale:** start in selected districts, evaluate, expand.
10. **Partnerships** with NGOs, universities, schools and religious leaders.

**International models**
**Japan's koban** system places small neighbourhood police posts throughout cities; officers know residents, visit households and handle minor problems, contributing to very low crime and high trust. The **UK's** neighbourhood policing teams combine police officers with community support officers who focus on local priorities set with residents. **Chicago's Alternative Policing Strategy (CAPS, 1993)** introduced regular beat meetings between police and residents to identify and solve problems. Pakistan can adapt these ideas: small police posts (chowkis) in markets and katchi abadis staffed by the same officers for several years; regular beat meetings in mosques, schools and community halls; and joint problem-solving with municipal bodies on lighting, encroachments and drug dens.

**Measuring success**
Community policing should be judged by indicators beyond arrests: reporting rates, public satisfaction surveys, fear-of-crime surveys, number of problems solved, complaints against police, and repeat victimisation. Publishing these indicators would create incentives for police leaders to invest in partnership.

**Community policing and counter-terrorism**
In areas affected by militancy, community policing can provide early warning — residents notice strangers, new recruiters or changes in young people's behaviour. But it must be done carefully: if communities feel used only as informants, or if information leads to abuses, trust collapses. Protecting informants' identities, acting lawfully and showing results that benefit the community are essential.

**Role of women officers**
Women officers are central to community policing in Pakistan. Many women will not visit a police station staffed only by men; women's desks and female beat officers make it possible to report domestic violence, harassment and child abuse, and to involve women in community safety forums.

**Conclusion**
Community policing offers Pakistan a way to turn the public from suspects into partners. Its benefits — trust, information, prevention — are proven elsewhere and visible in local successes such as the CPLC and KP's Dispute Resolution Councils. But it cannot be added onto a colonial structure as a project; it requires a change of police culture, incentives and oversight. With that change, it can become the foundation of law and order.
