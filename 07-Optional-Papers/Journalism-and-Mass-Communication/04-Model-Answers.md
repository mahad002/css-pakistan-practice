## Model Answer 1: How have media laws in Pakistan evolved historically, and how have they influenced freedom of expression and press control across political regimes?

*(CSS 2026 Q2; similar 2019, 2025; note 2022.)*

**Outline**
1. Introduction
2. Colonial foundations
3. Early Pakistan and Ayub's PPO
4. Bhutto and Zia
5. Democratic decade and the RPPPO
6. Musharraf: liberalisation and the 2002 laws
7. The digital era: PECA and its 2025 amendments
8. Assessment across regimes
9. Way forward
10. Conclusion

**Introduction**

Article 19 of the Constitution guarantees freedom of speech and of the press, subject to "reasonable restrictions" for the glory of Islam, security, public order, morality, friendly relations and contempt of court. In practice, how free the media have been has depended less on Article 19 than on the ordinary laws used to regulate them. This answer traces those laws from colonial rule to the PECA amendments of 2025 and argues that the instruments of control have changed — from presses to broadcast licences to online platforms — while the underlying logic of control has stayed remarkably constant.

**Colonial foundations**

British India built the toolkit Pakistan inherited. Wellesley's Censorship Act (1799) and Adams' Licensing Regulations (1823) introduced pre-censorship and licensing. Metcalfe's Press Act (1835) briefly liberalised, but the "Gagging Act" of 1857 and Lytton's Vernacular Press Act (1878) targeted Indian-language newspapers. The Indian Penal Code (1860) added criminal defamation and, from 1870, sedition. The Indian Press Act (1910) demanded security deposits and was used against Muslim papers such as Maulana Muhammad Ali Jauhar's *Comrade* and Maulana Zafar Ali Khan's *Zamindar*. The Official Secrets Act (1923) still operates in Pakistan.

**Early Pakistan and Ayub's PPO**

After 1947 the colonial laws stayed, joined by the Security of Pakistan Act 1952. Ayub Khan went further: in 1959 the government took over Progressive Papers Limited, and the Press and Publications Ordinance 1963 required declarations and security deposits and let officials seize presses and ban newspapers. The National Press Trust (1964) placed major papers under government control. PTV (1964) and Radio Pakistan were state monopolies.

**Bhutto and Zia**

Bhutto's civilian government kept the PPO and arrested critical editors. Zia's martial law brought pre-censorship, bans on papers such as *Musawat*, and military courts. In May 1978, during the PFUJ's press freedom movement, journalists were publicly flogged in Lahore — the low point of Pakistani press freedom.

**Democratic decade and the RPPPO**

The Registration of Printing Presses and Publications Ordinance 1988 replaced the PPO, simplified declarations and reduced official discretion. It was a real advance, though it remained an ordinance and governments still used advertising, tax cases and newsprint quotas to pressure owners.

**Musharraf: liberalisation and control**

The 2002 package transformed the landscape. The PEMRA Ordinance opened broadcasting to private companies; the Press Council Ordinance, the Defamation Ordinance and the Freedom of Information Ordinance followed. Dozens of private channels appeared. Yet when the media covered the lawyers' movement, the November 2007 emergency took Geo and ARY off air and amended PEMRA rules to restrict live coverage. Liberalisation and control came from the same regime.

**The digital era**

Civilian governments after 2008 added rights — Article 19-A on the right to information (2010), the Right of Access to Information Act 2017, and the Protection of Journalists and Media Professionals Act 2021 — but also new restrictions. The Prevention of Electronic Crimes Act 2016 criminalised online defamation (section 20) and let the PTA block "unlawful" content (section 37). PEMRA's 2023 amendment defined misinformation and disinformation. Punjab's Defamation Act 2024 created special tribunals with large interim damages; its key sections remain under challenge in the Lahore High Court. Most significantly, the PECA (Amendment) Act 2025 created a "fake news" offence punishable by up to three years and a Rs 2 million fine, a Social Media Protection and Regulatory Authority and a new cybercrime agency. X itself was blocked from February 2024 to May 2025.

**Assessment across regimes**

1. *Military regimes* relied on ordinances, censorship and ownership takeovers, but Musharraf also produced the most significant liberalisation.
2. *Civilian governments* passed rights-based laws yet used PEMRA, PECA and government advertising against critics.
3. *Constant tools*: licensing, discretionary advertising, and broadly worded restrictions on "national security", "public order" and religion.
4. *Changing targets*: presses (colonial era and PPO), broadcast licences (PEMRA), and now online speech and platforms (PECA).
5. *Resistance*: courts struck down some measures (the PECA ordinance of 2022, as reported; sedition by the Lahore High Court in 2023), and unions such as the PFUJ have challenged the 2025 amendments.

International indices reflect this: Reporters Without Borders ranked Pakistan 158th of 180 countries in 2025 and 153rd in 2026, in its "very serious" category.

**Way forward**

1. Narrow vague offences such as "fake news" and decriminalise defamation, relying on civil remedies.
2. Make PEMRA and SMPRA independent through transparent parliamentary appointments, security of tenure and judicial review.
3. Base government advertising on audited reach, not loyalty.
4. Fully operationalise the journalists' protection commission set up in November 2025.
5. Strengthen self-regulation through the Press Council and a broadcasters' complaints body.

**Conclusion**

Pakistan's media laws have evolved in form but not in spirit. Each era built new instruments to manage the media of its time, while promising freedom on paper. Real change requires laws that are narrow, necessary and proportionate, regulators independent of the government of the day, and a media industry able to regulate itself credibly.

## Model Answer 2: Discuss the role of Development Support Communication in Pakistan's progress. Explain its methods and difficulties, with examples.

*(CSS 2024 Q6; similar 2017, 2023, 2025.)*

**Outline**
1. Introduction and definitions
2. DSC compared with development communication and development journalism
3. Theoretical evolution
4. Role in Pakistan: sectors and examples
5. Methods
6. Difficulties
7. Way forward
8. Conclusion

**Introduction**

Nora Quebral defined development communication as the art and science of human communication applied to the speedy transformation of a country from poverty to dynamic growth with greater equality. Development support communication (DSC), a term associated with Erskine Childers and the UNDP in the late 1960s, is narrower: the planned use of communication to make specific development programmes succeed by informing, motivating and training the people they serve. This answer argues that DSC has delivered some of Pakistan's clearest social gains, but its potential is limited by top-down design, low trust and the digital divide.

**DSC, development communication and development journalism**

Development communication is the broad field of communication for social change. DSC is the communication component of particular projects, such as a polio campaign or a farm extension programme. Development journalism reports critically on whether development is working. All three matter, but DSC is the operational arm.

**Theoretical evolution**

The dominant paradigm of the 1950s–60s (Lerner, Schramm and Rogers' diffusion of innovations) treated mass media as "magic multipliers" carrying modern ideas top-down. Its failures — inequality, ignored local knowledge, blaming "traditional" people — produced the alternative paradigm: Paulo Freire's dialogue and conscientisation, Schumacher's "small is beautiful", participatory and multiplicity approaches (Servaes), and social marketing (Kotler and Zaltman, 1971). Modern DSC combines research, participation and multiple channels.

**Role in Pakistan**

*Health.* The Lady Health Worker Programme (1994) uses interpersonal communication by local women to promote family planning, immunisation and maternal care in millions of households. Polio eradication relies on community mobilisers, endorsements from ulema and celebrities, and myth-busting after vaccine rumours spread. During COVID-19, the National Command and Operation Centre's daily briefings and the 1166 helpline became a model of crisis communication.

*Education and literacy.* Allama Iqbal Open University has used radio and television for distance learning since 1974. During school closures in 2020, PTV's Taleem Ghar teleschool and radio lessons reached children at home. Enrolment campaigns and girls' stipend schemes rely on community outreach.

*Agriculture and climate.* Radio farm programmes, extension services and helplines spread new seeds and practices; NDMA and PMD warnings for floods and heatwaves, as in 2022, save lives.

*Social protection and governance.* BISP and Ehsaas used SMS and community campaigns to register beneficiaries, most of them women. The ECP's voter education and women's registration drives helped narrow the gender gap on electoral rolls.

*Social issues.* Campaigns on child abuse, dowry, tolerance and road safety, and entertainment-education dramas such as *Udaari* on child sexual abuse, show how media can open taboo topics.

**Methods**

1. Audience research and segmentation before designing messages.
2. Mass media: TV, radio, newspapers, public service announcements.
3. Interpersonal channels: health workers, extension agents, teachers, imams, community elders (two-step flow).
4. Folk media: puppetry, street theatre, Sufi poetry and melas.
5. Social marketing: product, price, place and promotion applied to behaviours (Greenstar's family planning work since 1991).
6. Entertainment-education dramas.
7. ICTs: SMS, WhatsApp, mobile apps, telemedicine (Sehat Kahani), dashboards.
8. Monitoring and evaluation to adapt campaigns.

**Difficulties**

1. *Low literacy and linguistic diversity*: national campaigns in Urdu miss many audiences.
2. *Top-down design*: officials decide messages without research or community participation.
3. *Low trust and conspiracy theories*: polio vaccine refusal, fuelled by rumours and the misuse of a fake vaccination campaign in 2011, shows how credibility can collapse.
4. *Security threats*: polio workers have been attacked and killed.
5. *Cultural and religious sensitivities*: family planning messages face resistance unless framed with religious support.
6. *Fragmentation*: weak coordination after devolution, short donor-funded projects.
7. *Digital divide*: the GSMA reported a gender gap in mobile ownership of about 27% in 2025.
8. *Commercial media*: little free airtime for public service messages.
9. *Weak evaluation*: success is measured by outputs (spots aired) rather than behaviour change.

**Way forward**

1. A national Communication for Development strategy with audience research units in key ministries.
2. A PEMRA licence condition requiring free public service airtime.
3. Local-language, culturally grounded content co-created with communities.
4. Partnerships with ulema, teachers, influencers and local radio.
5. Closing the digital gender gap so that ICT-based DSC reaches women.
6. Independent impact evaluation tied to funding.

**Conclusion**

DSC turns development plans into changes in people's lives. Pakistan's Lady Health Workers, polio campaigns and COVID-19 communication show what it can achieve. But one-way, Urdu-only, government-voiced campaigns cannot overcome distrust and diversity. Moving from telling people to talking with them is the key to making DSC a driver of progress.

## Model Answer 3: Define public relations. Is PR a tool of governance? Discuss its role in shaping organisational image, stakeholder relations and as an early-warning system.

*(Combines CSS 2026 Q6 and Q8(a) with 2019 Q6.)*

**Outline**
1. Introduction and definitions
2. Evolution of PR: Grunig and Hunt's models
3. PR, image and stakeholders
4. PR as a tool of governance
5. PR as an early-warning system
6. Pakistan: PID, ISPR and recent examples
7. Risks: spin and propaganda
8. Way forward
9. Conclusion

**Introduction**

The Chartered Institute of Public Relations defines PR as the planned and sustained effort to establish and maintain goodwill and mutual understanding between an organisation and its publics. The PRSA calls it a strategic communication process that builds mutually beneficial relationships. In government, PR is not decoration but part of governing: citizens cannot support, comply with or hold accountable policies they do not understand. This answer argues that PR is a vital tool of governance and corporate management when it is two-way and truthful, and a liability when it becomes spin.

**Evolution**

James Grunig and Todd Hunt (1984) identified four models: press agentry (publicity regardless of truth, P.T. Barnum), public information (truthful one-way dissemination, Ivy Lee's 1906 *Declaration of Principles*), two-way asymmetric (research-based persuasion, Edward Bernays) and two-way symmetric (dialogue for mutual understanding). Grunig's Excellence Study (1992) found the symmetric model most effective and ethical.

**PR, organisational image and stakeholders**

Image is the public's current perception; reputation is long-term judgement built on behaviour. PR shapes both by identifying stakeholders — employees, customers, investors, regulators, media and communities (Freeman's stakeholder theory) — and maintaining relationships with each through tailored messages, consistent identity, listening and responsive action.

*Corporate examples*: Johnson & Johnson's Tylenol recall (1982) protected its reputation through candour; in Pakistan, banks' digital-fraud awareness campaigns, telecom companies' flood-relief CSR and sports sponsorships such as the PSL build trust and visibility.

*Government examples*: NDMA's flood warnings, FBR taxpayer campaigns and the Pakistan Citizen Portal (2018), which lets citizens file complaints and see responses, all shape the state's image as competent and responsive.

**PR as a tool of governance**

1. *Information and transparency*: explaining policies, services and rights, and fulfilling Article 19-A and the RTI Act 2017.
2. *Legitimacy and trust*: explaining hard decisions such as IMF-linked tax measures or energy price changes.
3. *Two-way feedback*: surveys, social listening and complaint systems tell government what citizens need.
4. *Behaviour change*: vaccination, tax compliance, energy conservation and road safety campaigns.
5. *Crisis communication*: one voice, speed, accuracy and empathy in disasters and pandemics.
6. *Countering misinformation*: rapid, credible rebuttals.
7. *Public diplomacy*: national image, tourism and investment promotion.

**PR as an early-warning system**

Issues management (Howard Chase, 1976) treats PR as an organisation's radar. By monitoring media, social media and stakeholder opinion, PR detects emerging problems — public anger over prices, rumours of shortages, protests forming online — before they become crises, and advises leaders to act early. For example, monitoring social media in 2020 could have spotted panic over fuel shortages earlier; continuous scanning helps NDMA anticipate misinformation during floods. A PR department that only issues press releases after decisions are made wastes this function.

**Pakistan's machinery**

The Ministry of Information and Broadcasting oversees the Press Information Department, headed by the Principal Information Officer. The PID issues press releases and press notes, arranges press conferences, accredits journalists, monitors media and has historically handled government advertising. Provinces have Directorates General of Public Relations; the armed forces have ISPR (1949). The NCOC's daily COVID-19 briefings in 2020–21 are widely cited as effective governance communication: consistent, data-based and coordinated.

Weaknesses persist: the PID is often seen as the ruling party's mouthpiece; advertising has been used to reward and punish outlets; responses to online disinformation are slow; research and evaluation are rare.

**Risks: spin and propaganda**

PR officers are called "spin doctors" when they frame failures as successes, plant stories or withhold information. Such tactics may win a news cycle but destroy credibility, and in governance, lost credibility makes every later campaign harder. Ethical codes — the IPRA Code of Athens (1965) and the PRSA code — require honesty, disclosure and respect for the public interest.

**Way forward**

1. Reform the PID into a professional, non-partisan public information service.
2. Transparent, reach-based government advertising criteria.
3. Social listening and issues-management units in key ministries.
4. Crisis communication plans tested through drills.
5. Training at the Information Service Academy in digital and crisis communication.
6. Proactive disclosure under RTI laws.

**Conclusion**

PR is a tool of governance when it informs, listens and warns; it becomes a tool of manipulation when it spins. Governments and companies in Pakistan already use PR, but often in its one-way, press-agentry form. Adopting the two-way symmetric model would strengthen both organisational reputations and public trust in the state.

## Model Answer 4: Explain Everett Rogers' Diffusion of Innovations theory. How do adopter categories explain the spread of new ideas, and what factors decide the success or failure of innovations?

*(CSS 2026 Q7; similar 2021, 2022, 2023.)*

**Outline**
1. Introduction
2. Elements of diffusion
3. Innovation-decision process
4. Adopter categories and the S-curve
5. Attributes that decide adoption
6. Other factors in success or failure
7. Application to Pakistan
8. Criticisms
9. Conclusion

**Introduction**

Everett Rogers' *Diffusion of Innovations* (1962; fifth edition 2003) is one of the most cited theories in communication. Building on Ryan and Gross's 1943 study of hybrid seed corn among Iowa farmers, Rogers defined diffusion as the process by which an innovation is communicated through certain channels over time among the members of a social system. This answer explains the theory, applies it to Pakistan and argues that it remains useful if its pro-innovation and top-down biases are corrected.

**Elements of diffusion**

1. *Innovation*: an idea, practice or object perceived as new.
2. *Communication channels*: mass media create awareness; interpersonal channels persuade.
3. *Time*: the innovation-decision process, adopter categories and rate of adoption.
4. *Social system*: norms, opinion leaders and change agents shape adoption.

**Innovation-decision process**

Knowledge → persuasion → decision (adopt or reject) → implementation → confirmation. Mass media matter most at the knowledge stage; peers and opinion leaders at persuasion and decision. Rejection can happen at any stage, and users may "re-invent" the innovation.

**Adopter categories**

Rogers divided adopters along a bell curve by innovativeness:

| Category | Share | Traits |
|---|---|---|
| Innovators | 2.5% | Venturesome, cosmopolitan, can absorb losses |
| Early adopters | 13.5% | Respected opinion leaders; the key target |
| Early majority | 34% | Deliberate, follow after seeing results |
| Late majority | 34% | Sceptical, adopt from necessity or pressure |
| Laggards | 16% | Traditional, isolated, oriented to the past |

Cumulative adoption forms an S-curve: slow at first, accelerating once early adopters reach a critical mass, then levelling off. The categories explain spread because each group watches the one before it. Early adopters are crucial: they reduce uncertainty for the majority. Campaigns therefore target respected local figures first.

**Attributes that decide adoption**

1. *Relative advantage*: better than what it replaces.
2. *Compatibility*: fits values, experience and needs.
3. *Complexity*: harder innovations spread slowly.
4. *Trialability*: can be tried in small steps.
5. *Observability*: results are visible.

**Other factors in success or failure**

The credibility of the source and change agents; cost and infrastructure; government policy; religious and cultural acceptability; the strength of opinion leaders; homophily between change agents and clients; and how the innovation is communicated. The failure of 3-D television — costly, uncomfortable glasses and little content — shows low relative advantage and compatibility. Successes such as mobile phones show high advantage and observability.

**Application to Pakistan**

*Green Revolution (1960s)*: new seeds and fertilisers spread fast among larger farmers with credit and irrigation, while smallholders lagged — the "equality issue".

*Family planning*: low compatibility with religious and family norms slowed adoption; framing birth spacing through religious scholars and doorstep counselling by Lady Health Workers improved it, though contraceptive prevalence remains low.

*Polio vaccine*: rumours, militancy and the misuse of a fake vaccination campaign in 2011 destroyed source credibility, making Pakistan one of the last polio-endemic countries. The lesson is that compatibility and trust matter more than technical advantage.

*Mobile money and digital payments*: Easypaisa (2009), JazzCash and the State Bank's Raast (2021) offered clear advantage and observability, and spread quickly.

*Rooftop solar*: rising electricity tariffs gave solar high relative advantage, and visible panels on neighbours' roofs increased observability, driving rapid adoption in recent years.

*COVID-19 vaccines (2021)*: initial hesitancy gave way after mandates, celebrity and religious endorsements, and visible uptake.

**Criticisms**

1. *Pro-innovation bias*: assumes innovations are good.
2. *Individual-blame bias*: calls people "laggards" rather than examining poverty or poor access.
3. *Recall problem*: depends on memory of adoption.
4. *Equality issue*: diffusion can widen gaps between rich and poor.
5. *Top-down*: rooted in the dominant paradigm; ignores participation. Rogers himself declared the "passing of the dominant paradigm" in 1976.

**Conclusion**

Diffusion of innovations explains why some ideas sweep through societies while others stall. Its adopter categories show the importance of early adopters and opinion leaders, and its five attributes give planners a checklist. In Pakistan, it explains the success of mobile money and the struggles of polio and family planning campaigns. Used with attention to trust, equity and participation, it remains a powerful guide for development communication.

## Model Answer 5: Select any two normative theories of the press and discuss them in their historical context. How did they reflect mid-20th-century political conditions, and what criticisms have been raised about their applicability?

*(CSS 2026 Q4; similar 2019 Q3; social responsibility theory 2023.)*

**Outline**
1. Introduction
2. *Four Theories of the Press* and its context
3. Libertarian theory
4. Social responsibility theory
5. How they reflected mid-20th-century conditions
6. Criticisms
7. Applicability to Pakistan
8. Conclusion

**Introduction**

Normative theories describe how the media ought to operate in a given political and social system. The classic statement is *Four Theories of the Press* (1956) by Fred Siebert, Theodore Peterson and Wilbur Schramm, which identified authoritarian, libertarian, social responsibility and Soviet communist theories. This answer examines the libertarian and social responsibility theories, shows how they mirrored the politics of the 1940s and 1950s, and argues that, while outdated as a classification, they still provide useful standards for judging media systems, including Pakistan's.

**Context of the four theories**

The book was written in the United States at the height of the Cold War, shortly after the Second World War and the rise of the Soviet bloc. Its central claim was that the press "takes on the form and coloration of the social and political structures within which it operates". Authoritarian theory traced back to Tudor England's licensing; Soviet theory to Lenin's view of the press as propagandist, agitator and organiser. The two "free" theories were the libertarian and the social responsibility models.

**Libertarian theory**

*Origins.* It grew from the Enlightenment: John Milton's *Areopagitica* (1644) argued against licensing; John Locke and later John Stuart Mill's *On Liberty* (1859) defended free expression; the First Amendment to the US Constitution (1791) barred Congress from abridging press freedom.

*Principles.* People are rational and can distinguish truth from falsehood in a free "marketplace of ideas", where truth wins through a "self-righting process". The press should be privately owned, free from prior restraint and licensing, and able to criticise government. Its functions are to inform, entertain, sell and, crucially, act as a check on government.

*Achievements.* It produced vigorous independent journalism, from exposing corruption to the later Watergate investigation.

*Weaknesses.* By the twentieth century the market had produced concentration, sensationalism and newspapers controlled by a few owners. The marketplace of ideas was not open to all.

**Social responsibility theory**

*Origins.* It arose from the **Hutchins Commission on Freedom of the Press**, whose report *A Free and Responsible Press* (1947) responded to concerns about monopoly ownership, yellow journalism and the power of radio and cinema.

*Principles.* Freedom carries obligations. The press must provide a truthful, comprehensive and intelligent account of events in a meaningful context; serve as a forum for comment and criticism; present a representative picture of society's groups; clarify society's goals and values; and give full access to information. If the media fail to regulate themselves through codes, press councils and professional standards, government may intervene as a last resort.

*Achievements.* It inspired journalistic codes of ethics, press councils, public service broadcasting such as the BBC model, and ideas of objectivity and balance.

**How they reflected mid-20th-century conditions**

1. *Cold War ideology*: the book divided the world into "free" systems (libertarian, social responsibility) and "unfree" ones (authoritarian, Soviet), echoing US foreign policy.
2. *Post-war anxiety about mass media*: Nazi propaganda and the power of radio made scholars worry about media power, hence responsibility.
3. *American industrial conditions*: newspaper chains and declining competition pushed the shift from pure liberty to responsibility.
4. *Welfare-state thinking*: the idea that freedom must serve society mirrored broader acceptance of social obligations in the New Deal era.

**Criticisms**

1. *Cold War product*: John Nerone and colleagues in *Last Rights* (1995) argued that all four theories are seen through a liberal lens.
2. *Normative, not empirical*: real systems mix features.
3. *Western-centric*: little room for Asian, African, Latin American or Islamic traditions; developing countries' needs ignored — hence McQuail's later development and democratic-participant theories (1983).
4. *Ignores commercial censorship*: libertarian theory overlooks the power of owners and advertisers, as Herman and Chomsky's propaganda model (1988) shows.
5. *Vague "responsibility"*: governments can use it to justify censorship.
6. *Outdated*: the Soviet collapse and the internet changed media systems. Hallin and Mancini's *Comparing Media Systems* (2004) and Christians and others' *Normative Theories of the Media* (2009) offer better frameworks.

**Applicability to Pakistan**

Pakistan's system is a hybrid. Its private, competitive media resemble the libertarian model; PEMRA's 2015 code of conduct and the Press Council's ethical code use social responsibility language (accuracy, privacy, no glorification of terrorism, protection of victims). But authoritarian practices persist through licensing, advertising pressure, PECA prosecutions and bans on live coverage, such as PEMRA's October 2026 directive. Hallin and Mancini's "polarised pluralist" model, with its political parallelism and clientelism, describes Pakistan's polarised talk-show culture better than any of the four theories.

**Conclusion**

The libertarian and social responsibility theories capture an important evolution — from freedom from the state to freedom with obligations to society. They were shaped by the Cold War and by American anxieties about media power, and they ignore much of the world. Yet their core questions remain essential: who controls the media, whom do they serve, and how can freedom and responsibility be balanced without inviting censorship?

## Model Answer 6: Critically examine the impact of cultural imperialism on contemporary communication practices. How do dominant cultures influence the communication landscape, and what are the effects on cultural diversity and indigenous identities?

*(CSS 2024 Q4; similar 2016, 2018, 2022.)*

**Outline**
1. Introduction
2. The theory of cultural imperialism
3. Mechanisms of influence
4. Effects on diversity and identity
5. Critiques: active audiences, contra-flows, hybridity
6. From media to platform imperialism
7. Pakistan
8. Way forward
9. Conclusion

**Introduction**

Cultural imperialism refers to the domination of one culture over others through media and cultural products, leading to the erosion of local cultures and the spread of the dominant culture's values. The idea emerged in the 1960s and 1970s, when the US dominated film, television and news. This answer argues that the classic theory overstated audiences' passivity, but that a new form of dominance — by global digital platforms — gives its central concern fresh relevance.

**The theory**

Herbert Schiller's *Mass Communications and American Empire* (1969) argued that a US media-military-industrial complex exported content and consumerist values. Jeremy Tunstall's *The Media Are American* (1977), Dorfman and Mattelart's *How to Read Donald Duck* (1971) and Oliver Boyd-Barrett's concept of media imperialism (1977) developed the case. A UNESCO study by Nordenstreng and Varis (1974) found television traffic to be largely "a one-way street" from a few Western countries. The Non-Aligned Movement's call for a New World Information and Communication Order and the MacBride Commission's report *Many Voices, One World* (1980) responded to this imbalance.

**Mechanisms of influence**

1. *Ownership*: global conglomerates and now platforms own distribution.
2. *Economics*: programmes already profitable at home can be sold cheaply abroad.
3. *News agencies*: Reuters, AP and AFP historically set the international agenda.
4. *Advertising and brands*: global consumer culture.
5. *Language*: English dominates science, the internet and business.
6. *Technology and standards*: platforms' algorithms and terms of service decide visibility.

**Effects on diversity and identity**

Critics point to homogenisation of tastes, decline of local languages and art forms, consumerism, distorted images of developing countries ("coups and earthquakes" coverage), dependence of local media on imported content, and identity anxieties among young people torn between global and local norms. Indigenous and minority cultures, with the smallest markets, are most exposed.

**Critiques of the thesis**

1. *Active audiences*: Liebes and Katz's *The Export of Meaning* (1990) showed viewers in different cultures read the soap opera *Dallas* in their own ways — Stuart Hall's negotiated and oppositional decoding.
2. *Cultural proximity*: Joseph Straubhaar (1991) found audiences prefer local or regional content when available.
3. *Contra-flows*: Bollywood, Brazilian telenovelas, Korean K-dramas and K-pop, Turkish dramas and Al Jazeera show that flows run in many directions (Daya Thussu).
4. *Hybridity and glocalisation*: Roland Robertson's term describes global forms adapted locally, such as Coke Studio Pakistan's fusion of global production with folk and Sufi music.
5. *Romanticising "authentic" culture*: cultures have always changed through contact.

**From media imperialism to platform imperialism**

Dal Yong Jin (2015) argues that dominance has shifted from content to infrastructure. A handful of US companies — and increasingly Chinese ones such as TikTok's owner — control search, social media, app stores and advertising. Local news outlets lose advertising revenue to these platforms; algorithms favour content in dominant languages; AI systems trained mostly on Western data may reproduce Western norms. Communication practices worldwide, from how news is written for search engines to how politicians campaign, are shaped by platform logic.

**Pakistan**

In the 1990s satellite dishes and cable brought Indian films and Western shows, provoking fears for national culture. The private channel boom after 2002 reduced dependence: Pakistani dramas now attract large audiences in India, the Gulf and the diaspora, and Coke Studio's "Pasoori" (2022) reached global audiences. Turkish dramas such as *Diriliş: Ertuğrul*, aired on PTV from 2020, show a regional contra-flow framed as shared Muslim heritage. Yet young Pakistanis spend increasing time on global platforms, regional languages are under pressure, and policy debates swing between protection (bans on Indian content, PEMRA rules on foreign content) and openness.

**Way forward**

1. Invest in quality local content — drama, film, animation and children's programming in Urdu and regional languages.
2. Strengthen public service broadcasting and digital platforms for Pakistani content.
3. Promote co-productions and cultural exports as soft power.
4. Media and digital literacy so audiences engage critically.
5. Fair platform regulation that protects local news revenue and local-language moderation without censorship.

**Conclusion**

The classic cultural imperialism thesis exaggerated audiences' passivity and missed the rise of regional and Southern media. But inequality in global communication has not disappeared; it has moved from programme catalogues to platforms and algorithms. Preserving cultural diversity today means building strong local creative industries and fair rules for the digital infrastructure through which all cultures now travel.

*(about 1,050 words including headings)*
