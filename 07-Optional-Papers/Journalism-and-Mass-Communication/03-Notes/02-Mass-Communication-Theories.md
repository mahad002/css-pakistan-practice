## 2.1 The four eras of media effects theory (2018)

Following Stanley Baran and Dennis Davis (*Mass Communication Theory*), with McQuail's phases:

| Era | Period | View of effects | Theories | Context |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Mass society and propaganda (powerful effects)** | 1920s–1930s | Media are all-powerful; audiences passive, isolated, defenceless | Mass society theory; hypodermic needle/magic bullet; Lasswell's propaganda studies | WWI propaganda, rise of radio and cinema, Nazi propaganda (Goebbels), Payne Fund studies (1929–32) on films and children |
| **2. Limited effects (scientific/empirical)** | 1940s–1960s | Media mostly reinforce existing views; personal influence matters more | Two-step flow (Lazarsfeld 1944; Katz and Lazarsfeld 1955); selective exposure, perception, retention; Hovland's persuasion experiments (Yale, 1940s–50s); Klapper's reinforcement thesis (*The Effects of Mass Communication*, 1960) | Survey research, social psychology |
| **3. Moderate / cultural and critical (return to stronger, indirect effects)** | 1960s–1980s | Effects are cumulative, cognitive and long term; media shape agendas, perceptions and ideology | Agenda-setting (McCombs and Shaw 1972), cultivation (Gerbner 1970s), spiral of silence (Noelle-Neumann 1974), uses and gratifications (Katz et al. 1973–74), knowledge gap (Tichenor et al. 1970), dependency (Ball-Rokeach and DeFleur 1976), McLuhan's "powerful media revisited" (1964), critical/cultural studies (Frankfurt School, Birmingham School, Stuart Hall 1973), hegemony | Television era, social movements |
| **4. Meaning-making / negotiated effects (and digital)** | 1990s–present | Audiences actively construct meaning with media; effects are real but negotiated; networked and algorithmic media | Framing (Entman 1993), reception studies, social constructionism, media literacy, network society (Castells 1996), convergence culture (Jenkins 2006), filter bubbles (Pariser 2011), algorithmic gatekeeping | Internet, social media, AI |

**Exam tip**: a 2016-type question ("which effects model applies to Pakistan?") is best answered: no single model; powerful effects in crises and among low-literacy audiences (rumours, WhatsApp misinformation, sectarian incitement); limited effects in voting, where biradari and opinion leaders dominate; moderate/cumulative effects through agenda-setting on talk shows and cultivation through dramas.

## 2.2 Normative theories of the press (2019, 2023, 2026)

**Normative theories** describe how media *ought* to operate under a given political and social system. Source: **Fred Siebert, Theodore Peterson and Wilbur Schramm, *Four Theories of the Press* (1956)**.

| Theory | Origin | Ownership and control | Role of media | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Authoritarian** | 16th–17th century Europe after the printing press; Tudor England, absolute monarchy (Plato, Machiavelli, Hobbes) | Private or public, but licensed; censorship; criticism of rulers forbidden | Support and advance state policy | Licensing Act (England, 1662); colonial press laws in India; military regimes |
| **Libertarian (free press)** | 17th–18th century; Milton's *Areopagitica* (1644), Locke, J.S. Mill's *On Liberty* (1859); First Amendment (1791) | Private; free market; no prior restraint | Inform, entertain, sell, and check government — "marketplace of ideas", "self-righting process" | US, UK press |
| **Social responsibility** | 20th-century US; **Hutchins Commission on Freedom of the Press, *A Free and Responsible Press* (1947)** | Private, but with obligations to society; self-regulation, codes, press councils; government intervention as last resort | Freedom with responsibility: truthful, comprehensive, contextual news; a forum for comment; represent all groups; present society's goals and values; give full access to information | Press councils, public service broadcasting (BBC), PEMRA code, PCP |
| **Soviet communist** | After 1917 Russian Revolution; Marx, Lenin, Stalin | State/party owned | Serve the party and the working class; propaganda, agitation, organisation (Lenin) | Pravda, Soviet TV |

**Social responsibility theory (2023).** The Hutchins Commission (chaired by Robert Hutchins, 1942–47) responded to concentration of ownership and sensationalism. It held that freedom carries a duty: if the press fails to self-regulate, society may intervene. Its five requirements: (1) a truthful, comprehensive and intelligent account of the day's events in a context that gives them meaning; (2) a forum for exchange of comment and criticism; (3) a representative picture of the constituent groups in society; (4) presentation and clarification of society's goals and values; (5) full access to the day's intelligence. Importance in Pakistan: justifies codes of conduct (PEMRA's 2015 code, PCP's ethical code), responsible coverage of terrorism (no glorification), sectarian sensitivity, victims' privacy, election coverage rules. Danger: "responsibility" can be used as a pretext for censorship (e.g. bans on live coverage of attacks, 2023 and Oct 2026 PEMRA directives).

**Later additions (Denis McQuail, 1983):**
- **Development media theory**: in developing countries, media should support national development, nation-building, cultural identity; freedom may be restricted for development priorities. Linked to development journalism; popular with post-colonial governments (e.g. Pakistan's PTV and Radio Pakistan mandates).
- **Democratic-participant theory**: media should be small-scale, horizontal, participatory, serving communities and minorities, against commercialisation and state monopoly (community radio, alternative media).

**Criticisms of the four theories (2019, 2026).**
1. **Cold War product**: written in the US in 1956; frames the world as free (libertarian/social responsibility) vs unfree (authoritarian/Soviet). John Nerone et al., *Last Rights: Revisiting Four Theories of the Press* (1995), showed it treats all four through a liberal lens.
2. **Not empirically based**: normative, not descriptive; real systems are mixed.
3. **Western-centric**: ignores Asian, African and Islamic traditions; developing countries do not fit.
4. **Ignores commercial pressures**: libertarian theory assumes the market guarantees freedom, but advertisers and owners also censor (Herman and Chomsky).
5. **Outdated** after the Soviet collapse and the internet.
6. **Replacements**: Daniel Hallin and Paolo Mancini, *Comparing Media Systems* (2004): **Liberal** (US, UK), **Democratic Corporatist** (northern Europe), **Polarized Pluralist** (Mediterranean: strong state, political parallelism, clientelism). Christians, Glasser, McQuail, Nordenstreng and White, *Normative Theories of the Media* (2009): four roles — **monitorial, facilitative, radical, collaborative**.

**How the theories reflected mid-20th-century conditions (2026).** Authoritarian and Soviet models mirrored fascism and communism; libertarian mirrored US free-market liberalism; social responsibility reflected worry about monopoly press and radio in post-war America; the whole framework was part of US Cold War ideology.

**Pakistan.** A hybrid: libertarian in form (private ownership, many channels), authoritarian practices under military rule (PPO 1963, press advice, censorship under Zia, 2007 emergency bans), social-responsibility rhetoric in codes, development theory in state media, and features of Hallin and Mancini's **polarised pluralist** model (political parallelism, clientelism, state as big advertiser) — useful for the 2026 polarisation question.

## 2.3 Media as agents of power

The critical tradition argues that media serve the interests of dominant groups.

- **Marxist/political economy**: Marx and Engels, *The German Ideology* (1846): "the ideas of the ruling class are in every epoch the ruling ideas." Ownership determines content. Political economy scholars: Herbert Schiller, Graham Murdock and Peter Golding, Robert McChesney.
- **Frankfurt School**: Theodor Adorno and Max Horkheimer, "The Culture Industry: Enlightenment as Mass Deception" in *Dialectic of Enlightenment* (1944): mass culture is standardised, commodified and pacifies audiences (2018 note: "cultural industries").
- **Propaganda model**: **Edward Herman and Noam Chomsky, *Manufacturing Consent* (1988)**. News passes through **five filters**: (1) size, concentrated **ownership** and profit orientation; (2) **advertising** as the main income source; (3) reliance on government, business and "experts" as **sources**; (4) **flak** (negative responses, lawsuits, campaigns) as discipline; (5) **anti-communism** as a control mechanism (later updated to "the war on terror"/fear). (2026 MCQ: "gatekeeping by social media influencers" is NOT a filter.)
- **Power elite** (C. Wright Mills, 1956); **news as social construction** (Gaye Tuchman, *Making News*, 1978); **primary definers** (Stuart Hall et al., *Policing the Crisis*, 1978).
- **Pakistan**: government as the largest single advertiser; cross-media ownership (e.g. major groups own newspapers, TV and digital); "flak" via cable operators shifting channels, lawsuits, PECA cases; reliance on ISPR, ministries and party spokespeople as sources.
- **Criticism**: deterministic; underestimates journalists' agency, audience resistance and digital alternatives.

## 2.4 Spiral of silence (2018, 2019)

- **Elisabeth Noelle-Neumann**, German political scientist; first published 1974 ("The Spiral of Silence: A Theory of Public Opinion", *Journal of Communication*); book *The Spiral of Silence: Public Opinion — Our Social Skin* (1984). Developed after observing a "last-minute swing" in the 1965 West German election.
- **Assumptions**:
  1. Society threatens deviant individuals with **isolation**.
  2. Individuals experience **fear of isolation** continuously.
  3. This fear makes individuals constantly assess the climate of opinion using a **"quasi-statistical sense"**.
  4. The results affect their behaviour in public, especially their willingness to speak openly or stay silent.
- **Process**: if people perceive their view to be in the minority (or losing), they stay silent; the dominant view then seems even more dominant; more minority holders fall silent — a **spiral**.
- **Role of media**: the main source for judging the climate of opinion. Media effects are strong because of **ubiquity** (everywhere), **cumulation** (repetition over time) and **consonance** (similar messages across outlets).
- **Exceptions**: the **hardcore** (avant-garde, dissenters who speak regardless); the **train test** (would you discuss this issue with a stranger on a five-hour train journey?).
- **Diagram (describe)**: a downward-narrowing spiral. At the top, "mass media + interpersonal support for a view" increase its perceived dominance; on one side, people with the dominant view speak out more; on the other, minority-view holders, fearing isolation, speak less; the spiral narrows until the minority view is barely heard.
- **Criticism**: fear of isolation varies by culture and personality; reference groups matter more than society as a whole; hard to measure; ignores the internet, where minorities find supporters (though "online spiral of silence" studies, e.g. Pew 2014 on the Snowden debate, found people self-censor online too).
- **Pakistan**: self-censorship on blasphemy, the military's political role, sectarian and ethnic issues; social-media mobbing and trolling create fear of isolation; journalists avoid "red lines". Also relevant to election "bandwagon" effects of opinion polls.

## 2.5 Uses and gratifications (2017)

- **Origins**: Herta Herzog's study of radio soap opera listeners (1944); Bernard Berelson's "What missing the newspaper means" (1949, during a New York newspaper strike).
- **Elihu Katz, Jay Blumler and Michael Gurevitch**, "Uses and Gratifications Research" (1973–74); *The Uses of Mass Communications* (1974).
- **Core idea**: not "what do media do to people?" but **"what do people do with media?"** Audiences are **active** and goal-directed; they select media to satisfy needs (2026 MCQ).
- **Assumptions** (Katz et al.): (1) audience is active and goal-directed; (2) initiative in linking need gratification to a media choice lies with the audience member; (3) media compete with other sources of need satisfaction; (4) people are aware enough of their needs to report them; (5) value judgements about media content should be suspended.
- **Typologies of needs**:
  - Katz, Gurevitch and Haas (1973): **cognitive** (information), **affective** (emotional), **personal integrative** (credibility, status), **social integrative** (family, friends), **tension release** (escape).
  - McQuail, Blumler and Brown (1972): **diversion** (escape, emotional release), **personal relationships** (companionship, social utility), **personal identity** (self-reference, reality exploration, value reinforcement), **surveillance** (information).
- **Why "audience-centred"**: shifts power from the medium to the user; explains why the same programme gratifies different people differently.
- **Why "positivistic" (2017)**: it relies on empirical, quantitative methods (surveys, self-reports, measurable variables), treats needs as observable facts, and seeks general laws about behaviour — in line with positivist social science. Critics from cultural studies say it ignores meaning and power.
- **Criticism**: overestimates audience rationality and awareness; relies on self-reports; functionalist (assumes media use is always functional); ignores structural constraints (what is available) and content effects; tautological (people use media to gratify needs; needs are inferred from use).
- **Revival**: social media research (why people use Facebook, TikTok: social connection, self-presentation, entertainment, information); "uses and grats 2.0" (Sundar and Limperos, 2013).
- **Pakistan**: youth use TikTok and YouTube for entertainment and identity; families watch dramas for affective and social integrative needs; talk shows for surveillance and partisan reinforcement.

## 2.6 Media hegemony (2018 Gramsci question)

- **Antonio Gramsci** (Italian Marxist, *Prison Notebooks*, 1929–35): ruling classes maintain power not only by force (domination) but by winning **consent** through culture, religion, education and media — **hegemony**. Common sense is shaped to make the existing order seem natural. Intellectuals (traditional and organic) play key roles. Counter-hegemony is possible.
- **Media hegemony**: media reproduce the dominant ideology by presenting elite views as common sense, framing dissent as deviant, and marginalising alternatives (2026 MCQ: "the dominant ideology in media content").
- **Stuart Hall**, "Encoding/Decoding" (1973; Birmingham Centre for Contemporary Cultural Studies): media encode preferred meanings, but audiences decode in three ways: **dominant-hegemonic** (accept), **negotiated** (partly accept), **oppositional** (reject).
- **Todd Gitlin**, *The Whole World Is Watching* (1980): how US media framed the student New Left movement, marginalising it.
- **David Altheide**, "Media Hegemony: A Failure of Perspective" (1984), critiqued the concept for underestimating journalists' independence.
- **Marxist vs Gramscian approach to communication and culture (2018 Q4)**: classical Marxism sees culture as superstructure determined by the economic base (ownership determines content); Gramsci sees culture as a relatively autonomous field of struggle where consent is won and contested. In global communication: Marxist → media/cultural imperialism through ownership (Schiller); Gramscian → global media spread consumerist "common sense", but local audiences negotiate and resist (hybridity).
- **Pakistan**: narratives of national security and "national interest" in mainstream media; framing of protests (e.g. Pashtun Tahaffuz Movement's limited coverage, 2018–); counter-hegemonic spaces on YouTube and X.

## 2.7 Diffusion of innovations (2021, 2022, 2023, 2026) — high priority

- **Everett M. Rogers**, *Diffusion of Innovations* (1962; 5th ed. 2003, on the FPSC list). Built on Bryce Ryan and Neal Gross's study of hybrid seed corn among Iowa farmers (1943) and Gabriel Tarde's "laws of imitation" (1903).
- **Definition**: diffusion is the process by which an **innovation** is **communicated** through certain **channels** over **time** among the members of a **social system** — the four main elements.
- **Innovation**: an idea, practice or object perceived as new by an individual or other unit of adoption.

### Five attributes of innovations that explain the rate of adoption

1. **Relative advantage**: better than what it replaces.
2. **Compatibility**: fits existing values, experiences and needs.
3. **Complexity**: difficulty to understand and use (negative).
4. **Trialability**: can be tried on a limited basis.
5. **Observability**: results are visible to others.

### Innovation-decision process (five stages)

**Knowledge → Persuasion → Decision (adopt/reject) → Implementation → Confirmation.** Mass media matter most at the knowledge stage; interpersonal channels at persuasion and decision.

### Adopter categories (bell curve, by innovativeness)

| Category | Share | Traits |
|---|---|---|
| Innovators | 2.5% | Venturesome, cosmopolitan, can absorb risk and loss |
| Early adopters | 13.5% | Respected, opinion leaders, integrated in local system; key target |
| Early majority | 34% | Deliberate; adopt just before the average |
| Late majority | 34% | Sceptical; adopt from economic necessity or peer pressure |
| Laggards | 16% | Traditional; isolated; point of reference is the past |

Diagram: a **bell curve** split at standard deviations (mean minus 2 SD, minus 1 SD, mean, plus 1 SD), and an **S-shaped cumulative adoption curve** showing slow start, take-off at about 10–20% adoption ("critical mass"), saturation.

### Other concepts

- **Change agents** (extension workers) and **opinion leaders**; **homophily** vs **heterophily**; **re-invention** (users modify the innovation); **consequences** (desirable/undesirable, direct/indirect, anticipated/unanticipated); **knowledge gap** widening.
- Optional, collective and authority innovation decisions.

### Factors in success or failure (2026)

Attributes (above); communication channels; social system norms and opinion leaders; change agents' efforts and credibility; cost and infrastructure; government policy; religious and cultural acceptability; trust in the source.

### Criticisms

1. **Pro-innovation bias**: assumes all innovations are good and should spread.
2. **Individual-blame bias**: blames laggards instead of the system (poverty, lack of access).
3. **Recall problem**: relies on memory of adoption dates.
4. **Equality issue**: diffusion widens socio-economic gaps; early adopters are richer.
5. **Top-down, linear**: part of the dominant paradigm; ignores participation (Rogers himself revised it in 1976 and later editions).
6. Western, agricultural origins.

### Applicability to the Third World / Pakistan (2022, 2023)

| Innovation | Diffusion story |
|---|---|
| Green Revolution seeds and fertiliser (1960s) | Rapid adoption by larger farmers; small farmers lagged — equality issue |
| Family planning | Low compatibility with religious and family norms; ulema engagement and LHWs improved it; contraceptive prevalence still about 34% |
| **Polio vaccine** | Rejection fuelled by rumours (post-2011 fake vaccination campaign in Abbottabad), militancy, distrust; shows compatibility and source credibility problems |
| Mobile phones and mobile money (Easypaisa 2009, JazzCash), Raast (2021) | High relative advantage and observability; fast diffusion |
| Solar panels (2023–26 boom) | Relative advantage after power tariffs rose; trialability; rooftop solar spread fast [verify scale] |
| Ride-hailing, online shopping, freelancing | Urban youth as early adopters |
| COVID-19 vaccines (2021) | Mandates and NCOC communication increased uptake after initial hesitancy |

## 2.8 Powerful effects: hypodermic needle / magic bullet (2016, 2024)

- **Idea**: media messages are "injected" or "fired" directly into passive, isolated audiences, producing uniform, immediate effects.
- **Context**: 1920s–30s mass society theory; WWI propaganda (Lasswell, 1927); Nazi propaganda; behaviourist psychology (stimulus–response).
- **Evidence often cited**: Orson Welles' *The War of the Worlds* radio broadcast (30 Oct 1938) — reports of panic; but Hadley Cantril's study (*The Invasion from Mars*, 1940) found that only some listeners panicked, depending on education and critical ability — undermining the theory.
- **Note**: the theory was never formally proposed by one author; it is a later label for early assumptions.
- **Decline**: the People's Choice study (1940 election) found little direct media effect.

**Relevance in the digital age (2024).**
- *Arguments for a revival*: viral misinformation (WhatsApp lynchings in India, 2017–18), deepfakes, micro-targeted political ads (Cambridge Analytica, 2018), algorithmic amplification, filter bubbles, radicalisation via online propaganda (ISIS), panic buying and rumours during COVID; in Pakistan, fake news during the May 2025 India–Pakistan conflict and online mobs after blasphemy accusations.
- *Arguments against*: audiences are active and fragmented; selective exposure; effects vary by literacy and prior beliefs; people also fact-check; "minimal effects" findings on political ads.
- **Balanced view**: effects can be powerful for some people, some messages and some conditions (low literacy, high emotion, crisis, trust in source) — a conditional or "differential susceptibility" model (Valkenburg and Peter, 2013).

## 2.9 Moderate effects: two-step and multi-step flow (2016, 2024)

- **Paul Lazarsfeld, Bernard Berelson and Hazel Gaudet, *The People's Choice* (1944)**: Erie County, Ohio, 1940 US presidential election panel study. Found media had less direct influence than expected; ideas flow from media to **opinion leaders** and from them to less active sections of the population. (2025 MCQ: "voters are motivated less by mass media than by people" → two-step flow.)
- **Elihu Katz and Paul Lazarsfeld, *Personal Influence* (1955)**: Decatur, Illinois study of women's decisions on marketing, fashion, public affairs and films; confirmed opinion leaders in each domain.
- **Opinion leaders**: more exposed to media, more cosmopolitan, similar in status to followers (homophily), influential in specific areas.
- **Diagram**: Mass media → opinion leaders (○) → individuals in social contact with them (●).
- **Criticism**: oversimplified; many people get news directly; opinion leaders also get information from other people (**multi-step flow**); influence flows in many directions; the leader–follower split is not fixed; TV and the internet changed the flow.
- **Multi-step flow**: information passes through several relays and networks; in the social-media era, influencers, WhatsApp group admins and YouTubers act as opinion leaders; **"one-step flow"** argued by some for algorithmic media (Bennett and Manheim, 2006).
- **Pakistan**: rural voting shaped by biradari elders, pirs, landlords; religious leaders mediate health messages (polio, family planning); WhatsApp family groups.

## 2.10 Powerful media revisited: McLuhan's media determinism (2018, 2019)

- **Marshall McLuhan** (Canadian), *The Gutenberg Galaxy* (1962) and *Understanding Media: The Extensions of Man* (1964); *The Medium Is the Massage* (1967, with Quentin Fiore).
- **Technological (media) determinism**: the dominant medium of an era shapes how people think, feel and organise society, more than the content.
- **"The medium is the message"**: the social effects of a medium come from its form (how it extends our senses), not its content. E.g. electric light has no content but transforms social life.
- **Media as extensions of man**: the wheel extends the foot, the book the eye, clothing the skin, electronic media the central nervous system.
- **Hot and cool media**: *hot* media are high-definition, extend one sense, require low participation (radio, film, print, photograph); *cool* media are low-definition, require high participation to fill in (telephone, TV, cartoon, speech).
- **Four epochs**: (1) **tribal/oral** (ear, acoustic space, community); (2) **literate/manuscript** (phonetic alphabet; eye, linear thought); (3) **print/Gutenberg** (1450s; individualism, nationalism, standardisation); (4) **electronic** (telegraph to TV; retribalisation, the **global village**).
- **Global village**: electronic media shrink the world, creating instant, shared experience and interdependence.
- **Roots**: **Harold Innis**, *Empire and Communication* (1950), *The Bias of Communication* (1951): **time-biased** media (stone, clay; durable, favour tradition and religion) vs **space-biased** media (paper, papyrus; portable, favour empires and administration).
- **Why "powerful media revisited"**: after the limited-effects era, McLuhan restored the idea of strong media influence, but at the level of civilisation and perception, not individual attitudes.
- **Criticism**: deterministic and unfalsifiable; ignores content, ownership, politics and human agency (Raymond Williams, *Television: Technology and Cultural Form*, 1974); aphoristic, not scientific. **Revival**: predicted the internet's global village, social media participation and "retribalisation" into online tribes; Neil Postman's *Amusing Ourselves to Death* (1985) and Joshua Meyrowitz's *No Sense of Place* (1985) extend him.
- **Pakistan**: TV's shift of politics into talk-show performance; smartphones and TikTok reshaping attention and identity; the "global village" is real for youth but also divided by digital divides and censorship.
