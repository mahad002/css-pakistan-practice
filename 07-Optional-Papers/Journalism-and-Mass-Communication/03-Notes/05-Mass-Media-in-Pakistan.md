## 5.1 Historical and chronological review

### (a) Pre-partition: the Muslim press and the Pakistan Movement (2021)

| Paper | Founder / editor | Year | Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Aligarh Institute Gazette* | Sir Syed Ahmad Khan | 1866 | Modern education, loyalty to British, Muslim revival |
| *Tahzib-ul-Akhlaq* | Sir Syed | 1870 | Social and religious reform |
| *Paisa Akhbar* (Lahore) | Munshi Mehboob Alam | 1887 | Popular Urdu daily [verify year] |
| *Zamindar* (Lahore) | Founded by Maulvi Sirajuddin (1903); edited by **Maulana Zafar Ali Khan** | 1903/1909 | Fiery anti-colonial journalism; frequently penalised |
| *Comrade* (English, Calcutta then Delhi) and *Hamdard* (Urdu) | **Maulana Muhammad Ali Jauhar** | 1911; 1913 | Muslim political awakening; Khilafat movement |
| *Al-Hilal*, *Al-Balagh* | Maulana Abul Kalam Azad | 1912, 1915 | Pan-Islamic, anti-British (Azad later sided with Congress) |

| *Jang* (Delhi) | Mir Khalil-ur-Rahman | 1939 (moved to Karachi 1947) | Popular Urdu daily supporting the League |
| ***Nawa-i-Waqt*** (Lahore) | **Hameed Nizami** | 23 March 1940 (fortnightly; daily 1944) | Founded on the day of the Lahore Resolution; strong Pakistan Movement voice |
| ***Dawn*** (Delhi) | Founded by **Quaid-i-Azam Muhammad Ali Jinnah**; editor Pothan Joseph, then **Altaf Husain** | Weekly 26 Oct 1941; daily 1942 | Official voice of the Muslim League in English; moved to Karachi in 1947 |
| *Morning News* (Calcutta), *Star of India*, *Manshoor* (Delhi), *Eastern Times* (Lahore) | Various | 1930s–40s | League-supporting papers |
| *Pakistan Times* (Lahore) | Mian Iftikharuddin; editor Faiz Ahmed Faiz | Feb 1947 | Progressive English daily |
| Orient Press of India | Muslim-owned news agency | 1946 [verify] | Countered Hindu-dominated agencies (API) |

**Role of the Muslim press in the creation of Pakistan.**
1. **Political awakening and identity**: articulated the two-nation idea and Muslim grievances.
2. **Countering hostile propaganda**: the dominant English and Hindu-owned press (e.g. *The Statesman*, *Hindustan Times*) opposed the League; the Quaid urged Muslims to build their own press. After 1940 a fund was raised to support Muslim newspapers [verify details].
3. **Spreading the Lahore Resolution (1940)** and the League's programme; mobilising for the 1945–46 elections.
4. **Defending the Quaid and League leadership**.
5. **Training journalists** who later built Pakistan's media.
6. **Costs**: securities forfeited, papers banned, editors jailed under colonial press laws (Zafar Ali Khan, Muhammad Ali Jauhar).
Quaid's view: he regarded the press as essential to the movement and later told journalists that freedom must be exercised with responsibility (speech to journalists, 1948 [verify exact words; paraphrase]).

### (b) 1947–1958: early years

- At independence: a handful of newspapers moved to Pakistan (Dawn, Jang, Nawa-i-Waqt, Pakistan Times, Morning News, Imroze). **Radio Pakistan** began on **14 August 1947** with stations at Lahore, Peshawar and Dacca. **Associated Press of Pakistan (APP)** founded in 1947 (nationalised in 1961).
- Colonial laws continued: Security of Pakistan Act 1952; Public Safety Acts.
- Rawalpindi Conspiracy Case (1951) — Faiz Ahmed Faiz arrested.
- **PFUJ** (Pakistan Federal Union of Journalists) founded in 1950.
- Pakistan Press Commission (1954) — recommendations on press freedom and journalists' conditions [verify year].

### (c) 1958–1971: Ayub Khan's control

- **Progressive Papers Limited** (*Pakistan Times*, *Imroze*, *Lail-o-Nahar*) taken over in April 1959.
- **Press and Publications Ordinance (PPO) 1963** (preceded by the West Pakistan PPO 1960): declarations, security deposits, power to seize presses and ban papers — the most criticised press law.
- **National Press Trust (NPT)** formed 1964 to run government-controlled papers.
- **PTV** began on **26 November 1964** in Lahore (pilot project with Japan's NEC), then Dhaka (1964), Karachi and Rawalpindi–Islamabad (1967); **Pakistan Television Corporation** formed 1967.
- Press used to promote the "Decade of Development" (1968).

### (d) 1971–1977: Bhutto

- Promised freedom but used the PPO, closed papers (*Outlook*, *Punjab Punch*), arrested editors (Altaf Gauhar, editor of *Dawn*); PTV and Radio as government mouthpieces; *Musawat* as the PPP's paper.

### (e) 1977–1988: Zia ul Haq — censorship

- Martial law, pre-censorship, bans on newspapers (*Musawat* banned 1978).
- **Press freedom movement of 1978** led by the PFUJ (Minhaj Barna, Nisar Osmani): hundreds of journalists and supporters courted arrest; on **13 May 1978** three journalists (Nasir Zaidi, Iqbal Jafri, Khawar Naeem Hashmi) were publicly flogged in Lahore (a fourth, Masoodullah Khan, was spared on medical grounds). The PFUJ marks 13 May every year.
- Islamisation of media content; PTV dress codes for women presenters.
- *Viewpoint*, *The Muslim*, *Herald*, *Frontier Post* and others published within limits.

### (f) 1988–1999: return of democracy

- **Registration of Printing Presses and Publications Ordinance (RPPPO) 1988** replaced the PPO; declarations made easier; it was re-promulgated repeatedly as an ordinance and lapsed in 1997 [verify].
- Government ads as a lever; disputes with the Jang Group (1998–99 newsprint and tax dispute under Nawaz Sharif).
- **STN/NTM** (Shalimar Television Network, 1990) — first semi-private TV content. **FM 100** began (mid-1990s) as the first private FM radio [verify year].
- Satellite dishes spread foreign channels.

### (g) 1999–2008: Musharraf — liberalisation and crackdown

- **PEMRA Ordinance 2002** (March 2002) opened broadcasting to private players. Other 2002 laws: **Press Council of Pakistan Ordinance**, **Defamation Ordinance**, **Freedom of Information Ordinance**, and the **Press, Newspapers, News Agencies and Books Registration Ordinance (PNNABRO)**.
- Private channels: Indus Vision (2000), ARY Digital and **Geo TV** (2002, initially broadcast from Dubai), Aaj (2005), Express News, Dunya News (2008), Samaa, and dozens more.
- **2007 emergency (3 Nov)**: Geo and ARY taken off air; PEMRA amendments restricted coverage; media's role in the **lawyers' movement** to restore Chief Justice Iftikhar Chaudhry.
- Journalists killed: Daniel Pearl (2002), Hayatullah Khan (2006).

### (h) 2008–2018

- 24/7 news boom; talk shows dominate prime time; media as political actor.
- Journalists killed: Wali Khan Babar (2011), **Saleem Shahzad** (2011, commission formed), others; Pakistan ranked among the world's most dangerous countries for journalists.
- YouTube banned (2012–2016).
- **Hamid Mir attack** (April 2014) → Geo's 15-day suspension by PEMRA and cable blackouts.
- **Supreme Court Media Commission** (2013; Justice (retd) Nasir Aslam Zahid and Javed Jabbar) — report on media reform (on FPSC list).
- **PEMRA Code of Conduct 2015**.
- **PECA 2016**; **"Dawn Leaks"** (Oct 2016 story by Cyril Almeida) and pressure on *Dawn*'s distribution (2018).
- **Right of Access to Information Act 2017**.

### (i) 2018–2026

- Financial crisis in media: cuts to government ads, layoffs, salary delays (2018–20).
- Proposed **Pakistan Media Development Authority** (2021) to merge all regulators — dropped after protests.
- **Mir Shakil-ur-Rahman** (Jang/Geo owner) arrested by NAB (March 2020), released Nov 2020.
- **Protection of Journalists and Media Professionals Act 2021** (federal) and **Sindh Protection of Journalists and Other Media Practitioners Act 2021**.
- **Arshad Sharif** killed in Kenya (Oct 2022).
- **PEMRA (Amendment) Act 2023** (assented 15 Aug 2023): definitions of mis- and disinformation, salary protection for media workers, more members.
- Bans on airing Imran Khan's speeches and images (2023) [as reported].
- **X blocked 17 Feb 2024 – 7 May 2025**.
- **Punjab Defamation Act 2024** (May 2024): tribunals notified in Sept 2025; petitions still pending in the Lahore High Court (April 2026).
- **PECA (Amendment) Act 2025** (January 2025): "fake news" offence (s. 26A), Social Media Protection and Regulatory Authority, tribunal, National Cyber Crime Investigation Agency; petitions by PFUJ pending in the Islamabad High Court (2026).
- **Federal Commission for the Protection of Journalists and Media Professionals** constituted in November 2025 (chair: Kamal Uddin Tipu; first meeting 25 Nov 2025).
- PEMRA without a permanent chair from Oct 2025 (Feb 2026 report); **13 Oct 2026 PEMRA directive** barring live coverage of attacks and "unpleasant incidents"; **Aug 2026** information ministry rules requiring foreign journalists to register before reporting outside major cities (CPJ).
- RSF World Press Freedom Index: Pakistan **158th of 180 (2025)**, **153rd (2026)**, "very serious" category.

## 5.2 The system of journalism and the media system

**Media system components**: owners (family-based conglomerates), journalists and unions, regulators (PEMRA, PCP, PTA, MoIB, PID), advertisers (government is the biggest single advertiser; FMCG and telecom companies), distributors (cable operators, hawkers, platforms), audiences, ratings agencies.

**Media landscape (as of 31 March 2026, Pakistan Economic Survey 2025–26):** 142 satellite TV licences; 240 FM radio licences (172 commercial, 68 non-commercial); 2,951 cable TV licences; 28 landing rights permissions.

**Major groups (cross-media ownership)** — 2022 note on **media conglomerates**:
- **Jang Group** (Jang, The News, Geo TV channels).
- **Dawn Group / Herald Publications** (Dawn, Dawn News, Dawn.com).
- **Nawa-i-Waqt Group** (Nawa-i-Waqt, The Nation, Waqt TV — closed 2019 [verify]).
- **Express Media Group** (Lakson) (Express, The Express Tribune, Express News).
- **Dunya Group** (Dunya News, Roznama Dunya).
- **ARY Group**, **Samaa**, **Hum Network** (entertainment), **BOL**.
- State: **PTV** (several channels), **Radio Pakistan** (PBC), **APP**.

**Media conglomerates — pros and cons**: economies of scale, investment in quality; but concentration of power, homogenised content, owners' business interests influence editorial lines, political leverage, weaker plurality. Pakistan has no effective cross-media ownership limits; PEMRA Ordinance s. 23 restricts undue concentration [verify scope].

**System of journalism**: newsroom hierarchy (editor, news editor, sub-editors, reporters, bureau chiefs, stringers); in TV: director news, controller news, producers, anchors, DSNG teams; weak editorial independence; anchors as stars; district stringers unpaid or poorly paid; journalism education (mass communication departments since University of the Punjab's department in 1941 [verify]) but weak links to industry.

## 5.3 Employer–employee relations

- **Newspaper Employees (Conditions of Service) Act 1973 (NECOSA)**: wage boards for newspaper staff; **Implementation Tribunal for Newspaper Employees (ITNE)**. The **8th Wage Board award** was announced in 2001 and its implementation was ordered by the Supreme Court [verify]; later boards were delayed.
- **TV and digital workers** are not covered by NECOSA; most work on contracts without job security.
- **Problems**: salary delays for months; mass layoffs (2018–2020); no pensions; unsafe conditions for field reporters; no insurance in conflict zones; anti-union practices; pressure to bring advertising; "lifafa journalism" (bribes) where pay is low.
- **Remedies**: PEMRA (Amendment) Act 2023 lets workers complain to PEMRA's Council of Complaints on unpaid salaries and minimum wages, and links government ads to timely payment (within two months); Protection of Journalists Act 2021 requires employers to provide insurance and training; unions (PFUJ — split into factions; APNEC for newspaper employees).
- **Owners' bodies**: **APNS** (All Pakistan Newspapers Society, 1953), **CPNE** (Council of Pakistan Newspaper Editors, 1957), **PBA** (Pakistan Broadcasters Association, 2006 [verify]).

## 5.4 Government–press relations (2018, 2023, 2025)

### Why relations have been adversarial (2018)

1. **Authoritarian legacy**: colonial press laws inherited; four military regimes used censorship.
2. **Control tools**: licensing and declarations; PPO 1963; PEMRA bans and suspensions; government advertisements as carrot and stick; newsprint quotas (historically); cable operators pushed to block channels; tax and legal cases against owners; PECA prosecutions.
3. **Security state**: "national security" and "national interest" red lines; intelligence pressure; abductions.
4. **Political polarisation**: every government sees critical media as partisan.
5. **Media's own weaknesses**: sensationalism, partisan owners, unverified reporting, "media trials" — governments use these to justify control.
6. **Economic dependence**: government is the biggest advertiser; ads distributed through PID with discretion.

### Phases of the relationship

| Phase | Character |
|---|---|
| 1947–58 | Cautious cooperation, colonial laws |
| Ayub | Takeover and control (NPT, PPO) |
| Bhutto | Personalised confrontation |
| Zia | Repression and censorship |
| 1988–99 | Freedom with harassment |
| Musharraf | Liberalisation then crackdown (2007) |
| 2008–2018 | Media power peaks; clashes with governments and the military |
| 2018–2026 | "Managed" media environment; financial squeeze; digital controls (PECA 2025, X ban, PEMRA directives) |

### How governments can use media positively (2025)

- Public information campaigns (COVID NCOC, polio, flood alerts, Ehsaas/BISP registration, tax filing).
- Press briefings and transparent spokespersons; RTI compliance.
- Social media for direct communication (ministries' accounts, Pakistan Citizen Portal).
- Fair, transparent advertising policy (based on circulation/viewership audits).
- Dialogue and consultation with media bodies before laws.
- *Current examples*: NDMA flood warnings (2022, 2025); NCOC's daily COVID updates (2020–21); SBP's Raast campaign; ECP's voter education; ISPR's communication in the May 2025 conflict.

## 5.5 Press in Pakistan: the newspaper industry, from mission to market

- **Mission era (1900s–1950s)**: newspapers were political instruments of the Pakistan Movement and ideology; owners were activists (Jinnah, Hameed Nizami, Zafar Ali Khan).
- **Control era (1958–1988)**: state ownership (NPT), licensing, censorship; papers survived through government ads.
- **Market era (1988–)**: commercialisation; advertising-driven; conglomerates; supplements and colour printing; circulation battles; Urdu papers mass circulation, English papers elite and influential.
- **Digital disruption (2010s–)**: falling print circulation and ads, shift to websites, e-papers and social media; Google and Meta capture ad revenue; closures (e.g. some editions and magazines like *Herald* closed 2019 [verify]).
- **Consequences**: profit motive vs public interest; advertorials; editorial influence by advertisers; decline of investigative journalism; but also professionalisation and independence from the state for some outlets.

## 5.6 Electronic media: from total dependence to enormous power

- **Total dependence (1947–2002)**: PTV and Radio Pakistan were state monopolies under the Ministry of Information; news was government-controlled ("Khabarnama" at 9 pm).
- **Enormous power (2002–)**: private channels became kingmakers — lawyers' movement, 2014 PTI dharna coverage, judicial activism; talk shows set the national agenda; anchors became celebrities and political players; channels could make or break issues.
- **Counter-trend**: since 2018, financial pressure, PEMRA actions and digital competition have reduced TV's power; audiences (especially youth) moved to YouTube, TikTok and X; journalists moved to YouTube after being taken off air.

## 5.7 The new 24/7 television: uses and abuses (2016, 2019, 2020, 2026)

| Uses (pro-social) | Abuses (anti-social) |
|---|---|
| Instant information in crises (earthquake 2005, floods, attacks) | Sensationalism, "breaking news" without verification |
| Accountability and watchdog (lawyers' movement, corruption) | Live coverage of terror attacks aiding terrorists (e.g. 2008 Mumbai coverage debate; GHQ 2009; APS 2014) |
| Voice to citizens; public grievances programmes | Media trials and prejudicing courts |
| Entertainment and cultural production (dramas) | Hate speech, sectarianism, blasphemy allegations aired (e.g. accusations leading to violence) |
| Political debate and civic education | Privacy violations, showing victims' faces, raids on homes on camera |
| Employment and industry growth | Commercialism: ratings over public interest; cheap talk shows; advertising overload |
| Disaster fundraising | Polarisation and partisan anchors; fake news |

### Commercialism and news (2019)

Causes: ratings measured by a single private company with a small, urban sample of meters [verify current system]; advertisers chase ratings; cheap studio formats; owners' business diversification. Effects on **selection**: crime, politics and scandal over development, health, education and the provinces; Islamabad–Karachi–Lahore bias. Effects on **presentation**: dramatic music, re-enactments, split screens, shouting matches, ticker overload, celebrity anchors. Remedies: independent, larger ratings system; public service broadcasting reform (PTV); PEMRA enforcement of code on sensationalism; editorial ombudsmen; licence conditions for news content; audience media literacy.

### Political polarisation among anchors (2026)

Reasons:
1. **Owner–party alignment** and business interests; Hallin and Mancini's "political parallelism".
2. **Ratings logic**: conflict and outrage attract viewers; anchors build personal brands.
3. **Political crisis since 2017–2022** (Panama case, 2018 election, 2022 no-confidence vote, 9 May 2023 events, 2024 election disputes) split audiences into camps.
4. **State pressure**: some anchors taken off air and moved to YouTube, others seen as aligned with the establishment — audiences read every anchor as partisan.
5. **YouTube economics**: monetisation rewards partisan content; anchors' channels compete.
6. **Weak editorial control** and lack of fact-checking on talk shows; guests repeat unverified claims.
7. **Social media amplification** and trolling.
Consequences: loss of credibility of talk shows; audience retreat to echo chambers; difficulty in building national consensus; journalists harassed by opposing camps. Examples (use carefully, neutrally): debates over coverage of the PTI government's removal (2022) and the 2024 election; anchors leaving the country or facing PECA cases. Remedies: editorial standards; disclosure of conflicts of interest; mixing reporting with opinion clearly labelled; PEMRA code enforcement on "impartiality"; fact-check units; training.

### Credibility and the future of journalism (2020)

If big media groups and star anchors are unaffected by public criticism, it suggests concentration and audience loyalty insulate them. Future risks: further decline in trust (global Reuters Institute Digital News Report surveys show low trust in news in many countries; Pakistan data limited [verify]); migration of audiences to unverified social media; journalism seen as a branch of politics. Remedies: credibility as business strategy; independent ombudsmen; transparency; investigative units; digital-native outlets with reader funding.

## 5.8 The new radio: potential for change and present performance

- **History**: Radio Pakistan (14 Aug 1947); expanded to all provinces; Azad Kashmir Radio; external services.
- **Private FM**: FM 100 first private FM; FM 101 (Radio Pakistan's FM network, 1998); PEMRA licences since 2002; **240 FM licences** by March 2026 (172 commercial, 68 non-commercial — including university campus radios).
- **Potential**: cheap, portable, local-language, reaches illiterate and rural audiences; useful for agriculture, health, education (AIOU and Taleem Ghar radio, COVID 2020), disaster warnings, community voice; call-in shows give citizens a platform.
- **Present performance**: mostly music and entertainment in cities; news restricted (private FM stations are generally not allowed to air their own news bulletins and must relay Radio Pakistan news [verify current rule]); commercial pressures; little community radio in law; abuse by militants (e.g. **Mullah Fazlullah's illegal FM radio in Swat**, 2006–09, spread extremist messages — "Mullah Radio"); low investment in public radio.
- **Reforms**: allow community radio licences; permit FM news with standards; support local-language programming; integrate radio into disaster and education strategies.

## 5.9 The question of freedom and responsibility (2024, 2025, 2026)

### State of freedom of expression (2024–2026)

| Indicator | Detail |
|---|---|
| RSF World Press Freedom Index | 158th/180 (2025) → 153rd/180 (2026), "very serious" |
| Legal pressures | PECA 2016 and 2025 amendments (s. 26A fake news, up to 3 years and Rs2 million fine; SMPRA); PEMRA directives; Punjab Defamation Act 2024; Official Secrets (Amendment) Act 2023; sedition (PPC s. 124A — struck down by the Lahore High Court in March 2023 [verify appeal status]) |
| Digital controls | X blocked Feb 2024–May 2025; internet shutdowns during protests; slowdowns; VPN registration rules (2024 [verify]) |
| Physical safety | Killings, abductions and assaults of journalists; impunity in most cases (Freedom Network and CPJ reports) |
| Economic pressure | Ads withheld; salary delays; layoffs |
| Self-censorship | "Red lines" on military, judiciary, religion, ethnic movements |
| Institutions | Journalists' protection commission (federal, Nov 2025; Sindh); RTI Act 2017 and Pakistan Information Commission |

### Threats and challenges faced by journalists (2025)

Violence and killings (especially in KP, Balochistan and Sindh districts); abductions and enforced disappearances; legal cases (PECA, sedition, defamation); online harassment and smear campaigns (women journalists face sexualised abuse — a 2020 joint statement by women journalists highlighted this); job insecurity; lack of insurance and safety training; pressure from political parties, militants and sectarian groups; censorship by owners; surveillance.

### Social impacts (2024)

Weak accountability → corruption and misgovernance; misinformation fills the vacuum; polarisation; loss of investor confidence; brain drain of journalists; weakened democracy; global image damage.

### Measures

1. Amend PECA to remove vague "fake news" offences; independent oversight of SMPRA; judicial review.
2. Make PEMRA independent (appointment through a parliamentary committee is a start — insist on transparency and security of tenure); proportional, transparent sanctions with appeal.
3. Fully operationalise the journalists' protection commissions; special prosecutors for crimes against journalists.
4. Transparent government advertising policy based on audited reach.
5. Strengthen self-regulation (PCP; broadcasters' complaints body), editorial independence, ombudsmen.
6. Decriminalise defamation; use civil remedies.
7. Media literacy and fact-checking.
8. Implement RTI laws; proactive disclosure.
