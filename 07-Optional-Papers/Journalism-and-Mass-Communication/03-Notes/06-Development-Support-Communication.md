# 6. Development Support Communication (DSC)

Syllabus section VI. **Asked every year 2017–2025**: define DSC and its use for Pakistan's social problems (2017), DC vs DSC in a cosmopolitan society (2018), dominant paradigm note (2019), development journalism vs DSC (2020), glocalisation note (2021), history of DSC and ICTs; development communication and nation-building (2022), DSC for national development (2023), DSC methods and difficulties (2024), DSC for education and literacy (2025). This is the single safest question in the paper.

## 6.1 Key definitions

| Term | Definition | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Development** | A widely participatory process of social change intended to bring about social and material advancement (including equality, freedom and other valued qualities) for the majority through their greater control over their environment | Everett Rogers (1976) |
| **Development communication (DC)** | "The art and science of human communication applied to the speedy transformation of a country and the mass of its people from poverty to a dynamic state of economic growth that makes possible greater social equality and the larger fulfilment of the human potential" | **Nora Quebral** (University of the Philippines Los Baños, 1971/1972) — she coined the term |
| **Development support communication (DSC)** | The planned and systematic use of communication (interpersonal and mass media) to support specific development programmes and projects — informing, motivating and training the people the projects serve, and linking planners, technical experts and communities | **Erskine Childers** and Mallica Vajrathon, UNDP (late 1960s); UNDP's Development Support Communication Service (Bangkok, 1967 [verify]) |
| **Development journalism** | Reporting that critically examines and evaluates development projects, their relevance and implementation, and the gap between plan and reality | Press Foundation of Asia, Philippines (late 1960s; Alan Chalkley, Juan Mercado) |
| **Communication for development (C4D)** | UN term (from 2006 World Congress on Communication for Development, Rome): a social process based on dialogue, using a broad range of tools and methods | UN / FAO / UNICEF |
| **Social and behaviour change communication (SBCC / BCC)** | Strategic, research-based use of communication to change behaviours and social norms (e.g. health) | USAID, UNICEF |

### DC vs DSC vs development journalism (2018, 2020)

| | Development communication | Development support communication | Development journalism |
|---|---|---|---|
| Scope | Broad: the whole role of communication in social change | Narrow: communication component of specific projects | Journalism about development |
| Agent | Governments, NGOs, media, communities | Project planners, extension workers, communication specialists | Journalists |
| Aim | Transform society | Make a project succeed (adoption of practices) | Inform and hold development to account |
| Tone | Strategic, educational | Persuasive, instructional, participatory | Critical, investigative, but constructive |
| Example | National literacy strategy | Polio campaign, LHW counselling, farm extension programme | A report on whether a dam project helped local people |
| Risk | Top-down | Propaganda if one-way | Co-opted as "government-guided" journalism |

### Should DSC be used in an increasingly cosmopolitan society? (2018)

- *Yes*: even urban, connected societies need behaviour change (road safety, smoking, mental health, climate, digital safety, vaccination); DSC tools (research, segmentation, social marketing, social media campaigns) work in cosmopolitan contexts; Pakistan remains about 60% rural (Census 2023: urban about 39% [verify]) with large literacy gaps.
- *But*: methods must change — participatory, two-way, digitally targeted, respectful of diversity and individual choice; avoid paternalism.
- Conclusion: support DSC with modernised, participatory, evidence-based tools.

## 6.2 Theories of DSC and the developing world

1. **Modernisation and the dominant paradigm** (6.3): Lerner, Schramm, Rogers; mass media as "magic multipliers".
2. **Diffusion of innovations** (see notes 02): adoption through mass media and change agents.
3. **Dependency theory critique**: Latin American scholars (Luis Ramiro Beltrán, 1976; Juan Díaz Bordenave) — imported communication models served foreign interests and local elites.
4. **Participatory communication** (6.4): Paulo Freire's dialogue and conscientisation.
5. **Multiplicity paradigm** (Jan Servaes, *Communication for Development: One World, Multiple Cultures*, 1999): no universal path; development must be locally defined, participatory and culturally sensitive.
6. **Social marketing** (6.7).
7. **Entertainment-education** (Sabido; Singhal and Rogers): drama to model behaviour.
8. **Empowerment and communication for social change** (Rockefeller Foundation, 1997): people as agents, horizontal communication, community dialogue.

## 6.3 The dominant paradigm of development (2019 note)

**Historical background.** After WWII, Truman's Point Four programme (1949) and decolonisation; development defined as economic growth and industrialisation following the Western path.

**Key features (Rogers, 1976, "Communication and Development: The Passing of the Dominant Paradigm"):**
1. **Economic growth** (GNP) through industrialisation as the measure of development.
2. **Capital-intensive technology**, often imported.
3. **Centralised planning** by economists and experts.
4. **Causes of underdevelopment located within** the developing nations (traditional values, lack of modern attitudes).
5. Communication: **top-down, one-way, mass media** to persuade people to adopt modern practices (Lerner's empathy; Schramm's "magic multiplier"; Rogers' diffusion).

**Examples**: Green Revolution campaigns; family planning drives in the 1960s (Pakistan's 1965 family planning programme); rural radio forums; India's SITE satellite TV experiment (1975–76).

**Critique (why it "passed")**:
- Growth did not trickle down; inequality rose (Pakistan's "22 families" debate, 1968, Mahbub ul Haq).
- Ignored external causes (dependency theory).
- Top-down communication ignored local knowledge; "blamed the victim".
- Environmental damage; capital-intensive projects unsuited to labour-rich economies.
- Diffusion widened gaps between rich and poor farmers.
- Oil crisis (1973) and ecological concerns; China's alternative path; basic needs approach (ILO 1976).

## 6.4 The alternative paradigm of development

**Features**:
1. **Equity** in distribution; basic needs (food, health, shelter, education).
2. **Participation**: people define and lead their own development.
3. **Self-reliance and local resources**; appropriate technology.
4. **Integration of traditional and modern** knowledge.
5. **Sustainability** (Brundtland Report *Our Common Future*, 1987).
6. **Human development** (Mahbub ul Haq, UNDP *Human Development Report* from 1990; Amartya Sen's capabilities, *Development as Freedom*, 1999).
7. Communication: **two-way, horizontal, dialogic, participatory**, small media (community radio, video, theatre, folk media), interpersonal networks.

**Thinkers**: **Paulo Freire**, *Pedagogy of the Oppressed* (1968, English 1970): "banking education" vs dialogic, problem-posing education; **conscientização** (critical consciousness). Dag Hammarskjöld Foundation, *What Now: Another Development* (1975). E.F. Schumacher (6.5). Robert Chambers, *Rural Development: Putting the Last First* (1983), participatory rural appraisal.

**Dominant vs alternative paradigm (table to draw)**

| | Dominant | Alternative |
|---|---|---|
| Goal | Economic growth | Human development, equity, sustainability |
| Direction | Top-down | Bottom-up |
| Communication | One-way, mass media, persuasion | Two-way, dialogue, participation |
| View of people | Passive recipients, traditional obstacles | Active agents with knowledge |
| Technology | Capital-intensive, imported | Appropriate, local |
| Cause of underdevelopment | Internal (culture) | Structural, external and internal |
| Key thinkers | Lerner, Schramm, Rostow, early Rogers | Freire, Schumacher, Sen, ul Haq, Servaes |

## 6.5 "Small is beautiful": community development as a snowball effect

- **E.F. Schumacher**, *Small Is Beautiful: Economics as if People Mattered* (1973): human-scale, decentralised economics; **intermediate (appropriate) technology**; work with dignity; respect for nature; "Buddhist economics".
- **Snowball effect**: a small, successful community project, owned by people, builds trust and skills; neighbours copy it; it expands step by step at low cost — unlike large top-down projects.
- **Pakistani models**:
  - **Comilla model** (1959, East Pakistan, Akhtar Hameed Khan, Pakistan Academy for Rural Development): cooperatives, training, rural works.
  - **Orangi Pilot Project** (Karachi, 1980, Akhtar Hameed Khan): residents of Orangi built their own lane sewers with technical help; cost a fraction of government schemes; replicated in hundreds of settlements — the classic snowball. **Arif Hasan**'s *Participatory Development* (2010, on the FPSC list) documents it.
  - **Aga Khan Rural Support Programme** (AKRSP, 1982, Gilgit-Baltistan, Shoaib Sultan Khan): village organisations, savings, infrastructure → the national **Rural Support Programmes Network**.
  - Comparisons: Grameen Bank (Bangladesh, Muhammad Yunus); BRAC.
- **Communication role**: dialogue meetings, demonstration, peer learning, local media, documentation that spreads success.

## 6.6 Globalisation vs localisation; glocalisation (2021 note)

- **Globalisation**: integration and standardisation across borders (global brands, media, policies).
- **Localisation**: adapting to and strengthening local contexts, cultures and economies; resistance to homogenisation; "think globally, act locally".
- **Glocalisation**: a blend — global products or ideas adapted to local conditions. Term from the Japanese business concept *dochakuka* (adapting farming techniques to local conditions), popularised by sociologist **Roland Robertson** (1992, 1995).
- **Examples**: McDonald's McArabia or local burgers; KFC and halal; Coke Studio Pakistan (global brand + local folk, Sufi and regional music, from 2008); Netflix producing local-language shows; global health campaigns delivered in local languages through local religious leaders; Sesame Street's Pakistani version (*Sim Sim Hamara*, 2011 [verify]).
- **In DSC**: global development goals (SDGs) and donor programmes adapted to local culture, language and channels; glocal campaigns work better than imported ones (e.g. polio messages from ulema and local celebrities).

## 6.7 Social marketing: infusing new ideas into a developing population

- **Philip Kotler and Gerald Zaltman**, "Social Marketing: An Approach to Planned Social Change" (*Journal of Marketing*, 1971): using marketing principles to promote socially beneficial ideas and behaviours.
- **The 4 Ps** adapted: **Product** (the behaviour or service, e.g. using condoms, ORS), **Price** (monetary and non-monetary costs: time, effort, social disapproval), **Place** (access points), **Promotion** (messages and channels). Additional Ps: **Publics, Partnership, Policy, Purse strings** (Weinreich).
- **Process**: research and audience segmentation → objectives → message design (pretesting) → channels mix → implementation → monitoring and evaluation.
- **Behaviour change models used**: Health Belief Model, Theory of Planned Behaviour, Stages of Change (Prochaska), diffusion.
- **Pakistan examples**: **Greenstar Social Marketing** (1991; family planning products and clinics); Population Welfare campaigns; iodised salt (UNICEF); ORS and hand-washing; polio and measles campaigns; anti-dengue (2011); tobacco control; road safety; COVID vaccination.
- **Critiques**: can be manipulative; treats people as consumers; individual behaviour focus ignores structural causes (poverty); dependent on donors.

## 6.8 How ICTs transform DSC (2022)

| Old DSC | ICT-enabled DSC |
|---|---|
| Radio, TV, posters, extension workers | Mobile phones, SMS, WhatsApp, social media, apps, IVR (voice) for low-literacy users |
| One-way | Two-way, interactive, real-time feedback |
| Mass audience | Targeted and personalised (data, segmentation) |
| Slow evaluation | Real-time monitoring (dashboards, geo-tagging) |
| Expensive | Low cost per contact, scalable |
| Examples: Radio Pakistan farm programmes | Examples: **Taleem Ghar** (PTV Teleschool, launched April 2020 during COVID) and radio school; **Sehat Kahani** telemedicine; **BISP and Ehsaas** registration by SMS (8171 [verify]); polio microplanning with GIS and mobile reporting; **Kissan** apps and helplines; NDMA disaster alert app; e-Khidmat centres; DigiSkills freelancing training; COVID-19 1166 helpline and NCOC dashboard |

Challenges: digital divide (gender, rural, literacy), misinformation, data privacy, internet shutdowns, platform dependence, sustainability after donor funding.

## 6.9 DSC in Pakistan: role, methods, difficulties (2017, 2023, 2024, 2025)

### Sectors and examples

| Sector | DSC use |
|---|---|
| **Health** | Lady Health Workers (1994; interpersonal counselling to millions of households); polio eradication communication (community mobilisers, ulema endorsements, celebrities, myth-busting); family planning; maternal and newborn health; COVID-19 (2020–22) |
| **Education and literacy (2025)** | **AIOU** distance education via radio and TV (since 1974); literacy campaigns (e.g. Iqra, Punjab Literacy and Non-Formal Basic Education programmes); Taleem Ghar (2020); enrolment drives ("Parho Punjab, Barho Punjab", 2009); girls' education campaigns; Article 25-A awareness |
| **Agriculture** | Radio and TV farm programmes; extension services; Kissan helplines; climate-smart farming messages; locust emergency messaging (2020) |
| **Disaster and climate** | NDMA/PDMA early warnings; PMD heatwave alerts; flood evacuation messaging (2022, 2025) |
| **Governance and social protection** | BISP/Ehsaas registration and payment information; Pakistan Citizen Portal; ECP voter education |
| **Social issues (2017)** | Child abuse awareness, anti-dowry, anti-drug, tolerance and interfaith harmony, women's rights, population growth, tobacco, road safety, water conservation |

### Methods (2024)

Audience research → strategy; mass media (TV, radio, newspapers); interpersonal communication (LHWs, extension workers, teachers, imams); folk media (puppetry, street theatre, Sufi poetry, melas); community meetings and participatory appraisal; social marketing; entertainment-education dramas; digital and mobile tools; celebrity and opinion-leader endorsements; monitoring and evaluation.

### Difficulties

1. Low literacy and linguistic diversity (many languages).
2. Top-down design by officials; little audience research.
3. Low credibility of government messages; conspiracy theories (polio vaccine rumours since 2011–12).
4. Security threats to field workers (polio workers killed).
5. Cultural and religious sensitivities (family planning).
6. Weak coordination after devolution; donor dependence and short projects.
7. Digital divide (gender gap in phone ownership: about 27% in 2025 per GSMA [press report]).
8. Commercial media give little free airtime for public service messages.
9. Weak evaluation of impact.

### How DSC helps national development (2023)

Raises awareness and knowledge; changes attitudes and behaviour; increases demand for services; mobilises participation; improves project efficiency; builds social cohesion and trust; supports SDG targets (health, education, gender, climate).

### Recommendations

National C4D strategy; audience research units in ministries; mandatory public service airtime on private channels (PEMRA licence condition); local-language content; train communicators; partner with ulema, teachers, influencers; digital inclusion for women; independent evaluation.

## 6.10 Development communication and nation-building (2022)

**Nation-building**: building national identity, unity and institutions (Karl Deutsch on communication and nationalism; Benedict Anderson's "imagined communities" through print).
- **Roles**: shared narratives and symbols (national songs, PTV's 1965 and 1971 war songs; Independence Day programming), language (Urdu as link language), education, integration of regions (regional-language programming), mobilising for development, crisis unity (2005 earthquake, 2022 floods), sports (cricket broadcasts, 1992 World Cup).
- **International examples**: India's SITE (1975–76) and Doordarshan's role; Malaysia's national development broadcasting; South Korea's Saemaul Undong (1970s) campaigns; Rwanda's post-genocide reconciliation radio (and its earlier misuse by RTLM in 1994 shows danger).
- **Risks**: propaganda, suppression of diversity, top-down uniformity; centripetal vs centrifugal balance (see notes 04).
