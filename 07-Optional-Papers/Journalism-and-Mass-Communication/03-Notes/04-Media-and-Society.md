## 4.1 Mass media and social change (2017, 2025)

**Social change**: significant alteration over time in behaviour patterns, cultural values, norms and social structures (Wilbert Moore). Sources: technology, economy, ideas, conflict, demography, communication.

**Theoretical views of the media's role.**

| View | Thinkers | Claim |
|---|---|---|
| Modernisation | **Daniel Lerner**, *The Passing of Traditional Society* (1958) — study of the Middle East; mass media create **empathy** (the capacity to imagine oneself in another's situation), a "mobility multiplier"; urbanisation → literacy → media exposure → political participation | Media drive change from traditional to modern |
| Development communication | **Wilbur Schramm**, *Mass Media and National Development* (UNESCO, 1964): media as **watchman** (surveillance), **policy-maker** (decision support) and **teacher** | Media accelerate development |
| Diffusion | Everett Rogers (1962) | Media spread awareness of innovations |
| Technological determinism | McLuhan, Innis | Media form changes society |
| Critical | Frankfurt School, Schiller, Herman and Chomsky | Media preserve the status quo and elite power |
| Cultural studies | Stuart Hall | Change is negotiated; audiences resist |
| Entertainment-education | **Miguel Sabido** (Mexico, 1970s); Peruvian telenovela *Simplemente María* (1969) inspired migrants to learn sewing and literacy; Singhal and Rogers (1999) | Drama can model new behaviour (social learning, Bandura) |

**Media can cause, facilitate, or resist change.** Robert Merton and Paul Lazarsfeld ("Mass Communication, Popular Taste and Organized Social Action", 1948) argued media often **conform** to the status quo and have a **narcotising dysfunction** (people mistake knowing about issues for doing something). They also named **status conferral** and **enforcement of social norms** as functions.

**Conditions for media-led change**: credibility, repetition, interpersonal reinforcement (two-step flow), compatibility with values, supporting services (e.g. clinics), and policy backing.

### Do Pakistani soaps empower women or contain them? (2020)

- *Empowerment arguments*: show women's problems publicly; feature strong women professionals (*Sinf-e-Aahan*, *Zindagi Gulzar Hai*); spread information about rights; women writers (Haseena Moin, Bano Qudsia, Umera Ahmed, Farhat Ishtiaq) and many women viewers.
- *Ideological containment arguments* (feminist media theory; Laura Mulvey's male gaze; Tuchman's "symbolic annihilation of women", 1978): women shown suffering silently, rewarded for sacrifice and obedience; divorce and independence punished in plots; marriage as the only goal; beauty standards (fair skin); women as conspirators against each other (mother-in-law vs daughter-in-law).
- **Balanced conclusion**: dramas are a contested space — they raise issues but often resolve them by restoring patriarchal order. Their effect depends on how audiences decode them (Hall's encoding/decoding) and on writers and regulators.

## 4.2 Media as a social system: interrelation and interdependence

- **Melvin DeFleur**, *Theories of Mass Communication* (1966): the mass media system is part of the larger social system; its parts (audiences, financers, producers, distributors, regulators, research) are interdependent. Media content (often "low-taste" content) persists because it keeps the system stable and profitable.
- **Media system dependency theory** (Sandra Ball-Rokeach and Melvin DeFleur, 1976): a tripartite relationship between **media, audiences and society**. The more people depend on media to meet needs (understanding, orientation, play), the greater the media's influence. Dependency increases in times of **conflict, crisis and rapid change** and in complex, urban societies.
- **Interrelation**: media influence politics, economy and culture; they in turn shape media (laws, advertising, audience tastes).
- **Interdependence**: media need government (licences, ads, information) and business (ads); government needs media (legitimacy, information); audiences need media (information, entertainment) and media need audiences (ratings).
- **Balance**: a healthy system balances these dependencies; imbalance produces either state control (media dependent on government ads) or commercial dominance (sensationalism for ratings).
- **Pakistan**: government ads and PEMRA licensing create dependence; channels depend on cable operators (who can be pressured to shift or block channels); ratings agencies influence content; audiences rely on TV in crises (2025 India–Pakistan conflict; floods).

## 4.3 Media freedom and its role in democracy (2016, 2022, 2023)

### Concepts

- **Fourth estate**: Thomas Carlyle attributed the phrase to Edmund Burke (the press gallery as a "Fourth Estate more important far than they all", *On Heroes*, 1841). The press as a fourth pillar alongside legislature, executive and judiciary.
- **Watchdog role**: scrutinising power, exposing corruption and abuse (Watergate, 1972–74; Panama Papers, 2016 — which led to Nawaz Sharif's disqualification in 2017).
- **Marketplace of ideas**: John Milton (*Areopagitica*, 1644), J.S. Mill (*On Liberty*, 1859); US Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes (1919): truth emerges from free competition of ideas.
- **Public sphere**: Jürgen Habermas, *The Structural Transformation of the Public Sphere* (1962): a space where citizens discuss public affairs rationally; mass commercial media later "refeudalised" it.
- **Constitution of Pakistan**: **Article 19** (freedom of speech, expression and press, subject to reasonable restrictions: glory of Islam, integrity, security or defence of Pakistan, friendly relations with foreign states, public order, decency or morality, contempt of court, incitement to an offence); **Article 19-A** (right to information, added by the 18th Amendment, 2010).
- International: UDHR Art. 19; ICCPR Art. 19 (Pakistan ratified ICCPR in 2010).

### Roles of media in democracy

1. **Information**: citizens need information to vote and participate.
2. **Watchdog**: accountability of government and powerful interests.
3. **Forum**: platform for diverse views and debate.
4. **Agenda-setting**: bringing public concerns to policy-makers.
5. **Mobilisation**: encouraging participation (voter education).
6. **Representation**: giving voice to marginalised groups.
7. **Education**: civic education about rights and institutions.
8. **Election coverage**: fair coverage, results transmission, monitoring.

### The functional approach and the ideal role (2016)

Functional approach: asks what functions media perform for society's stability (see 4.4). The *ideal* role in democracy combines the social responsibility model (truth, context, diversity, forum) with independence from owners and state.

**Sensationalism toward political issues (2016) — causes**: ratings competition (rating meters in a few cities), cheap studio talk shows instead of reporting, polarised owners, anchors as celebrities, "breaking news" culture, weak editorial control. **Workable solutions**: editorial independence and in-house ombudsmen; enforce PEMRA code on talk shows (no hate speech, right of reply); independent, transparent ratings system with a bigger sample; invest in investigative reporting units; training; self-regulatory body for broadcasters with real sanctions; media literacy.

### Pakistani examples of watchdog role (2022)

- Media coverage of the **lawyers' movement** (2007–09) helped restore the judiciary.
- Exposés: Panama Papers reporting (2016); coverage of sugar and wheat crises; investigative reporting on corruption cases; reporting on the 2022 floods' relief failures.
- Exposing abuse: viral videos of police torture; coverage of the Sahiwal incident (Jan 2019).
- Limits: self-censorship, "red lines", pressure on owners, journalists killed and abducted; media sometimes act as partisan attack dogs rather than watchdogs; "media trials" prejudicing court cases.

## 4.4 The functional approach: four social functions of the media

| Function | Source | Meaning | Dysfunction |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Surveillance** | Lasswell (1948) | Informing about threats and opportunities (news, weather, markets) | Panic, information overload, narcotisation |
| **Correlation** | Lasswell | Interpreting and linking parts of society; editorial, commentary; helps society respond | Conformity, reinforcing stereotypes |
| **Transmission of culture (socialisation)** | Lasswell | Passing on values and heritage across generations | Homogenisation, loss of local cultures |
| **Entertainment** | Charles Wright (1959/1960) | Relaxation and diversion | Escapism, low taste, passivity |

- McQuail adds **mobilisation** (campaigning for societal objectives, e.g. health, war, development).
- Lazarsfeld and Merton (1948): **status conferral** (media coverage legitimises people and issues), **enforcement of social norms** (exposure of deviance), **narcotising dysfunction**.
- **Wright** used Merton's functional analysis: functions and dysfunctions, manifest and latent, for society, subgroups, individuals and culture.
- **Critique of functionalism**: assumes stability is good; ignores power and conflict; hard to test.

## 4.5 Media as an awareness agent

Media create awareness of risks, rights and opportunities. Pakistani examples:

| Area | Example |
|---|---|
| Health | Polio campaigns (Sehat Tahaffuz), dengue awareness (Punjab 2011), COVID-19 (NCOC messaging 2020–21), breast cancer awareness (Pink Ribbon Pakistan) |
| Disasters | NDMA and PMD warnings for floods and heatwaves; TV coverage of the 2005 earthquake and 2010/2022 floods drove donations |
| Elections | ECP voter education; women's registration drives |
| Rights | Child abuse awareness after the Kasur scandal (2015) and Zainab case (2018) → Zainab Alert Act 2020 |
| Environment | Smog in Lahore; climate change |
| Education | Taleem Ghar/PTV Teleschool during COVID (2020) |
| Financial | SBP campaigns on digital payments and fraud |

Limits: awareness does not equal behaviour change (knowledge–practice gap); misinformation competes with awareness messages; urban bias.

## 4.6 Mass media and social representation

- **Social representations theory**: Serge **Moscovici** (*La Psychanalyse, son image et son public*, 1961): shared systems of values, ideas and practices that let people make sense of the world; formed through **anchoring** (fitting the new into existing categories) and **objectification** (making abstract ideas concrete). Media are a major producer of social representations.
- **Representation** (Stuart Hall, *Representation*, 1997): media do not simply reflect reality; they construct meanings through language and images.
- **Stereotyping**: simplified, often negative images of groups — women (homemakers, victims), ethnic groups (Pashtuns as guards, Punjabis as loud, Baloch as backward), religious minorities (absent or as sweepers), transgender people (comic relief), rural people (naïve), people with disabilities (pitiable).
- **Symbolic annihilation**: Gerbner and Gaye Tuchman (1978) — under-representation, trivialisation or condemnation of a group.
- **Consequences**: shape public attitudes and self-image; legitimise discrimination; affect policy.
- **Improvement**: diverse newsrooms; PEMRA and PCP codes on non-discrimination; guidelines on reporting minorities, gender and disability; audience feedback; training.

## 4.7 Centrifugal and centripetal effects (2020)

From **Denis McQuail**, *Mass Communication Theory*: media effects on society pull in two opposite directions:

| | Centripetal (unifying) | Centrifugal (dispersing) |
|---|---|---|
| Optimistic version | **Integration**: shared national culture, solidarity, common identity, social cohesion | **Freedom and diversity**: modernisation, individualism, new identities, plural voices |
| Pessimistic version | **Domination / homogenisation**: control, uniformity, manipulation, loss of minority cultures | **Fragmentation / normlessness**: loss of shared values, isolation, polarisation, anomie |

**Pakistani media and diverse groups (2020).**
- *Helped*: regional-language channels (KTN in Sindhi, AVT Khyber in Pashto, Rohi in Saraiki, Balochi programming), FM radio in local languages, social media for subcultures (youth, music, gender and religious minorities), cross-regional dramas.
- *Hindered*: urban, Urdu-centred mainstream content; stereotyping; limited coverage of Balochistan, ex-FATA and minorities; national-security framing silences ethnic movements; polarised talk shows fragment public opinion; online echo chambers deepen divisions.
- **Conclusion**: digital media have shifted the balance toward centrifugal effects — more diversity, but also more fragmentation — so public service media and shared national platforms matter for cohesion.
