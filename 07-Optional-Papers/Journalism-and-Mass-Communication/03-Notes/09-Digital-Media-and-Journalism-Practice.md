## 9.2 Social media in Pakistan: politics, news and society (2016, 2017, 2021)

### Role and uses

- **News source**: many young people get news first from social media, YouTube and WhatsApp; journalists break news on X; TV channels follow trends.
- **Political communication**: parties run social media teams; PTI was the first to build a large digital operation (from the 2013 election onward), other parties followed; hashtag campaigns and "trends"; online rallies; AI-generated speeches used by a jailed leader's party in the 2024 campaign (as reported).
- **Accountability**: viral videos expose abuse (police brutality, harassment); citizen journalism.
- **Activism**: Aurat March, students' movements, climate campaigns, flood relief crowdfunding.
- **Economy**: e-commerce, freelancing, influencer marketing, digital payments.
- **Diaspora engagement** and remittance-linked communication.

### Social media as a reinforcement tool for voters (2016)

Based on **reinforcement** (Klapper) and **selective exposure**: parties use social media to strengthen supporters' loyalty, mobilise them to vote and attend rallies, spread talking points, attack opponents, and create a bandwagon effect (trends, follower counts). Tools: micro-targeting, WhatsApp groups by constituency, influencer partnerships, live streams.

**Countering propaganda, abusive language and sectarianism online (2016)**:
1. Platform enforcement in local languages (Urdu, Punjabi, Pashto, Sindhi) and transparency reports.
2. Narrow, rights-compatible laws on incitement and hate speech; avoid vague offences.
3. ECP code of conduct for parties' digital campaigns; disclosure of political ads.
4. Fact-checking partnerships; rapid rebuttal units.
5. Media and digital literacy in schools and madrassas.
6. Religious leaders' joint statements against sectarian content (e.g. Paigham-e-Pakistan, 2018).
7. Counter-narratives by youth and influencers.
8. Party internal codes for social media wings.

### Social media as a news source (2021 note)

*Advantages*: speed, eyewitness content, diverse voices, interactivity, access for marginalised groups, low cost.
*Problems*: unverified claims, rumours, propaganda, bots, echo chambers, lack of context, algorithmic amplification of outrage, decline in trust, journalists' safety (harassment).
*Verdict*: valuable as a **starting point**; verification by professional journalism remains essential.

## 9.3 Fake news, disinformation and fact-checking (2024)

- **Information disorder** (Claire Wardle and Hossein Derakhshan, Council of Europe, 2017):
  - **Misinformation**: false information shared without intent to harm.
  - **Disinformation**: false information deliberately created to harm.
  - **Malinformation**: genuine information shared to cause harm (leaks, doxxing).
- **Seven types** (Wardle, First Draft): satire/parody; misleading content; imposter content; fabricated content; false connection (clickbait headlines); false context; manipulated content.
- **Causes**: political polarisation, low media literacy, algorithms rewarding engagement, low trust in mainstream media, anonymity, monetisation, state and foreign influence operations, AI tools.
- **Pakistani examples**: rumours during COVID-19 (e.g. vaccine myths); fake news during the **May 2025 India–Pakistan conflict** on both sides' media and social media; deepfake videos of politicians; recurring polio vaccine rumours; fake notifications of school closures and fuel shortages; blasphemy-related rumours triggering mob violence (e.g. Jaranwala, Aug 2023).
- **Responses**: fact-checking organisations (international and local: AFP Fact Check Pakistan, Soch Fact Check, Geo Fact Check, and others [verify current list]); government fact-check accounts; platform labels; PECA 2025 s. 26A (criminal approach — criticised); media literacy; pre-bunking (inoculation theory).
- **Critique of criminalising "fake news"**: who decides truth? Risk of silencing critics and journalists; chilling effect; better to use transparency, corrections and literacy.

## 9.4 Digital divide and access to information (2024 note)

- **Digital divide**: the gap between those who have access to and can effectively use digital technologies and those who cannot. Levels: (1) **access** (infrastructure, devices, cost), (2) **skills and use**, (3) **outcomes** (benefits gained) — van Dijk; Hargittai's "second-level divide".
- **Dimensions in Pakistan**: rural–urban; gender (GSMA figures above); income; literacy and language; age; disability; regional (Balochistan, ex-FATA, Gilgit-Baltistan coverage gaps); shutdowns and blocking.
- **Consequences**: knowledge gap (Tichenor et al.), exclusion from e-government, banking, education and jobs; women's exclusion; digital-only relief and registration excludes the poorest.
- **Policies**: Digital Pakistan vision (2018); Universal Service Fund rural connectivity; Digital Nation Pakistan Act 2025 (assented Jan 2025 alongside the PECA amendment, as reported); affordable smartphones; women's digital inclusion strategies; digital skills (DigiSkills); local-language content; right to information (Article 19-A, RTI Act 2017).

## 9.5 New technologies and the TV industry (2020)

- **Streaming / OTT**: Netflix, Amazon Prime, YouTube, local OTT apps (e.g. Tapmad, Tamasha [verify]); cord-cutting; on-demand viewing; binge culture.
- **Effects**: fragmentation of audiences; decline of appointment viewing and cable revenue; advertising shifts to digital; content globalisation; dramas now earn huge YouTube views (Pakistani dramas' YouTube channels among the most viewed in the region [verify]); data-driven commissioning; new genres (web series); regulation gap (PEMRA's attempts to regulate web TV and OTT, 2020 consultation [verify]).
- **3-D TV**: failed commercially (glasses, cost, little content) — an example of an innovation that did not diffuse (low compatibility and relative advantage).
- **Other tech**: 4K/UHD, smart TVs, IPTV, mobile TV, AI-generated news presenters, virtual studios, drone journalism, mobile journalism (MoJo).

## 9.6 Mobile phones and students (2023 opinion question)

- *Negative effects*: distraction and multitasking reduce learning; reduced sleep; addiction to short videos; cyberbullying; exposure to harmful content; reduced reading and face-to-face interaction; cheating.
- *Positive uses*: access to online lectures (YouTube, Khan Academy), e-books, educational apps, communication with teachers, research skills; vital during COVID-19 school closures.
- *Evidence*: some international studies link school phone bans with small gains in results; UNESCO's *Global Education Monitoring Report 2023* recommended that technology in schools be used only when it supports learning and noted many countries restricting phones in schools.
- *Balanced opinion*: the problem is excessive, unguided use; solutions — digital literacy, parental guidance, school policies (phone-free classrooms), screen-time tools, using phones for structured learning.
- **Theory link**: uses and gratifications (students use phones for entertainment and social needs); displacement hypothesis; McLuhan (medium shapes attention).

## 9.7 Journalism basics (MCQ bank)

### News

- **News**: timely, accurate account of events or ideas of interest to the public. "When a dog bites a man, that is not news; when a man bites a dog, that is news" (attributed to John B. Bogart or Charles Dana, *New York Sun*).
- **News values** (Galtung and Ruge, 1965): frequency, threshold, unambiguity, meaningfulness (cultural proximity), consonance, unexpectedness, continuity, composition, reference to elite nations, reference to elite persons, personalisation, negativity. Simpler set: timeliness, proximity, prominence, impact, conflict, human interest, novelty.
- **5Ws and 1H**: who, what, when, where, why, how.
- **Inverted pyramid**: most important information first.
- **Lead/intro**: first paragraph; **dateline**: place and date; **byline**: writer's name; **slug**: short label for a story; **embargo**: request not to publish before a set time; **kicker**; **headline**, **strapline**.
- **Scoop**: an exclusive story obtained before rivals (2025 MCQ).
- **Feature**: a soft, in-depth or human-interest story, not tied to immediate news (2025 MCQ).
- **Editorial**: an article expressing the newspaper's own opinion, usually unsigned (2025 MCQ); **op-ed**: opinion piece by an outside writer opposite the editorial page; **column**; **letters to the editor**.
- **Masthead**, **nameplate**, **broadsheet** vs **tabloid**.
- **Teleprompter (prompter)**: device that displays the script for presenters to read on camera (2025 MCQ).
- **Attribution / reference** to a source makes news more credible (2025 MCQ).
- **Objectivity**: presenting facts fairly without personal bias (2026 MCQ); critics say total objectivity is impossible, so aim for fairness, balance and transparency.
- **Gatekeeping** in the digital age is challenged by the democratisation of information (2026 MCQ).
- **Citizen journalism**: members of the public collecting and sharing news (2026 MCQ).
- **Microblogging**: short posts — X (Twitter), Threads, Tumblr; Facebook and Instagram are usually classed as social networking/photo-sharing (2026 MCQ, keyed "None of these" for "Facebook / Instagram / both" [likely]).

### Types of journalism

Investigative (Watergate, 1972–74; Panama Papers, 2016); **yellow journalism** (1890s Pulitzer vs Hearst circulation war; "The Yellow Kid" comic); **precision journalism** (Philip Meyer, 1973 — social science methods; on FPSC list); **data journalism**; **development journalism**; **peace journalism** (Johan Galtung); **advocacy journalism**; **embedded journalism** (Iraq 2003); **solutions journalism**; **mobile journalism**; **citizen journalism**; **chequebook journalism** (paying for stories).
