# 1. Introduction to Mass Communication and Basic Models

Syllabus section I. Full questions: Shannon-Weaver (2017); notes on Shannon-Weaver (2016), Gerbner (2017, 2025), business communication (2016). Models are also a rich MCQ source (2026: Lasswell's formula; Shannon-Weaver as "linear model"; intrapersonal communication). Draw each model as a labelled box diagram in the exam.

## 1.1 Concept and definitions

- **Communication** comes from the Latin *communis* (common) and *communicare* (to share, to make common). It is the process of creating shared meaning by sending and receiving messages.
- Definitions to quote:
  - **Wilbur Schramm**: communication is an attempt to establish "commonness" with a receiver about information, an idea or an attitude.
  - **Charles Cooley**: the mechanism through which human relations exist and develop.
  - **Harold Lasswell**: "Who says what in which channel to whom with what effect?"
  - **Carl Hovland** (1953): the process by which an individual (communicator) transmits stimuli to modify the behaviour of others.
- **Mass communication**: the process by which professional communicators use technological media to send messages rapidly and simultaneously to large, heterogeneous, anonymous and scattered audiences (after **Charles Wright**, *Mass Communication: A Sociological Perspective*, 1959; **Morris Janowitz**, 1968).

### Characteristics of mass communication (Wright)

| Feature | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Audience | Large, heterogeneous, anonymous, scattered |
| Message | Public, rapid, transient (largely), standardised |
| Source | Complex organisation; high cost; professional communicators; gatekeepers |
| Feedback | Delayed and indirect (letters, ratings) — now faster through social media |
| Channel | Technological: print, radio, TV, film, internet |

## 1.2 Need, importance and purposes

- **Individual**: information, decision-making, identity, relationships, entertainment.
- **Social**: socialisation, transmission of culture, integration, coordination of action.
- **Political**: public opinion, accountability, democracy, mobilisation.
- **Economic**: markets, advertising, coordination of business.
- **Development**: spreading innovations, health and education messages.
- **Purposes** (classic trio): to inform, to educate, to entertain — plus to persuade.

## 1.3 Types of communication

| Type | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Intrapersonal** | Communication within oneself (thinking, self-talk) | Deciding what to say (2026 MCQ) |
| **Interpersonal** | Between two or a few people, face to face | Conversation, interview |
| **Group** | Small groups (3–15) | Committee meeting |
| **Public** | One speaker to an audience | Lecture, speech |
| **Mass** | Through mass media to large audiences | TV news |
| **Organisational** | Within and between organisations: upward, downward, horizontal; formal and informal (grapevine) | Memo, staff meeting |
| **Intercultural / international** | Across cultures or nations | Diplomacy, global media |
| **Verbal vs non-verbal** | Words vs body language, gestures, proxemics, paralanguage | Tone of voice |
| **Business communication** (2016 note) | Communication within and by businesses to employees, customers, investors: letters, reports, meetings, advertising, PR, now email and social media. Principles: the "7 Cs" — clear, concise, concrete, correct, coherent, complete, courteous | Annual report, customer service |

## 1.4 Process of communication — elements

Source/sender → encoding → message → channel/medium → decoding → receiver → feedback, all inside a **context**, with **noise** at every stage.

| Element | Note |
|---|---|
| Source | Has a purpose, credibility, field of experience |
| Encoding | Turning ideas into symbols |
| Message | Content and form |
| Channel | Medium carrying the message |
| Decoding | Interpreting symbols |
| Receiver | Audience, with their own field of experience |
| Feedback | Response that lets the source adjust |
| Noise | Physical (static), semantic (jargon), psychological (bias), technical |
| Context | Physical, social, cultural, historical |

**Three broad kinds of model**: linear (one-way: Lasswell, Shannon-Weaver), interactional (circular, with feedback: Osgood-Schramm), transactional (simultaneous sending and receiving: Barnlund, 1970).

## 1.5 Functions of a model; evaluating a model

**What is a model?** A simplified, often diagrammatic, representation of a process that shows its main parts and the relations between them (Denis McQuail and Sven Windahl, *Communication Models for the Study of Mass Communications*, Longman, 1982; 2nd ed. 1993 — on the FPSC list).

**Functions of a model** (Karl Deutsch, 1952; McQuail and Windahl):
1. **Organising**: orders and relates parts of a system; gives a picture of the whole.
2. **Heuristic (explaining)**: helps explain processes and generate new questions and research.
3. **Predictive**: allows predictions of outcomes ("if feedback is absent, misunderstanding increases").
4. **Measuring**: where the model's parts can be quantified, allows measurement (e.g. Shannon's information theory).

**Evaluating a model** (Deutsch's criteria):
1. **Generality**: how many situations does it fit?
2. **Heuristic value**: does it suggest new research?
3. **Importance / significance** of predictions.
4. **Originality**: new insights?
5. **Parsimony / simplicity**: no unnecessary parts.
6. **Accuracy / realism**: does it match reality?
Weaknesses of all models: oversimplification, static view of a dynamic process, risk of being taken as reality.

## 1.6 Lasswell's model (1948)

- **Source**: Harold Lasswell, "The Structure and Function of Communication in Society" (1948). Lasswell was a political scientist who studied WWI propaganda (*Propaganda Technique in the World War*, 1927) — often called the father of propaganda studies (2025 MCQ).
- **Formula**: **Who** (communicator) **says what** (message) **in which channel** (medium) **to whom** (audience) **with what effect** (effect).
- **Research areas** that follow: control analysis (who), content analysis (what), media analysis (channel), audience analysis (whom), effect analysis (effect).
- **Three functions of communication** (same article): surveillance of the environment; correlation of parts of society in responding to the environment; transmission of social heritage. (Charles Wright added **entertainment** in 1959/1960.)

**Diagram (describe):** five boxes in a row: Communicator → Message → Medium → Receiver → Effect.

**Strengths**: simple, versatile, introduced the idea of effects; guided media research for decades.
**Weaknesses**: linear and one-way; no feedback or noise; assumes an effect always occurs; ignores context and audience activity; reflects the propaganda-era "powerful effects" assumption.
**Braddock (1958)** added two questions: under what circumstances, and for what purpose.

## 1.7 Shannon-Weaver model (1948/1949) — asked 2016 and 2017

- **Source**: Claude Shannon, "A Mathematical Theory of Communication", *Bell System Technical Journal* (1948); published as a book with Warren Weaver's interpretation in 1949. Shannon was an engineer at Bell Laboratories; the aim was efficient signal transmission over telephone lines.
- **Elements**: Information source → **transmitter** (encoder) → signal → **channel** → received signal → **receiver** (decoder) → destination; **noise source** interferes with the signal in the channel.
- Called the "mother of all models"; a **linear** (transmission) model (2026 MCQ).
- Weaver's three levels of problems: **technical** (how accurately can symbols be transmitted?), **semantic** (how precisely do symbols convey the intended meaning?), **effectiveness** (how effectively does the received meaning affect conduct?).
- Key concepts: noise, **redundancy** (repetition that counters noise), **entropy** (unpredictability/information), channel capacity, bits.
- Later versions added a **feedback** loop (Norbert Wiener's cybernetics, 1948).

**Implications / importance (2016, 2017).**
1. Introduced **noise**, a concept now used in all models (physical, semantic, psychological).
2. Shifted attention to the **channel** and technology; basis of information theory, telecommunications and computing.
3. Gave a common vocabulary (encoder, decoder, channel) to later models: Schramm's encoder–decoder, Berlo's SMCR (1960), Westley-MacLean, DeFleur's model (1970, which added feedback and the meaning dimension).
4. Practical: advertising and PR use redundancy and multiple channels to cut through noise.

**Shortcomings.**
1. Linear, one-way; original version has no feedback.
2. Mechanistic: treats communication as signal transfer, ignores **meaning** and context.
3. Receiver is passive.
4. Designed for machines (telephony); human communication involves culture, emotion, relationships.
5. Does not explain why messages are sent or their social effects.
6. Equates information with meaning; semantic noise is hard to quantify.

## 1.8 Osgood's model (1954) and Schramm's models (1954)

### Osgood–Schramm circular model

- **Charles Osgood** (psychologist, semantic differential) argued that each person is both source and receiver, and that **interpretation** is central. Each communicator performs **encoding, decoding and interpreting**.
- **Wilbur Schramm**, "How Communication Works" (in *The Process and Effects of Mass Communication*, 1954), drew this as a **circular model**: Encoder–Interpreter–Decoder ⇄ message ⇄ Decoder–Interpreter–Encoder.
- **Strengths**: two-way, dynamic, emphasises interpretation and feedback; no fixed sender or receiver.
- **Weaknesses**: implies equal partners (not true in mass communication); fits interpersonal more than mass communication; ignores noise and channel.

### Schramm's "field of experience" model

- Communication succeeds only where the sender's and receiver's **fields of experience** overlap (shared language, culture, knowledge). Diagram: two overlapping circles, encoder → signal → decoder, the overlap is shared meaning.
- Use in Pakistan: health campaigns fail when messages use urban English terms for rural audiences — no overlapping field.

### Schramm's mass communication model

- The media organisation acts as decoder, interpreter and encoder of events; it sends many identical messages to a mass audience made of individuals embedded in **groups** that reinterpret messages; feedback is inferential and delayed (ratings, letters). Links to the two-step flow.

## 1.9 Newcomb's symmetry (ABX) model (1953)

- **Theodore Newcomb**, "An Approach to the Study of Communicative Acts", *Psychological Review* (1953).
- A **triangle**: two communicators **A** and **B** and an object of communication **X** (a person, issue or thing). Communication maintains **balance (symmetry)** in the orientations of A and B toward each other and toward X.
- If A and B like each other but disagree about X, there is **strain toward symmetry**; they communicate to reduce it (one changes their view, they change their view of each other, or they reduce X's importance).
- Roots: Fritz Heider's balance theory (1946); related to Festinger's cognitive dissonance (1957).
- **Strengths**: first model to show communication's social function of maintaining relationships and consensus; applies to public opinion (people seek information that keeps their group in balance).
- **Weaknesses**: interpersonal focus; assumes people seek balance; static; ignores power differences.
- **Mass media application**: Westley and MacLean built on it.

## 1.10 Westley–MacLean model (1957; the FPSC syllabus dates it 1976)

- **Bruce Westley and Malcolm MacLean**, "A Conceptual Model for Communications Research", *Journalism Quarterly* (1957). (The FPSC syllabus lists 1976, probably a reprint or later edition [verify]; write "1957" with the note if asked.)
- Adapts Newcomb to mass communication. Elements:
  - **X** = events or objects in the environment (X1…Xn).
  - **A** = advocacy role: a source with a purpose (politician, advertiser).
  - **C** = channel role: the **media/gatekeeper** who selects from A's messages and from X directly, and passes them to B.
  - **B** = behavioural system role: the audience.
  - **X′, X″** = messages as encoded by A and by C.
  - **f** = feedback: fBA (B to A), fBC (B to C), fCA (C to A).
- **Insights**: introduced the **gatekeeper** (linked to Kurt Lewin, 1947, and David Manning White's "Mr. Gates" study, 1950); audiences also observe some X directly; media select according to audience needs; feedback flows in several directions.
- **Strengths**: realistic for journalism; explains selection and agenda.
- **Weaknesses**: does not show power and ownership; feedback in mass media is weak; complex.

**Diagram (describe):** X's on the left feed into A and directly into C; A sends X′ to C; C sends X″ to B; dotted feedback lines run back from B to C and A, and from C to A.

## 1.11 Gerbner's general model (1956) — asked as a note in 2017 and 2025

- **George Gerbner**, "Toward a General Model of Communication", *Audio Visual Communication Review* (1956). Gerbner later led the **Cultural Indicators** project and **cultivation theory**.
- **Verbal formula**: Someone perceives an event and reacts in a situation through some means to make available materials in some form and context conveying content with some consequence.
- **Two dimensions**:
  1. **Horizontal (perceptual) dimension**: the event **E** is perceived by a person or machine **M** as **E1** (the percept). Perception depends on selection, context and availability — what M sees is not the full reality.
  2. **Vertical (means and control) dimension**: M produces a **statement about the event**, **SE**, using channels and media over which M has varying control. The **form (S)** and **content (E)** are joined.
  Then a second perceiver **M2** perceives SE as **SE1**, and the process can continue in a chain.
- **Strengths**: links communication with **perception and reality** (the gap between event and report); applies to both human and machine perception; dynamic and chain-like; useful for journalism (how a news event becomes a news story) and for studying bias.
- **Weaknesses**: complex; little on feedback and effects on audience behaviour; mostly descriptive.
- **Journalism application**: a protest (E) is seen by a reporter (M) from one side of the crowd (E1); the reporter writes a story (SE) shaped by the channel's format and editorial control; viewers (M2) perceive it through their own frames (SE1). Explains why reports differ from events.
