## 3.1 Historical context of international communication

| Period | Developments |
|---|---|
| Pre-modern | Trade routes, couriers, religious networks; Mughal *dak* postal system |
| 1830s–1860s | **Telegraph** (Morse, 1837–44); news agencies: **Havas** (France, 1835), **Associated Press** (US, 1846), **Wolff** (Germany, 1849), **Reuters** (London, 1851). First successful transatlantic cable 1866. **International Telegraph Union** (1865, now ITU — the oldest UN agency) |
| 1870 | **News agency cartel ("ring combination")**: Reuters, Havas and Wolff divided the world into exclusive zones; Reuters covered the British Empire including India — colonial control of information |
| 1900s–1940s | **Radio**: Marconi (1901 transatlantic signal); state propaganda in WWI and WWII; **BBC Empire Service** (1932); Radio Moscow; **Voice of America** (1942); All India Radio (1936). Cinema: Hollywood dominance |
| 1945–1989: Cold War | Radio Free Europe; propaganda war; **UNESCO** (1945); **UDHR Article 19** (1948) and the US-backed "free flow of information" doctrine; decolonisation and demands for NWICO; **satellites**: Sputnik (1957), Telstar (1962), **Intelsat** (1964), Early Bird (1965) |
| 1970s–1980s | NAM and UNESCO debates; **MacBride Commission** (1977–80); US withdrawal from UNESCO (1984), UK (1985) |
| 1980s–1990s | Deregulation and privatisation; **CNN** (1980) and the Gulf War (1991) — "CNN effect" on foreign policy; transnational satellite TV (Star TV, 1991, in Asia); **Al Jazeera** (1996) as a Southern counter-voice |
| 1990s–2000s | **Internet**: ARPANET (1969), World Wide Web (Tim Berners-Lee, 1989–91); globalisation of media conglomerates (Time Warner, News Corp, Disney, Bertelsmann) |
| 2000s–present | Social media (Facebook 2004, YouTube 2005, Twitter 2006, WhatsApp 2009, TikTok 2016–17 internationally); platform power (Google, Meta, Apple, Amazon, Microsoft, ByteDance); streaming (Netflix global since 2016); state international broadcasters (RT, CGTN, TRT World); AI and misinformation |

## 3.2 Globalisation, technology and the mass media

**Globalisation**: the intensification of worldwide social relations linking distant places so that local events are shaped by events far away (Anthony Giddens, 1990). Dimensions: economic, political, cultural, technological.

**Key thinkers.**
- **Marshall McLuhan**: the **global village** (1962).
- **Anthony Giddens**: time–space distanciation.
- **David Harvey**: time–space compression (1989).
- **Manuel Castells**: *The Rise of the Network Society* (1996) — networks and information flows as the basis of social organisation.
- **Arjun Appadurai**, "Disjuncture and Difference in the Global Cultural Economy" (1990): five "scapes" — **ethnoscapes, mediascapes, technoscapes, financescapes, ideoscapes**; global flows are disjunctive, not one-way.
- **Roland Robertson**: **glocalisation** (1992/1995).
- **Thomas Friedman**: *The World Is Flat* (2005).
- **Henry Jenkins**: *Convergence Culture* (2006, on the FPSC list).

**How media and technology drive globalisation (2019).**
1. Satellites and cables make instant global news possible.
2. Transnational media corporations distribute the same content worldwide.
3. Internet and social media connect individuals across borders.
4. Advertising globalises consumer culture and brands.
5. Media spread political ideas (democracy, human rights, also extremism).
6. Digital platforms enable global markets (freelancing, e-commerce).

**Are we living in a global village? (2019)**
- *Yes*: instant global events (World Cup, wars live on social media); shared popular culture; diaspora connections; global movements (#MeToo, climate strikes).
- *No / only partly*: **digital divide** (about a third of humanity is still offline — ITU estimated about 2.6 billion people offline in 2024 [verify]); language barriers; censorship and internet shutdowns; algorithmic bubbles create many "villages"; information flows are unequal (North to South); nationalism has risen alongside connectivity.
- Balanced answer: a "global village" with gated neighbourhoods and unequal wealth — connected but not equal.

**Globalisation vs Westernisation (2022).**

| | Globalisation | Westernisation |
|---|---|---|
| Direction | Multidirectional flows (Turkish dramas, K-pop, Bollywood, Chinese apps) | One-way adoption of Western (mainly US/European) culture |
| Scope | Economic, technological, cultural, political | Mainly cultural and value change |
| Agency | Local actors adapt (glocalisation, hybridity) | Implies imitation |
| Example in Pakistan | TikTok (Chinese), Ertuğrul (Turkish), Coke Studio (local remix of global format) | Western fashion, English-medium culture, fast food |

**Impact on Pakistani culture (2022).** Positive: exposure to ideas, global markets for Pakistani music and dramas (Coke Studio's "Pasoori", 2022, reached global audiences), diaspora links, women's awareness. Negative: consumerism, erosion of regional languages, Indian and Western content dominance in the past, identity anxieties, moral panic debates, brain drain. Responses: PEMRA content quotas (limits on foreign content share [verify current rule]), promotion of local content, Supreme Court's 2018 ban on Indian content on TV channels [verify current status].

## 3.3 Communication and cultural imperialism (2016, 2018, 2024)

- **Cultural imperialism**: the domination of one culture over others through media and cultural products, leading to the erosion of local cultures and the spread of the dominant culture's values (mainly American consumer capitalism).
- **Herbert Schiller**, *Mass Communications and American Empire* (1969); *Communication and Cultural Domination* (1976): US media-military-industrial complex exports content and values.
- **Jeremy Tunstall**, *The Media Are American* (1977).
- **Ariel Dorfman and Armand Mattelart**, *How to Read Donald Duck* (1971): Disney comics carried capitalist, imperialist ideology in Chile.
- **Oliver Boyd-Barrett** (1977): **media imperialism** — ownership, structure, distribution or content of media in one country subject to pressure from another's media interests.
- **Johan Galtung**, structural theory of imperialism (1971): centre–periphery relations in communication.
- **Nordenstreng and Varis**, *Television Traffic — A One-Way Street?* (UNESCO, 1974): most TV programmes flowed from the US and a few European countries to the rest.
- **George Ritzer**: McDonaldization (1993).

**Mechanisms.** Ownership of global media and platforms; cheap export of programmes (amortised in home market); advertising and brands; news agencies and wire services setting the agenda; English as lingua franca; tech platforms' algorithms and terms of service.

**Effects claimed.** Homogenisation; consumerism; erosion of local languages and traditions; distorted images of the South; dependence of local media on imported content; loss of indigenous identities (2024).

**Critiques of the thesis.**
1. **Active audiences**: Tamar Liebes and Elihu Katz, *The Export of Meaning* (1990) — viewers of *Dallas* in Israel and elsewhere read it in different ways.
2. **Cultural proximity**: Joseph Straubhaar (1991) — audiences prefer local and regional content when available.
3. **Contra-flow and regional hubs**: Bollywood, Brazilian telenovelas, Korean Wave (Hallyu), Turkish dramas, Al Jazeera, Chinese apps. Daya Thussu's "contra-flow" (2007).
4. **Hybridity and glocalisation**: Nestor García Canclini; Robertson.
5. Overstates passivity; romanticises "authentic" local culture; ignores local elites' role.

**From cultural imperialism to "platform imperialism"** (Dal Yong Jin, 2015): US (and Chinese) platforms now dominate data, advertising revenue and infrastructure. Local news outlets lose ad revenue to Google and Meta.

**Pakistan.** Past dominance of Indian films and Western shows on cable; satellite dishes in the 1990s; then the boom of local private channels (2002–) reduced dependence; Turkish dramas (*Diriliş: Ertuğrul* on PTV from April 2020, encouraged by PM Imran Khan) — a Muslim-world contra-flow; Pakistani dramas popular in India, the Gulf and diaspora; Netflix/YouTube consumption among youth; concerns about "fifth-generation warfare" narratives (state discourse framing foreign information influence).

**How to avoid cultural imperialism (2016).** Invest in quality local content (drama, film, animation, children's programming); PEMRA local-content quotas; support regional-language media; media literacy; co-productions with regional partners; digital platforms for Pakistani content (e.g. state streaming such as PTV's digital platforms); protect public service broadcasting; promote Pakistani soft power (music, cricket, cuisine, Sufi heritage).

## 3.4 Communication flow: imbalance between North and South

**The imbalance (1970s critique).**
- **Big Four news agencies** — AP, UPI (US), Reuters (UK), AFP (France) — supplied most international news; about 80% of world news flow was said to originate in London, Paris and New York [figure commonly cited; verify].
- Coverage of the South focused on "coups and earthquakes": crises, disasters, conflict (the "four Cs" — conflict, crisis, catastrophe, corruption); little development news.
- Unequal infrastructure: telecom tariffs, satellites, radio frequencies (the North held most spectrum), newsprint.
- Content flows: TV programmes, films, advertising from the North.

**NWICO — New World Information and Communication Order.**
- Demanded by the **Non-Aligned Movement** (Algiers summit, 1973; NAM symposium in Tunis 1976; NAM News Agencies Pool, 1975, coordinated by Tanjug of Yugoslavia).
- Tunisian information minister **Mustapha Masmoudi**'s paper "The New World Information Order" (1978).
- **UNESCO Mass Media Declaration** (1978).
- Principles: balanced flow, national sovereignty over information, right to communicate, support for Southern news agencies and infrastructure, a code of conduct for journalists (controversial).
- Opposed by the US and UK as a threat to press freedom ("free flow" doctrine); led to their withdrawal from UNESCO (US 1984, UK 1985; UK rejoined 1997, US 2003, left again 2018, rejoined 2023, announced exit again in 2025 [verify]).

### National sovereignty vs free flow of information (2020)

| Free flow doctrine | Sovereignty / balanced flow |
|---|---|
| Information should cross borders without government barriers (UDHR Art. 19; US policy since 1946) | States have a right to protect their culture, security and development priorities |
| Promotes democracy, markets, human rights | "Free flow" means one-way flow from powerful media; disguises commercial dominance |
| Backed by the US, Western media | Backed by NAM, Soviet bloc, UNESCO in the 1970s |
| Today: open internet, platform freedom | Today: data localisation, platform regulation, content blocking, internet shutdowns, "digital sovereignty" (EU's Digital Services Act 2022; China's Great Firewall) |

**Pakistan examples**: YouTube ban (Sept 2012–Jan 2016) over a blasphemous video; repeated TikTok bans (2020–21) on obscenity grounds; **X blocked 17 Feb 2024 – 7 May 2025** (after election-rigging allegations; restored during the India–Pakistan conflict); PECA 2025's Social Media Protection and Regulatory Authority; mobile internet shutdowns during protests (e.g. May 2023, Nov 2024 [verify]); ban on Indian content. Balance: legitimate concerns (blasphemy, security, child protection, disinformation) vs costs (economic losses to freelancers and businesses, rights violations, credibility).

## 3.5 The MacBride (McBride) Commission (2017, 2019, 2026)

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Full name | International Commission for the Study of Communication Problems |
| Set up | 1977 by UNESCO Director-General **Amadou-Mahtar M'Bow** (Senegal), following the 1976 Nairobi General Conference |
| Chair | **Seán MacBride** (Ireland): founder of Amnesty International (co-founder, 1961), Nobel Peace Prize 1974, Lenin Peace Prize 1977 |
| Members | 16 members from different regions and ideological blocs, including Gabriel García Márquez (Colombia), Mustapha Masmoudi (Tunisia), Elie Abel (USA), Sergei Losev (USSR) and B.G. Verghese (India) [verify names before citing] |
| Report | ***Many Voices, One World: Towards a New, More Just and More Efficient World Information and Communication Order*** (1980) |
| Adoption | Presented and adopted (in substance) at the UNESCO General Conference in **Belgrade, 1980** |
| Recommendations | **82** recommendations |
| Follow-up | **International Programme for the Development of Communication (IPDC)**, created 1980, to fund media development in developing countries |

### How the report analysed communication problems (2017)

1. **Imbalance and inequality** in flows, infrastructure, news agencies, and technology between North and South.
2. **Concentration and commercialisation**: transnational corporations and advertising dominate.
3. **Distorted images** of developing countries; cultural dependence.
4. **Threats to journalists** and press freedom (censorship by governments).
5. **One-way, top-down communication** within societies; lack of access and participation.
6. **Right to communicate** as an emerging human right.

### Main recommendations (group them)

| Area | Recommendations |
|---|---|
| Communication policies | National communication policies linked to development; strengthen independence and self-reliance |
| Infrastructure | Develop news agencies, broadcasting, printing and newsprint in developing countries; reduce telecom tariffs; fair allocation of the radio spectrum and satellite orbits |
| Professional standards | Training of journalists; codes of ethics adopted by the profession; protection of journalists (but **no special licensing** of journalists) |
| Democratisation | Right to communicate; access and participation; alternative and community media; reduce concentration and monopolies |
| Cultural identity | Protect cultural identity; promote local production; reduce one-way flows |
| International cooperation | Strengthen IPDC; South–South exchange (NAM news pool); balanced reporting |
| Freedom | Abolish censorship; free access to sources; responsibility with freedom |

### Criticisms and fate

- The US and UK saw it as a cover for state control of media and licensing journalists; Western press groups (World Press Freedom Committee) campaigned against it; the US left UNESCO in 1984.
- Some Southern governments used NWICO rhetoric to justify control of their own media.
- Too broad and compromise-driven; little enforcement.
- UNESCO shifted after 1989 to a "free flow + balance" approach (Windhoek Declaration on independent press, 1991; World Press Freedom Day, 3 May, from 1993).

### Relevance today (2026 note)

The internet seemed to solve scarcity, but new imbalances appeared: **platform concentration**, data flows to US and Chinese firms, algorithmic visibility, collapse of local news revenue, AI trained mostly on Northern data, and global South languages under-represented. Calls for a "new NWICO" in digital governance (UN Global Digital Compact, 2024) echo MacBride.

**Pakistan**: APP (founded 1947, nationalised 1961) and PPI (1956) as national agencies; dependence on Reuters, AFP, AP for world news; PTV World/PTV Global; state push to project Pakistan's "narrative" internationally.

## 3.6 International communication in the internet age: new social media and the developing world (2016, 2025)

**What is "new social media"? (2025).** Internet-based platforms that let users create, share and interact with content in networks: social networking (Facebook), microblogging (X/Twitter, Threads), video sharing (YouTube, TikTok), messaging (WhatsApp, Telegram), image sharing (Instagram), professional (LinkedIn). Features: user-generated content, interactivity, many-to-many, real-time, mobile, algorithmic curation, convergence (Jenkins).

**Effects on the developing world.**

| Positive | Negative |
|---|---|
| Voice for marginalised groups; citizen journalism | Misinformation and disinformation; deepfakes |
| Political mobilisation (Arab Spring 2010–11; Pakistan's 2007 lawyers' movement used SMS and blogs) | Hate speech, ethnic and religious violence (Facebook's role in Myanmar against the Rohingya, 2017, acknowledged by Meta) |
| Economic opportunities: e-commerce, freelancing, digital payments | Surveillance and repression; online harassment (especially women journalists) |
| Access to education and health information | Digital divide; platform dependency; data extraction |
| Diaspora connection and remittances | Polarisation, echo chambers, addiction, effects on youth mental health |
| Accountability (viral videos of abuse) | Decline of professional journalism revenue |

**Ethical guidelines to make social media use responsible (2025).**
1. Verify before sharing; label opinion vs fact; correct errors openly.
2. Respect privacy and dignity; avoid sharing images of victims, children or the dead.
3. No hate speech, incitement or blasphemy-based mob calls.
4. Transparency about sponsorship and paid content (influencer disclosures).
5. Platform accountability: clear content moderation, local-language moderators, appeals, transparency reports (models: EU Digital Services Act 2022; UNESCO Guidelines for the Governance of Digital Platforms, 2023).
6. Media and digital literacy in schools.
7. Laws that are narrowly drawn, proportionate and reviewed by courts — not vague "fake news" offences.

## 3.7 Communication and culture: Marxist and Gramscian approaches (2018)

- **Marxist (political economy)**: culture is shaped by economic ownership; global media conglomerates spread capitalist ideology for profit; leads to cultural imperialism theory (Schiller).
- **Gramscian (cultural studies)**: culture is a site where **hegemony** (consent) is built and contested; global media spread a consumerist common sense, but local groups negotiate and resist; audiences decode differently (Hall).
- **Cultural industries** (2018 note): Adorno and Horkheimer's "culture industry" (1944) — standardised mass culture; later "cultural industries" (plural; Bernard Miège, UNESCO 1980s; David Hesmondhalgh, *The Cultural Industries*, 2002) recognises diverse industries (film, music, publishing, broadcasting, games) producing symbolic goods, with complex, risky, creative work. Pakistan: drama industry, music (Coke Studio), film revival (*The Legend of Maula Jatt*, 2022, highest-grossing Pakistani film [verify]).
