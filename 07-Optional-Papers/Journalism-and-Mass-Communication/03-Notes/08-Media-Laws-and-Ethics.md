## 8.1 Constitutional framework

- **Article 19 (freedom of speech and press)**: "Every citizen shall have the right to freedom of speech and expression, and there shall be freedom of the press", subject to reasonable restrictions imposed by law in the interest of: the **glory of Islam**; the **integrity, security or defence of Pakistan** or any part of it; **friendly relations with foreign states**; **public order, decency or morality**; or in relation to **contempt of court**, **commission of or incitement to an offence**.
- **Article 19-A (right to information)**: added by the 18th Amendment (2010): every citizen has the right to access information in all matters of public importance, subject to regulation and reasonable restrictions imposed by law.
- Related: Article 9 (life and liberty), 14 (dignity and privacy of home), 10-A (fair trial — relevant to "media trials"), 204 (contempt of court), 63(1)(g) (disqualification for propagating opinions against the judiciary, armed forces, ideology [check current text]).
- International: UDHR Art. 19; ICCPR Art. 19 (ratified 2010); UN Human Rights Committee General Comment 34 (2011) — restrictions must be provided by law, necessary and proportionate.

## 8.2 Development of media regulation: colonial era to independent Pakistan (2019)

### Colonial era (British India)

| Year | Law | Key point |
|---|---|---|
| 1780 | *Hicky's Bengal Gazette* (first newspaper in India) | Closed in 1782 after criticising the East India Company |
| 1799 | **Censorship of the Press Act** (Lord Wellesley) | Pre-censorship during war with France |
| 1823 | **Licensing Regulations** (John Adams) | Licence required to start a press; aimed at Indian-language papers |
| 1835 | **Press Act (Metcalfe Act)** | Repealed 1823 regulations; Charles Metcalfe called "liberator of the Indian press"; required only a declaration |
| 1857 | **Licensing Act ("Gagging Act")** (Lord Canning) | Reimposed licensing after the War of Independence |
| 1860 | **Indian Penal Code** | Defamation (ss. 499–502); sedition (s. 124A added 1870) |
| 1867 | **Press and Registration of Books Act** | Registration of presses and newspapers; printer/publisher declaration |
| 1878 | **Vernacular Press Act** (Lord Lytton) | Targeted Indian-language papers; repealed 1882 (Lord Ripon) |
| 1885 | Indian Telegraph Act | State control of telegraph (later radio) |
| 1908 | Newspapers (Incitement to Offences) Act | Seize presses publishing "incitement" |
| 1910 | **Indian Press Act** | Security deposits; forfeiture — used heavily against Muslim papers (Comrade, Zamindar, Al-Hilal) |
| 1923 | **Official Secrets Act** | Still in force in Pakistan (amended 2023) |
| 1931 | Indian Press (Emergency Powers) Act | Wide powers against civil disobedience coverage |

**Pattern**: licensing, security deposits, forfeiture, pre-censorship, sedition — tools of authoritarian control, later inherited by Pakistan.

### Effect across regimes (2026)

- **Military regimes** (Ayub, Zia, Musharraf's later years) used ordinances, censorship and takeovers, but Musharraf also liberalised broadcasting.
- **Civilian governments** promised freedom (RPPPO 1988, RTI laws 2017, journalists' protection 2021) but used PEMRA, PECA, defamation and advertising to pressure critics.
- **Constant tools**: licensing, ads, "national security" and religion-based restrictions; **new tools**: cybercrime law, platform blocking, social media regulation.
- **Conclusion**: Pakistan's media laws have moved from controlling **presses** (colonial and PPO era) to controlling **broadcast licences** (PEMRA) to controlling **online speech and platforms** (PECA 2016/2025) — the form changes, the logic of control remains, while courts and journalists' unions have repeatedly pushed back.

## 8.3 Libel, defamation and the PPC (2016, 2021)

- **Defamation**: a false statement, communicated to a third party, that harms a person's reputation.
- **Libel**: defamation in permanent form (writing, print, broadcast, online); **slander**: transient form (speech, gesture).
- **Elements**: statement is defamatory; refers to the plaintiff; published to others; (false); damage (presumed for libel).
- **Defences**: **truth/justification** in the public interest; **fair comment** on matters of public interest (honest opinion); **privilege** (absolute: parliamentary and court proceedings; qualified: fair and accurate reports); **consent**; apology and correction (mitigation).

### PPC provisions (criminal defamation)

| Section | Content |
|---|---|
| 499 | Defines defamation: making or publishing any imputation concerning a person intending to harm, or knowing it will harm, their reputation; **ten exceptions** (e.g. imputation of truth for the public good, public conduct of public servants, reports of court proceedings, opinions on merits of cases, censure by a person in lawful authority, accusations made in good faith to authorities, caution in good faith) |
| 500 | Punishment: simple imprisonment up to two years, or fine, or both |
| 501 | Printing or engraving matter known to be defamatory |
| 502 | Sale of printed matter containing defamatory matter |
| 124A | Sedition (struck down by LHC, 2023 [verify]) |
| 153A | Promoting enmity between groups |
| 295-A to 295-C, 298 | Offences relating to religion (blasphemy) |
| 505 | Statements conducing to public mischief |
| 509 | Insulting the modesty of a woman / harassment |

### Defamation Ordinance 2002 (civil)

- Covers **libel and slander**; defamation includes false statements or representations that injure reputation, tend to lower a person in the estimation of others, or expose them to ridicule, hatred or contempt.
- **Defences**: truth and public good; fair comment; privilege; innocent dissemination; consent; offer of apology and correction.
- **Remedies**: apology, publication of correction, **general damages** (with a minimum amount set in the Ordinance) and special damages [verify the current minimum figure].
- **Procedure**: notice within a short period after publication or knowledge; suit within a set limitation period; district courts; expected decision in a fixed time (90 days) [verify time limits].
- **Criticism**: slow courts, rarely used effectively; criminal defamation (PPC) and PECA s. 20 remain, so journalists face jail as well as damages.

### Punjab Defamation Act 2024

Special defamation tribunals; claims against holders of constitutional offices heard by High Court benches; interim damages of up to Rs 3 million before final decision; covers social media. Journalists' bodies called it a gag law. LHC suspended key sections (June 2024); tribunals in Lahore, Multan and Rawalpindi notified (Sept 2025); petitions referred to the Chief Justice (April 2026), still pending.

### PECA s. 20 (online defamation)

Offences against the dignity of a natural person (false information that harms reputation or privacy) — up to three years' imprisonment and/or fine [verify after 2022 ruling]. Used widely against journalists and social media users.

## 8.4 PPO, RPPPO and press registration

| Law | Year | Main features | Assessment |
|---|---|---|---|
| **PPO** | 1963 (West Pakistan 1960) | Declaration by printer and publisher before a District Magistrate; security deposits; government could demand deposits, forfeit them, seize presses, ban papers; compulsory publication of government press notes; restrictions on content (e.g. anything "likely to bring into hatred" the government) | Widely called a "black law"; tool of Ayub, Bhutto and Zia; repealed 1988 |

## 8.5 PEMRA: establishment, development and operational mechanisms (2016, 2018, 2023, 2026)

### Establishment

- **Pakistan Electronic Media Regulatory Authority Ordinance 2002** (promulgated 1 March 2002 by General Musharraf); later amended and validated (PEMRA (Amendment) Act 2007 [verify]).
- **Objectives (s. 4)**: improve the standards of information, education and entertainment; enlarge the choice available to the people in media for news, current affairs, religious knowledge, art and culture, science and technology, economic development, social sector concerns, music, sports, drama and other subjects of public and national interest; facilitate devolution of responsibility and power to the grassroots by improving access to mass media at the local and community level; ensure accountability, transparency and good governance by optimising the free flow of information.

### Composition

- **Chairman** plus members (12, raised to **13** by the 2023 amendment), including ex officio members (Secretary Information, Secretary Interior, Chairman PTA) and members from civil society, media and law, with provincial representation [verify exact composition].
- **Appointment of chairman**: under an amendment to the PEMRA Act, a four-member parliamentary committee (two from the National Assembly and two from the Senate, treasury and opposition) recommends a candidate for appointment (Dawn, Feb 2026). The post was vacant from Oct 2025; a candidate (former information secretary Ambreen Jan) was recommended but awaited approval (Feb 2026) [check current chair].

### Operational mechanisms

| Mechanism | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Licensing** | Satellite TV, FM radio, cable TV (distribution), landing rights for foreign channels, IPTV, mobile TV, DTH. Licences through bidding/approval; conditions on content, local programming, ownership. As of 31 March 2026: **142 satellite TV, 240 FM (172 commercial, 68 non-commercial), 2,951 cable TV licences, 28 landing rights** (Economic Survey 2025–26) |
| **Code of Conduct** | **Electronic Media (Programmes and Advertisements) Code of Conduct 2015** (after Supreme Court directions): content not against Islamic values, ideology of Pakistan, integrity, defence and security; no hate speech, sectarianism, glorification of terrorists; no live coverage of hostage situations or security operations; protect victims' identity (especially children and rape survivors); accuracy and right of reply; delay mechanism for live programmes; in-house **editorial committees** and **monitoring committees**; advertisements standards |
| **Councils of Complaints** | In Islamabad and provincial capitals; hear public complaints against licensees and recommend action (censure, fine, suspension). Since 2023, also hear media workers' complaints on unpaid salaries and minimum wages |
| **Monitoring** | Central monitoring centre recording channels 24/7 |
| **Enforcement** | Show-cause notices, warnings, fines (maximum raised to Rs 10 million by the 2023 amendment [as reported]), suspension of programmes, prohibition of broadcast (s. 27), suspension or revocation of licences |
| **Appeals** | Aggrieved licensees can appeal to the High Court (s. 30A) [verify] |
| **Directives** | Advisories and bans (e.g. Feb 2023 and **13 Oct 2026**: no live coverage of attacks and "unpleasant incidents"; only officially verified information; time delays) |

### Development (milestones)

2002 Ordinance → 2007 emergency amendments → 2007 Act → 2009–2013 Supreme Court cases on media standards → 2013 Media Commission recommends independence → 2015 Code of Conduct → 2018–2022 regular bans and suspensions → **2023 amendment** (definitions of **misinformation** and **disinformation**; salary protection within two months linked to government ads; workers' complaints; 13 members; higher fines) → 2025 amendment bill (after the Supreme Court's Mustafa Impex judgment on "federal government" meaning cabinet) in committee → 2026 leadership vacuum and live-coverage directive.

### Critical evaluation

| Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|
| Opened broadcasting to private sector; huge growth in channels and FM | Not independent: government appoints leadership and can issue policy directives |
| Code of conduct protects victims, children, minorities | Selective, politically motivated bans (speeches of opposition leaders; channel suspensions) |
| Councils of Complaints give the public a forum | Councils slow, little public awareness |
| 2023 salary protection for workers | Vague definitions ("disinformation", "against national interest") enable censorship |
| Monitoring capacity | Cable operators used to black out channels outside formal process |
| Licensing for local and community stations possible | No real community radio framework; weak on digital |

**Reforms**: statutory independence (transparent, parliamentary appointment with security of tenure); narrowly defined offences; proportionate, reasoned decisions with appeal; public hearings; separate content regulation from licensing; co-regulation with a broadcasters' self-regulatory body; transparency reports.

## 8.6 Press Council of Pakistan (PCP)

- **Press Council of Pakistan Ordinance 2002**: an autonomous, quasi-judicial body for print media. The Council was actually constituted years later [verify year — about 2007–08].
- **Composition**: chairman (a retired judge qualified to be a Supreme Court judge, or an eminent person), and members nominated by **APNS**, **CPNE**, **PFUJ**, the National Assembly (government and opposition), HRCP and others [verify].
- **Functions**: implement the **Ethical Code of Practice** (schedule to the Ordinance); hear complaints against newspapers and journalists; protect press freedom; promote high professional standards; review developments likely to restrict free flow of information.
- **Ethical Code of Practice** (main points): accuracy and fairness; distinguish comment and fact; correct errors promptly; give right of reply; respect privacy; protect confidential sources; avoid incitement to violence, hatred or sectarianism; do not glorify crime; avoid obscenity; no plagiarism; protect identities of victims of sexual offences and minors; no payment to criminals for stories; avoid conflicts of interest.
- **Weaknesses**: limited powers (can only direct publication of a correction or censure); owners' and unions' boycotts at times; little public awareness; slow; under-funded; seen as government-linked.

## 8.7 Citizens Media Commission: need, present status, reasons for inactivity

- **Concept**: an independent, citizen-led body to monitor media content and performance on behalf of the public, receive complaints, issue reports, and promote media accountability without state censorship — complementary to PEMRA's Councils of Complaints and the PCP. The idea grew from civil society after the 2007 emergency and the 2013 Supreme Court Media Commission's call for public-interest oversight.
- **Need**: PEMRA is government-dominated; PCP is weak; owners' bodies protect members; the public (viewers, minorities, women, children) has no strong voice; growing concerns about sensationalism, hate speech and fake news; a citizen body could provide credible, non-state accountability.
- **Present status**: I could not find evidence of an active, functioning Citizens Media Commission in Pakistan as of Nov 2026 [verify]. PEMRA's Councils of Complaints (which include citizens) are the nearest existing mechanism.
- **Reasons for inactivity**: no legal mandate or powers; lack of funding and independence; resistance from owners and sometimes journalists who fear another regulator; government preference for direct control through PEMRA and PECA; low public awareness and civic engagement; fragmented civil society; overlapping bodies (PEMRA councils, PCP, ombudsmen).
- **Way forward**: statutory recognition with clear advisory and reporting powers; diverse membership (academics, consumer bodies, minorities, women); funding from a neutral source; transparency reports; links with media literacy programmes.

## 8.8 Press code of ethics (2017, 2022, 2023)

### Core principles of journalism ethics

1. **Truth and accuracy** (verify facts, sources, images).
2. **Independence** (avoid conflicts of interest, gifts, political ties).
3. **Fairness and impartiality** (balance, right of reply, separate news and opinion).
4. **Humanity / minimising harm** (privacy, dignity, sensitivity with victims and children).
5. **Accountability** (correct errors, explain decisions).

### International codes

- **SPJ Code of Ethics** (US Society of Professional Journalists; first 1926, revised 1996 and 2014): seek truth and report it; minimise harm; act independently; be accountable and transparent.
- **IFJ Global Charter of Ethics for Journalists** (Tunis, 2019; replaced the 1954 Bordeaux Declaration).
- **UNESCO International Principles of Professional Ethics in Journalism** (1983).
- BBC Editorial Guidelines; Reuters Trust Principles.

### Pakistani codes

PCP Ethical Code of Practice (2002); PEMRA Code of Conduct (2015); CPNE and APNS codes; PFUJ's code (journalists' charter) [verify]; individual newsroom style books (e.g. Dawn's).

### Code of ethics for media professionals (2023 answer frame)

Accuracy; verification; attribution and sourcing; fairness and right of reply; privacy; protection of sources; no plagiarism; avoidance of conflicts of interest and "lifafa"; sensitivity in reporting terrorism (no glorification, no live operations), sectarian issues, blasphemy allegations, gender-based violence (no victim identification), children, mental health and suicide (WHO guidelines); corrections policy; separation of advertising and editorial (label advertorials); respect for court proceedings; ethical use of AI and images.

## 8.9 Why the media cannot develop an institutional code / self-regulate (2017, 2018)

**Reasons**:
1. **Commercial competition**: ratings and advertising reward sensationalism; no channel wants to disarm alone.
2. **Owner dominance**: owners' business and political interests override editorial ethics; journalists lack job security to resist.
3. **Fragmented and polarised industry**: APNS, CPNE, PBA and PFUJ factions rarely agree; rival groups in open conflict (e.g. channel wars in 2014).
4. **Weak professional training** and low pay (leading to "lifafa journalism").
5. **No independent ombudsman** with power and public trust.
6. **Government co-option and pressure**: self-regulation is pre-empted by state regulation (PEMRA, PECA), and the media fear any code will be used against them.
7. **Political polarisation**: ethics seen through partisan lenses.
8. **Rapid growth after 2002** without institutional maturity; digital journalists outside any framework.

**"Quest for freedom and inability to self-regulate"**: the media demand freedom from the state but do not regulate themselves, which gives governments the excuse to regulate (the 2018 question's "national security" argument). Counter-argument: Pakistan's media have built some mechanisms (PCP, in-house committees under PEMRA code, journalists' unions' statements), and the state's record of misusing laws justifies caution.

**Solutions**: a broadcasters' independent complaints commission funded by industry but with independent members (like the UK's IPSO or Ofcom co-regulation model); newsroom ombudsmen; binding codes in employment contracts; training; transparency in ownership; public complaints; media literacy.

### National security vs freedom of expression (2018)

- *Case for regulation*: terrorism coverage, leaks of security information, incitement, foreign propaganda, hybrid warfare.
- *Case against overreach*: vague "national interest" clauses silence legitimate criticism; Article 19 restrictions must be narrow, necessary and proportionate (Johannesburg Principles, 1995, on national security and freedom of expression; Tshwane Principles, 2013); courts should review.
- *Laws in question*: PEMRA ss. on prohibition of broadcasts; PECA s. 37 (PTA blocking powers) and 2025 s. 26A; Official Secrets (Amendment) Act 2023; Anti-Terrorism Act 1997 (s. 11W on publication of material inciting hatred) [verify section].
- *Balanced conclusion*: protect genuine security through specific, court-supervised rules; avoid blanket bans that erode credibility and push audiences to rumours.

## 8.10 PECA 2016 and the 2025 amendments (2020; current)

### PECA 2016 — salient features

| Section | Offence / power |
|---|---|
| 3–8 | Unauthorised access to and interference with information systems and data; critical infrastructure |
| 9 | Glorification of an offence (terrorism) |
| 10 | Cyber terrorism |
| 11 | Hate speech |
| 13–14 | Electronic forgery and fraud |
| 16 | Unauthorised use of identity information |
| 20 | Offences against the dignity of a natural person (online defamation) |
| 21 | Offences against the modesty of a natural person and minors (sexual images) |
| 22 | Child pornography |
| 24 | Cyberstalking |
| 37 | **PTA's power to remove or block unlawful online content** (on grounds mirroring Article 19 restrictions); Removal and Blocking of Unlawful Online Content Rules 2021 |
| Investigation | FIA Cyber Crime Wing (replaced by the **National Cyber Crime Investigation Agency**, NCCIA, under the 2025 amendments and notifications) |

**Effect on freedom of expression (2020)**: vague terms ("dignity", "glory of Islam", "integrity"); broad PTA blocking powers without judicial oversight; criminal defamation; used against journalists, activists and opposition social media users; chilling effect; but also protects women and children against online harassment and abuse — present both sides.

### PECA (Amendment) Act 2025 (signed January 2025)

- **Section 26A**: intentionally spreading **false or fake information** likely to cause fear, panic, disorder or unrest — up to **three years' imprisonment**, fine up to **Rs 2 million**, or both; reported as cognisable and non-bailable.
- **Social Media Protection and Regulatory Authority (SMPRA)**: registers social media platforms, sets standards, orders removal/blocking of unlawful content, can take action against platforms.
- **Social Media Complaint Council** and **Social Media Protection Tribunals** (appeals to the Supreme Court within 60 days, as reported).
- **National Cyber Crime Investigation Agency** created.
- Wider definition of "social media platform" (including tools used to access platforms).
- **Reaction**: PFUJ, HRCP, Amnesty International and RSF condemned it as a gag law; nationwide journalist protests (Jan 2025); petitions in the Islamabad High Court (IHC observed in Feb 2026 that the amendment could not be suspended by injunction; petitions pending); RSF reported multiple journalists and YouTube channels targeted under PECA within a year; the **National Commission for Human Rights** published a critical report on PECA and the 2025 amendments (Feb 2026).

## 8.11 Ethics in the digital age (2024, 2026)

**Challenges**:
1. **Speed vs accuracy**: pressure to post first; viral errors.
2. **Verification** of user-generated content, images and videos; **deepfakes** and AI-generated content.
3. **Fake news and disinformation** campaigns; journalists sharing unverified claims.
4. **Clickbait** and metric-driven journalism.
5. **Privacy**: publishing personal data, doxxing, images of victims.
6. **Plagiarism** and content theft.
7. **Conflicts of interest**: sponsored content, influencer journalism, undisclosed paid partnerships, YouTube monetisation.
8. **Partisanship** and opinion presented as news.
9. **Online harassment** of journalists; safety.
10. **Use of AI** in newsrooms: transparency, bias, errors.
11. **Algorithmic dependence**: platforms decide reach.
12. **Source protection** in a surveillance era.

**Effect on credibility in Pakistan (2026)**: declining trust; audiences shift to partisan YouTubers; PECA cases justified by "fake news"; professional journalists blamed for social media rumours.

**Solutions**: verification desks and fact-check units (e.g. AFP Fact Check, Soch Fact Check, Geo Fact Check [verify names]); digital ethics codes (PCP and PEMRA updates); clear correction policies; labelling opinion, sponsored content and AI use; training in digital verification (reverse image search, metadata); safety protocols; collaboration with platforms; media literacy campaigns; self-regulation for digital-only outlets; narrow, rights-compatible laws.
