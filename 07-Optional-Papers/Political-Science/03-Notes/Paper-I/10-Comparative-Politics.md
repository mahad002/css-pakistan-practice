# 10. Comparative Politics: Socialisation, Political Culture, Development, Recruitment, Social Change, Civil Society, Violence and Terrorism, Gender and Politics, Women's Empowerment

Asked: political culture 2022 (note: forms), 2026 (levels of culture and modes of participation); women's empowerment 2016, 2025 (notes); civil society 2016 (note); middle class and democracy 2018 (note). This section is where **Pakistan examples** earn marks.

**What comparative politics is.** The systematic comparison of political systems to find patterns and explanations (CSS 2026 MCQ). Approaches: traditional/institutional (constitutions, legal), behavioural (1950s–60s: individuals, culture), **structural-functional** (Almond and Powell: every system performs the same functions through different structures), **systems theory** (David Easton), political economy, new institutionalism, rational choice.

**David Easton's systems model** (*A Systems Analysis of Political Life*, 1965). *Diagram in words*: a box labelled "Political system" sits inside a wider "Environment". Arrows enter from the left labelled **inputs**: **demands** and **supports**. Arrows leave on the right labelled **outputs**: **decisions and policies**. A loop at the bottom labelled **feedback** carries the effect of outputs back to inputs. **Gatekeepers** (parties, interest groups, media) filter demands before they reach the system (CSS 2026 MCQ). Easton defined politics as "the authoritative allocation of values for a society".

**Almond's functions**: input functions (political socialisation and recruitment, interest articulation, interest aggregation, political communication) and output functions (rule-making, rule application, rule adjudication).

## 1. POLITICAL SOCIALISATION

**Definition**: the process by which people acquire political values, beliefs, attitudes and knowledge, and by which political culture is passed from one generation to the next. (CSS 2026 MCQ: "the purpose of society is to pass its culture on to the next generation" — framed around Ibn Khaldun in that paper.)

**Agents**

| Agent | Role | Pakistan example |
|---|---|---|
| Family | First and most lasting: party loyalty, religion, attitudes to authority | Party loyalty passed down in biradari and feudal families |
| School and curriculum | Civic knowledge, national identity, ideology | Single National Curriculum (2020–21) debates; Pakistan Studies textbooks |
| Peer groups | Reinforce or challenge family views | University student groups |
| Religious institutions | Values, moral authority, mobilisation | Madrassas (over 30,000 [verify]); Friday sermons |
| Mass media and social media | News, framing, agenda-setting; now the strongest for youth | PTI's social media dominance; TikTok and YouTube political content; X ban (2024–25) |
| Political parties | Mobilise and educate supporters | Party rallies, youth wings |
| Workplace, unions, army | Discipline, interests | Military as a socialising institution for officers |
| Political events | Wars, crises, charismatic leaders | 1971, 9/11, May 2025 India–Pakistan conflict |

**Types**: direct (explicit political teaching) and indirect (attitudes to authority learned in family or school); primary (childhood) and secondary (adult).

**Significance**: stability and legitimacy of the system; continuity of culture; but also change (new generations, new media).

## 2. POLITICAL CULTURE (2022, 2026)

### 2.1 Definition
"The pattern of individual attitudes and orientations towards politics among the members of a political system" (Almond and Verba). Sidney Verba: "the system of empirical beliefs, expressive symbols and values which defines the situation in which political action takes place."

### 2.2 Almond and Verba, *The Civic Culture* (1963)
Survey of five countries: **USA, UK, West Germany, Italy, Mexico**.

**Orientations**: cognitive (knowledge), affective (feelings), evaluative (judgements) towards the system, its inputs, its outputs and the self as participant.

**Three pure types**

| Type | Awareness of system | Awareness of inputs (participation) | Self as participant | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Parochial** | Low | Low | None | Traditional tribal and remote rural communities |
| **Subject** | High (of outputs) | Low | Passive; obey | Authoritarian states; colonial subjects; Germany and Italy in their data (1959) |
| **Participant** | High | High | Active | USA, UK in their data |

**Civic culture**: a **mixed** culture in which participant attitudes are balanced by subject and parochial ones: citizens are active enough to hold government accountable but trusting and passive enough to allow it to govern. They found it closest in the **UK** and **USA**; **Mexico** had aspirations without experience; **Italy** was alienated.

**Criticism**: US/UK-centred and ethnocentric; static; ignores class, elites and power; culture may be a result, not a cause, of institutions; survey-based snapshots. *The Civic Culture Revisited* (1980) noted declining trust in the US and UK.

### 2.3 Other classifications (useful for "forms of political culture")
- **Homogeneous vs fragmented** (Almond): shared values (UK, Japan) vs subcultures in conflict (Lebanon, Pakistan's ethnic and sectarian divides).
- **Elite vs mass culture**: rulers' values may differ from citizens' (Pakistan: an elite consensus on security vs mass concern for economy).
- **Secular vs traditional**; **consensual vs conflictual**; **democratic vs authoritarian**.
- **Inglehart's** post-materialism (*The Silent Revolution*, 1977): rich societies shift from material to post-material values (environment, self-expression); World Values Survey cultural map.
- **Putnam**, *Making Democracy Work* (1993): Italian regions with dense civic networks (social capital) governed better.

### 2.4 Levels of political culture (2026)
1. **System level**: attitudes to the nation, regime and constitution (legitimacy, national identity).
2. **Process level**: attitudes to one's own role and to inputs (participation, parties, elections, interest groups).
3. **Policy level**: expectations of government outputs (welfare, security, economy).

### 2.5 Political culture of Pakistan
Features:
- **Mixed parochial–subject culture** with a growing participant element (urban youth, social media, the 2024 election turnout of about 47–48% [verify]).
- **Fragmented**: ethnic (Punjabi, Sindhi, Pashtun, Baloch, Mohajir), sectarian, rural–urban.
- **Personality and charisma** dominate over programmes (Bhutto, Imran Khan, Sharifs).
- **Biradari, tribe and patronage** shape voting; "electables".
- **Dynastic politics** (Bhuttos, Sharifs, Chaudhrys).
- **Low trust** in institutions; belief in conspiracy; alienation in Balochistan and parts of KP.
- **Praetorian tradition**: acceptance of military intervention by sections of society; "hybrid" governance.
- **Religion** as a source of legitimacy and mobilisation.
- **Confrontational** style: losers reject results (every election since 1977 disputed).
- **Weak tolerance of dissent**; politics of agitation (dharnas, long marches).
- Changing: urbanisation, youth bulge (about two-thirds under 30), digital media, middle class.

## 3. POLITICAL DEVELOPMENT

### 3.1 Meaning
The growth of a political system's capacity, institutions and participation. Lucian Pye listed ten meanings (e.g. political prerequisite of economic development; politics typical of industrial societies; political modernisation; nation-state building; administrative and legal development; mass mobilisation and participation; democracy building; stability and orderly change; mobilisation and power; one aspect of multi-dimensional social change).

### 3.2 Pye's "development syndrome"
Three features: **equality** (participation, universal laws, merit recruitment), **capacity** (effectiveness and efficiency of government), **differentiation and specialisation** (of structures and roles).

### 3.3 Crises of development (Binder, Pye et al., *Crises and Sequences in Political Development*, 1971)
**Identity** (who are we?), **legitimacy** (is authority rightful?), **penetration** (can the government reach the whole territory?), **participation** (who can take part?), **distribution** (who gets resources?). Countries that face all at once (Pakistan in 1947–71) struggle most. Pakistan's 1971 break-up is a classic case of identity, participation and distribution crises together.

### 3.4 Samuel Huntington, *Political Order in Changing Societies* (1968)
- Rapid social mobilisation and rising participation without strong **institutions** produce instability and **political decay**.
- Institutionalisation measured by **adaptability, complexity, autonomy and coherence**.
- **Praetorian societies**: all groups (army, students, clergy, workers) act directly in politics; the military intervenes.
- "The primary problem of politics is the lag in the development of political institutions behind social and economic change."

### 3.5 Modernisation theory and its critics
- **Lipset** (1959): wealth, education and a middle class support democracy. **Rostow**: stages of growth.
- **Dependency theory** (Frank, Cardoso): underdevelopment results from exploitation by the core.
- **Barrington Moore** (*Social Origins of Dictatorship and Democracy*, 1966): "no bourgeoisie, no democracy".

### 3.6 Middle class and democracy (2018 note)
The middle class demands accountability, education, rule of law and property rights; is less dependent on patrons; forms civil society; votes on issues. Aristotle's polity, Lipset and Moore. In Pakistan: a growing urban middle class backed the Lawyers' Movement (2007–09) and PTI; but much of it is tied to the state (bureaucracy, army) and sometimes supports authoritarian "clean-up" rule. Conclusion: a necessary but not sufficient condition.

## 4. POLITICAL RECRUITMENT

**Definition**: the process by which individuals are selected for political roles (legislators, ministers, officials, party leaders). Almond lists it as an input function.

**Methods**: heredity (monarchy, dynasties); election; appointment; competitive examination (civil service: CSS); lot (ancient Athens); co-option; seizure of power; party nomination.

**Factors**: education, wealth, social status, ethnicity, gender, party loyalty, charisma, patronage.

**Political parties as recruiters** (CSS 2025: parties as "engines of social change and political recruitment"): they identify, train and nominate leaders; provide ladders from local to national office; balance groups in tickets.

**Pakistan**: recruitment through dynasties and "**electables**" (landed and tribal notables who win regardless of party); party tickets bought or awarded for loyalty; few routes for the middle class and women (Elections Act 2017 requires 5% of general-seat tickets to go to women); civil service through FPSC; military through ISSB.

## 5. SOCIAL CHANGE

**Definition**: significant change in social structures, institutions, values and behaviour over time.

**Factors**: technology (printing, industrialisation, internet); economy (capitalism, globalisation); demography (population growth, urbanisation, migration); ideas and ideology (religion, nationalism, feminism); environment (climate change); conflict and war; leaders and movements; education.

**Theories**: evolutionary (Comte, Spencer); conflict (Marx); cyclical (Ibn Khaldun, Spengler, Toynbee, Sorokin); functionalist (Parsons: equilibrium); modernisation.

**Relation to politics**: social change creates new groups and demands (Huntington); politics directs or resists change (land reform, women's rights laws); revolutions are rapid political-social change.

**Pakistan**: urbanisation (about 39% urban in Census 2023), youth bulge, media revolution, remittance economy, rising female education, climate displacement (2022 floods), shifting religious attitudes.

## 6. CIVIL SOCIETY (2016 note)

### 6.1 Definition
The sphere of voluntary associations between the family, the market and the state: NGOs, community groups, professional associations, trade unions, religious bodies, media, think-tanks, charities, student groups.

### 6.2 Thinkers
- **Hegel**: civil society as the "system of needs" between family and state.
- **Tocqueville** (*Democracy in America*, 1835–40): Americans' habit of forming associations sustains democracy.
- **Gramsci**: civil society as the terrain of hegemony and counter-hegemony.
- **Robert Putnam**: **social capital**, the networks, norms and trust that make cooperation possible (*Making Democracy Work*, 1993; *Bowling Alone*, 2000: decline of US civic life).
- **Global civil society**: see Paper II, file 05.

### 6.3 Functions
Check state power; voice for citizens; aggregate interests; deliver services; civic education; build trust and social capital; monitor elections and rights; bridge divisions.

### 6.4 Limits
Can be elite-dominated and donor-driven; can be "uncivil" (extremist groups); can weaken parties; may be co-opted by the state.

### 6.5 Pakistan
- Strengths: **Edhi Foundation**, Shaukat Khanum, The Citizens Foundation, Akhuwat (interest-free microfinance); HRCP; Aurat March; the **Lawyers' Movement (2007–09)** that restored the Chief Justice; FAFEN election monitoring; flood relief.
- Constraints: INGO registration policy (2015) and later restrictions; security pressures on journalists and activists; terrorist threats; dependence on foreign funding; "uncivil" groups (militant and sectarian organisations).

## 7. VIOLENCE AND TERRORISM IN POLITICS

### 7.1 Political violence
Types: state violence (repression, war), revolutionary violence, insurgency, terrorism, riots and communal violence, assassination, coups. **Ted Gurr**, *Why Men Rebel* (1970): **relative deprivation** (the gap between expectations and achievements) causes political violence. **Frantz Fanon**: violence as liberation for the colonised. **Hannah Arendt** (*On Violence*, 1970): violence and power are opposites; violence appears where power is lost.

### 7.2 Terrorism
- **Definition**: no universally agreed UN definition (a draft Comprehensive Convention has been stalled since 1996 over freedom-fighters and state terrorism). Common elements: violence or threat against civilians/non-combatants, to create fear, for political, religious or ideological aims.
- **Types**: nationalist-separatist, religious, left-wing, right-wing, state-sponsored, cyber-terrorism, lone-wolf.
- **Causes**: political grievances, occupation, state repression, poverty and unemployment, identity and ideology, radicalisation, foreign intervention, weak state capacity, social media.
- **"Waves" of modern terrorism** (David Rapoport): anarchist (1880s), anti-colonial (1920s), new left (1960s), religious (1979–).
- **Counter-terrorism**: hard (military, policing, intelligence) and soft (deradicalisation, education, development, counter-narrative); UN Global Counter-Terrorism Strategy (2006); UNSC Resolution 1373 (2001); FATF (Pakistan on the grey list 2018–2022).

### 7.3 Pakistan
- **Global Terrorism Index 2026: Pakistan ranked 1st** (1,139 deaths in 2025; TTP responsible for 56%) — see the Current Affairs facts sheet.
- Main actors: TTP (resurgent since the Taliban took Kabul in August 2021), Baloch separatist groups (BLA; the Jaffar Express hijacking of March 2025 [verify]), ISKP, sectarian outfits.
- Responses: military operations (Rah-e-Rast 2009, Zarb-e-Azb 2014, Radd-ul-Fasaad 2017, Azm-e-Istehkam 2024); **National Action Plan** (2014, after the Army Public School attack, 16 December 2014); military courts (2015–19); NACTA; Paigham-e-Pakistan fatwa (2018).

## 8. GENDER AND POLITICS

### 8.1 Concepts
- **Sex** (biological) vs **gender** (socially constructed roles).
- **Patriarchy**: male-dominated structures in family, economy and politics.
- **Public/private divide**: feminists argue "the personal is political" (Carol Hanisch, 1970).
- **Feminist waves**: first (suffrage: New Zealand 1893 first national women's vote; UK 1918/1928; US 1920); second (1960s–80s: equality at work, reproductive rights; Simone de Beauvoir, Betty Friedan); third (1990s: diversity, intersectionality—Kimberlé Crenshaw, 1989); fourth (2010s: digital, #MeToo).
- **Strands**: liberal, radical, Marxist/socialist, postcolonial, Islamic feminism.

### 8.2 Women in politics: global
- Women held about 27% of seats in national parliaments worldwide in 2025 [verify; IPU]. Rwanda has the highest share (over 60%).
- **Gender quotas**: reserved seats, legal candidate quotas, voluntary party quotas.
- **Beijing Declaration and Platform for Action** (1995): 12 critical areas; **gender mainstreaming**.
- **CEDAW** (1979): "international bill of rights for women".
- **UNSC Resolution 1325** (2000): women, peace and security.
- **SDG 5**: gender equality.

### 8.3 Pakistan
- **Fatima Jinnah** challenged Ayub Khan (1965 presidential election). **Benazir Bhutto**: first woman prime minister of a Muslim-majority country (1988). **Maryam Nawaz**: first woman chief minister of Punjab (February 2024). Fehmida Mirza: first woman Speaker of the National Assembly (2008).
- **Reserved seats**: 60 of 336 in the National Assembly; 17 in the Senate; 22% in provincial assemblies (by party-list PR) [verify Senate figure: 17 women's seats in a 96-member Senate].
- **Elections Act 2017**: parties must give at least **5%** of general-seat tickets to women; results void if women's turnout in a constituency is below **10%**.
- **Gender gap**: the electoral rolls had about 10 million fewer women than men before NADRA–ECP drives [verify]; WEF Global Gender Gap Report 2026: **143rd of 145**.

## 9. WOMEN'S EMPOWERMENT (2016, 2025 notes)

### 9.1 Meaning
The process by which women gain power and control over their own lives: the ability to make strategic choices (Naila Kabeer: **resources**, **agency** and **achievements**).

### 9.2 Dimensions
Political (vote, office, leadership); economic (work, income, property, credit); social (education, health, mobility, freedom from violence); legal (equal rights and access to justice); psychological (self-confidence).

### 9.3 Islamic perspective
Spiritual equality (33:35); right to property and inheritance (4:7), to earn (4:32), to consent in marriage, to education ("seeking knowledge is an obligation on every Muslim"); examples: Khadija (businesswoman), Aisha (scholar), Umm Salama (counsel at Hudaybiyyah), al-Shifa bint Abdullah (market inspector under Umar). Many obstacles in Muslim societies stem from custom, not religion.

### 9.4 Pakistan: laws and institutions
| Measure | Year |
|---|---|
| Muslim Family Laws Ordinance | 1961 |
| Protection of Women (Criminal Laws Amendment) Act (reformed Hudood rape provisions) | 2006 |
| Protection against Harassment of Women at the Workplace Act (amended 2022) | 2010 |
| Acid Control and Acid Crime Prevention Act | 2011 |
| Prevention of Anti-Women Practices Act | 2011 |
| Anti-honour killing law (Criminal Law Amendment, closing the pardon loophole for murder) | 2016 |
| Punjab Protection of Women against Violence Act | 2016 |
| Anti-Rape (Investigation and Trial) Act | 2021 |
| Enforcement of Women's Property Rights Act | 2020 |
| National Commission on the Status of Women | 2000 (statutory 2012) |
| **Benazir Income Support Programme** (cash to women) | 2008 |

### 9.5 Obstacles
Patriarchal customs (honour, *vani*/*swara*, karo-kari); low female literacy (about 54% in FY26 vs 63% overall); low labour-force participation (about 21–25% [verify]); violence; weak enforcement; limited mobility; under-representation in decision-making; climate disasters hitting women hardest.

### 9.6 Way forward
Girls' education (Art. 25-A); economic inclusion (digital finance, BISP); enforcement of existing laws and special courts; women in police and judiciary; political quotas in party structures; changing attitudes through media and religious scholars.
