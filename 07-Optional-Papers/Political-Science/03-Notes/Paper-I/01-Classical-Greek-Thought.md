# 01. Classical Greek Thought: Plato, Aristotle (and the Epicureans and Stoics)

Asked: Aristotle 2016, 2021, 2024, 2025, 2026 (note); Plato 2018, 2022, 2025; Epicureans 2026 (note). Together these two thinkers produced a long question in 7 of the 11 years 2016–2026.

**Background you need for both.** The Greek *polis* (city-state) was small: Athens had perhaps 30,000–40,000 adult male citizens. Citizenship meant direct participation. Athens' direct democracy lost the Peloponnesian War to Sparta (431–404 BC), went through the rule of the Thirty Tyrants (404–403 BC), and then, under restored democracy, executed Socrates (399 BC). Both Plato and Aristotle wrote in the shadow of that decline. Their question was: what makes a city good and stable?

## PART 1: PLATO (c. 427–347 BC)

### 1.1 Life and context

- Born into an aristocratic Athenian family. Pupil of **Socrates** for about eight years.
- The trial and death of Socrates (399 BC) convinced him that democracy, the rule of the ignorant many, destroys the wise. He left Athens and travelled (Megara, Egypt [verify], Italy, Sicily).
- Visited Syracuse three times, trying to train the rulers Dionysius I and II as philosopher-kings. He failed and was reportedly sold into slavery once before being ransomed.
- Founded the **Academy** in Athens around 387 BC, often called the first university. Aristotle studied there for about 20 years.

### 1.2 Key works

| Work | What it contains |
|---|---|
| *Republic* (c. 375 BC) | Justice, the ideal state, philosopher-kings, education, communism, decline of states. Written as dialogues with Socrates as speaker |
| *Statesman* (*Politicus*) | The statesman as a weaver; rule by law as a second best |
| *Laws* (his last work) | A "second-best" state run by law, with a mixed constitution |
| *Apology*, *Crito*, *Gorgias* | Trial of Socrates; duty to obey law; rhetoric versus philosophy |

### 1.3 Method

Plato is an **idealist** and uses **deduction**: he starts from ideas and reasons down to the state. His theory of **Forms** holds that the world we see is a shadow of perfect, unchanging ideas (the Form of the Good, of Justice). The **Allegory of the Cave** (Republic VII): prisoners see only shadows on a wall; the philosopher escapes, sees the sun (the Good), and must return to rule. Politics, for Plato, is the application of true knowledge.

### 1.4 Theory of justice (core of the *Republic*; asked 2022)

**Rejected views** (Book I–II):
- **Cephalus**: justice is telling the truth and paying debts. Rejected: returning a weapon to a madman is not just.
- **Polemarchus**: helping friends and harming enemies. Rejected: harming anyone makes them worse, and a just man cannot make others unjust.
- **Thrasymachus**: "justice is the interest of the stronger". Rejected: a true ruler, like a doctor, serves those in his care.
- **Glaucon**: justice is a convention (a contract) made by the weak out of fear. Rejected: justice is natural to the soul, not imposed from outside.

**Plato's own view.** Justice is **each part doing its own proper function and not interfering with others**. It exists at two levels:

| Soul | Class in the state | Virtue | Metal (myth) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reason | Rulers (philosopher-guardians) | Wisdom | Gold |
| Spirit | Auxiliaries (soldiers) | Courage | Silver |
| Appetite | Producers (farmers, artisans, traders) | Temperance (shared by all classes as agreement on who rules) | Bronze and iron |

Justice is the fourth virtue: the harmony of the three. The state is "the individual writ large".

*Diagram in words:* draw a triangle with Reason at the top, Spirit and Appetite at the base corners; beside it a matching triangle of Rulers, Auxiliaries, Producers. A line across both labelled "Justice = each doing its own work".

**Features of Platonic justice:** it is moral, not legal; internal, not imposed; functional specialisation; based on division of labour; a principle of social order.

**Criticism:** it is a duty, not a right; it ignores individual freedom; it freezes people in classes; who decides each person's "nature"? (Popper). Barker called it "a kind of social ethics" rather than law.

### 1.5 The philosopher-king

"Until philosophers rule as kings or kings become philosophers… there will be no end to the troubles of states" (Republic V, paraphrased). Reasons:
- Ruling is a skill, like medicine or navigation; only those with knowledge of the Good can do it.
- Philosophers love truth, not wealth or power, so they will not abuse office.
- They are not bound by written law; knowledge is above law.

Plato's "ship of state" image: the crew (the democratic mob) argue about who should steer, while the true navigator, who studies the stars, is called a useless stargazer.

### 1.6 Scheme of education

State-controlled, compulsory for the guardian classes, equal for men and women.

| Age | Stage |
|---|---|
| Birth–6 | Stories and myths, carefully censored |
| 6–18 | Music (literature, poetry, arts for the soul) and gymnastics (for the body) |
| 18–20 | Military and physical training |
| 20–30 | Higher education: mathematics, astronomy, harmonics (for those who pass a test) |
| 30–35 | Dialectic (philosophy) for the best |
| 35–50 | Practical service in public posts |
| 50+ | Rule as philosopher-kings, alternating with study |

(CSS 2024 MCQ asked the age at which one reaches the top of the state order: **50**.)

### 1.7 Communism of property and family

For the **guardian classes only** (rulers and auxiliaries):
- No private property; they live in common barracks and eat together; the state supplies needs.
- No private family: temporary marriages arranged by the state at festivals ("eugenics"); children raised in common nurseries and not told their parents.

**Purpose:** to remove the two great sources of corruption—greed and family favouritism—so rulers think only of the common good. "Mine" and "not mine" should be said of the same things by all.

**Differences from Marxian communism:** Plato's is aristocratic, for the rulers only, political and moral in aim; Marx's is economic, for all, and arises from class struggle.

### 1.8 Equality of women

Plato argued that women differ from men in degree of strength, not in kind. Women of ability should receive the same education and could be guardians and even philosopher-rulers. This was radical in Athens.

### 1.9 Decline of states (Republic VIII–IX)

The ideal state decays in stages, each caused by a change in the ruling class's character:

**Aristocracy (rule of the wise)** → **Timocracy** (rule of honour-loving soldiers, like Sparta) → **Oligarchy** (rule of the rich) → **Democracy** (rule of the many, excessive freedom) → **Tyranny** (rule of one who rose as the people's champion).

Democracy fails because freedom becomes licence; people demand a protector, who becomes a tyrant.

### 1.10 *Statesman* and *Laws*: the later Plato

- In the *Statesman* he admits that true philosopher-kings are rare; rule by **law** is the next best. Classification: monarchy/tyranny, aristocracy/oligarchy, lawful/lawless democracy. Under law, monarchy is best and democracy worst; without law, democracy is the least bad.
- In the *Laws*: a state of **5,040** citizen households; private property allowed with limits; family restored; a **mixed constitution** (monarchy + democracy); a "Nocturnal Council" to guard the laws; religion and education closely controlled.
- The shift from the *Republic* to the *Laws* is from idealism towards realism, and from rule of men to rule of law. It prepares the way for Aristotle.

### 1.11 Critique

| Critic | Objection |
|---|---|
| **Aristotle** | Communism destroys natural affection and makes no one care ("what is common to the greatest number gets the least care"); excessive unity turns a state into a household; the ideal ignores human nature |
| **Karl Popper**, *The Open Society and Its Enemies* (1945) | Plato is the first totalitarian: censorship, the "noble lie", rigid classes, historicism, suppression of the individual |
| **Liberals** | No rights, no consent, no elections; knowledge does not guarantee virtue ("who guards the guardians?") |
| **Feminists** | His equality is for the elite only and denies women private family life |
| Practical | No philosopher-king has been found; Syracuse failed |

**In his defence:** Plato aimed at justice, not oppression; he wanted rulers without private interest; he first saw politics as a moral and educational enterprise.

### 1.12 Relevance today

1. **Meritocracy and competitive exams**: the idea that rulers and officials must be trained and tested. The CSS exam and the old Indian Civil Service rest on this.
2. **Conflict of interest**: asset declarations, bans on officials running businesses echo his communism for guardians.
3. **Technocracy debates**: experts versus voters (Brexit, COVID policy).
4. **State-led education** for citizenship.
5. **Warning about demagogues**: his account of democracy sliding into tyranny is quoted in debates on populism.
6. **Pakistan**: elite capture, family favouritism in parties and the bureaucracy are exactly what his communism of family and property tried to stop.

## PART 2: ARISTOTLE (384–322 BC)

### 2.1 Life and context

- Born at **Stagira** in northern Greece; father was physician to the Macedonian king.
- Studied and taught at Plato's Academy for about 20 years (367–347 BC).
- Tutor to the young **Alexander the Great** (from about 343 BC).
- Founded the **Lyceum** in Athens (335 BC); his school was called "Peripatetic" because he taught while walking.
- Left Athens after Alexander's death (323 BC), fearing anti-Macedonian anger ("lest Athens sin twice against philosophy"), and died the next year.
- He witnessed the end of the independent polis as Macedon conquered Greece—yet he still defended the polis.

### 2.2 Key works

| Work | Content |
|---|---|
| *Politics* (8 books) | Nature of the state, household, slavery, citizenship, constitutions, revolution, ideal state, education |
| *Nicomachean Ethics* | Virtue, the golden mean, justice, friendship |
| *Constitution of Athens* | One of 158 constitutions his school studied; the only one surviving |
| *Rhetoric*, *Poetics* | Persuasion; drama |

### 2.3 Method: why he is called the "father of political science" and a realist

- **Inductive and empirical**: he collected and compared 158 constitutions before theorising. This is the first comparative politics.
- **Teleological**: everything has an end (*telos*); the end of the state is the good life.
- **Realist**: he asks what is best in practice for most states, not only what is ideal.
- **Moderate**: the "golden mean" between extremes.

(CSS 2024 asked: "Aristotle was a realist and not an idealist", with reference to the ends and functions of the state.)

### 2.4 Nature and origin of the state

- "Man is by nature a political animal" (*zoon politikon*). A being who lives outside the polis "is either a beast or a god".
- The state grows naturally: **household** (man–wife, master–slave, parent–child) → **village** (for more than daily needs) → **polis** (self-sufficient).
- "The state comes into existence for the sake of life, and continues for the sake of the good life."
- The state is **prior to the individual** in the order of nature, as the whole is prior to the part (a hand cut from the body is no longer a hand).
- The state is not a contract, a business partnership or a military alliance: it exists for virtue.

### 2.5 Ends and functions of the state

- Promote the **good life**: moral and intellectual virtue, happiness (*eudaimonia*).
- Educate citizens in virtue (education is the state's main task and must be public).
- Ensure justice, stability, the rule of law.
- Provide self-sufficiency.
- **Realist point:** he accepts that most states cannot reach the ideal, so he studies how to preserve whatever constitution exists.

### 2.6 Classification of constitutions (asked 2016, 2021, 2026)

Two criteria: **number of rulers** and **whose interest they rule for**.

| Rulers | True (common good) | Perverted (own interest) |
|---|---|---|
| One | **Monarchy** | **Tyranny** |
| Few | **Aristocracy** | **Oligarchy** (in reality, rule of the rich) |
| Many | **Polity** | **Democracy** (in reality, rule of the poor) |

Key points:
- In reality, oligarchy is rule of the **rich** and democracy rule of the **poor**: the real criterion is economic class.
- **Cycle**: monarchy → tyranny → aristocracy → oligarchy → democracy → monarchy again (constitutions decay and replace each other).
- **Best ideal**: monarchy or aristocracy of truly virtuous men.
- **Best practicable**: **polity**, a mix of oligarchy and democracy, ruled by a large **middle class**. The middle class is neither arrogant like the rich nor envious like the poor; it is the most stable base. Democracy is the least bad of the perverted forms.

**Criticism of the classification:** too mechanical; uses number, a quantitative test; does not fit modern states (federal, parliamentary, presidential); confuses state and government. Defence: it is still the starting point of comparative politics (Polybius, Cicero and Montesquieu built on it).

### 2.7 Citizenship

- A citizen is one who **shares in the administration of justice and in deliberative office** (the assembly and courts). Birth and residence are not enough.
- Excludes slaves, women, children, resident foreigners, and in the best state labourers and artisans (they lack leisure for virtue).
- The good man and the good citizen are the same only in the ideal state.

### 2.8 Slavery

- Some people are "slaves by nature": they can follow reason but not exercise it. Slavery benefits both master and slave.
- He distinguishes **natural** slavery (justified) from **legal** slavery by war capture (not always just).
- Criticism: a defence of Athenian practice; contradicts human dignity; modern thought rejects it. Defence offered by some: he insisted slaves be treated humanely and hinted they could be freed as a reward.

### 2.9 Property and family

- Rejects Plato's communism. Property should be **private in ownership and common in use** (generosity).
- The family is natural and the school of virtue.
- Property is needed for virtue (generosity, leisure) but should be moderate; excessive wealth and poverty both cause disorder.

### 2.10 Justice

- **Distributive justice**: honours and offices distributed in proportion to merit or contribution (geometric equality). Democrats measure merit by free birth, oligarchs by wealth, aristocrats by virtue.
- **Corrective (remedial) justice**: restoring equality in transactions and wrongs (arithmetic equality), the work of courts.
- Justice is "giving each his due" and the virtue that holds the state together.

### 2.11 Rule of law

- "Law is reason unaffected by desire." The rule of law is preferable to the rule of any individual, because even the best man has passions.
- Laws should rule; rulers are "guardians and servants of the laws". This is the root of **constitutionalism** (PMS 2020 asked about Aristotle's constitutionalism and distributive justice).

### 2.12 Revolution (Politics Book V)

**General cause:** the passion for equality—men who are equal in one respect (free birth) think they should be equal in all; men unequal in one respect (wealth) think they are superior in all.

**Particular causes:** insolence and profit-seeking of rulers; fear; contempt; disproportionate growth of one class; election intrigues; neglect of small changes; differences of race; foreign influence.

**Revolutions in each constitution:** democracy falls through demagogues attacking the rich; oligarchy through rulers' misrule and rivalry among the rich; aristocracy when too few share honours; monarchy and tyranny through insult, fear and contempt.

**Prevention:** obey the law, even in small things; do not deceive the people; keep the middle class strong; prevent any one person gaining excessive power (short terms); make office unprofitable; educate citizens in the spirit of the constitution (the most important); keep watch on foreign threats. **Tyrants** survive either by repression (spies, divide and rule, keeping people poor and busy) or by appearing as moderate kings.

### 2.13 The ideal state (Books VII–VIII)

Small enough that citizens know each other; near the sea but not dependent on it; citizens are a leisured class; farmers and artisans are not citizens; public education aimed at virtue; the young serve as soldiers, the middle-aged rule, the old serve as priests.

### 2.14 Aristotle versus Plato (asked 2025)

| | Plato | Aristotle |
|---|---|---|
| Method | Deductive, idealist, philosophical | Inductive, empirical, realist |
| Ideal state | Rule of philosopher-kings | Polity / rule of law |
| Rule | Of wise men, above law (Republic) | Of law |
| Property | Communism for guardians | Private ownership, common use |
| Family | Abolished for guardians | Natural and necessary |
| Women | Equal for guardians | Inferior; household role |
| Unity | Maximum unity | Too much unity destroys the state; plurality is natural |
| Slavery | Hardly discussed | Natural slavery defended |
| Change | Decline through moral decay | Revolution through inequality; can be prevented |
| Influence | Utopian thought, Popper's "enemy" | Comparative politics, constitutionalism, Aquinas, Islamic falsafa |

Similarities: both see the state as natural and moral; both want education by the state; both distrust pure democracy; both want rule for the common good; both defend the small polis.

### 2.15 Critique

- Defends **slavery** and the subordination of **women**.
- Narrow, aristocratic **citizenship**.
- Tied to the **city-state** just as it was disappearing; ignored Alexander's empire.
- **Conservative**: more interested in stability than reform.
- Classification is rigid and outdated.
- Yet: first empirical political science; rule of law; middle class; revolution analysis; distributive justice.

### 2.16 Relevance today

1. **Middle class and democracy**: Seymour Martin Lipset's thesis that a large middle class sustains democracy restates Aristotle. Pakistan's weak middle class is often cited in the same way (CSS 2018 note).
2. **Mixed government and polity**: modern constitutional democracy mixes the one (president/PM), the few (senate, courts) and the many (elected house).
3. **Rule of law**: the base of constitutionalism; relevant to debates on the 26th and 27th Amendments in Pakistan.
4. **Revolution theory**: inequality as the root of unrest (Arab Spring, 2022 Sri Lanka protests).
5. **Comparative method**: today's comparative politics.
6. **Civic education**: preserving a constitution by teaching its spirit.

## PART 3: AFTER ARISTOTLE — EPICUREANS AND STOICS (2026 short note)

After Alexander (d. 323 BC) the polis lost its independence. Philosophy turned from "how to run the city" to "how to live well as an individual". Three schools matter.

### 3.1 The Epicureans

- **Founder:** Epicurus (341–270 BC), who taught in his "Garden" in Athens from about 307 BC. Later spread to Rome through the poet **Lucretius**, *On the Nature of Things*.
- **Ethics:** the goal of life is pleasure, understood as **absence of pain in the body and of disturbance in the mind** (*ataraxia*). Simple living, friendship and freedom from fear (of gods and of death) bring it. This is not indulgence.
- **Politics:**
  - Advised withdrawal from public life: "live unnoticed" (*lathe biosas*). Politics brings anxiety and danger.
  - The state and law rest on **agreement**: justice is "a pact neither to harm nor be harmed". There is no justice by nature apart from this contract. This is an early **social contract** idea, later echoed by Hobbes.
  - Laws are just only while they are useful for mutual security; when they stop being useful, they stop being just.
  - Atomism: the world is atoms moving in a void; gods exist but do not interfere.
- **Significance:** individualism, utilitarian view of law (Bentham was influenced by this line of thinking), contract theory, private life over public duty.
- **Criticism:** political quietism; leaves the state to the ambitious; reduces justice to convenience.

### 3.2 The Stoics

- **Founder:** Zeno of Citium (c. 334–262 BC), who taught at the *Stoa Poikile* (Painted Porch) in Athens. Roman Stoics: **Cicero** (sympathetic), **Seneca**, **Epictetus** (a former slave) and the emperor **Marcus Aurelius**.
- **Ideas:**
  - The universe is governed by divine **reason** (*logos*). Living according to nature means living by reason.
  - **Natural law**: a universal law of reason binding all people, higher than the laws of any city.
  - **Cosmopolitanism**: all humans are citizens of one world-city (*cosmopolis*); brotherhood of man; spiritual equality of slaves and masters, Greeks and barbarians.
  - Duty to take part in public life (unlike Epicureans).
- **Significance:** the bridge from the polis to the world empire; foundation of Roman law (*jus gentium*), Christian natural law (Aquinas), modern human rights and international law (Grotius).

### 3.3 The Cynics (for MCQs)

Diogenes of Sinope (c. 412–323 BC): rejection of convention, wealth and status; called himself "citizen of the world".

### 3.4 Comparison table

| | Epicureans | Stoics |
|---|---|---|
| Goal | Pleasure as tranquillity | Virtue as living by reason |
| Politics | Withdraw | Participate as duty |
| Law and justice | Contract for mutual advantage | Natural law of reason |
| Humanity | Small circles of friends | One world-city |
| Legacy | Contract theory, utilitarianism | Natural law, human rights, Roman law |
