## 1. LOCAL SELF-GOVERNMENT: THEORY

### 1.1 Meaning
Government of local affairs (a city, district, town or village) by **locally elected bodies** with their own powers and resources. It enjoys **autonomy within limits set by statute** (CSS 2026 MCQ: "quasi-autonomy"), unlike local administration, which is run by appointed officials of the central or provincial government.

### 1.2 Forms of decentralisation (Dennis Rondinelli)
| Form | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Deconcentration** | Central ministries shift work to their own field offices | Deputy commissioners |
| **Delegation** | Responsibilities given to semi-autonomous agencies | Development authorities (LDA, KDA) |
| **Devolution** | Powers, functions and funds transferred to elected local governments with legal autonomy | Musharraf's 2001 district governments; Art. 140A |
| **Privatisation / divestment** | Functions transferred to private or voluntary bodies | Waste management contracts |

### 1.3 Why local government matters (2018)
1. **School of democracy**: J.S. Mill ("the practical part of the political education of a free people"); Tocqueville ("town meetings are to liberty what primary schools are to science"); Bryce ("the best school of democracy").
2. **Efficient service delivery**: water, sanitation, solid waste, primary health and education, local roads, street lighting.
3. **Responsiveness and accountability**: officials close to citizens; local knowledge.
4. **Participation** and inclusion: reserved seats for women, peasants, workers, minorities, youth.
5. **Relieves the centre and provinces** of local work.
6. **Leadership recruitment**: training ground for national politicians.
7. **Subsidiarity**: decisions at the lowest effective level (EU principle; Catholic social teaching).
8. **Disaster response**: early warning, evacuation, reconstruction (2022 and 2025 floods).
9. **National integration**: gives every area a stake in the system.
10. **Resource mobilisation**: local taxes (property tax).

### 1.4 Limits
Elite and biradari capture; corruption; low capacity; financial dependence; conflicts with provincial politicians; uneven development.

## 2. COMPARATIVE SYSTEMS OF LOCAL GOVERNANCE

### 2.1 United Kingdom (unitary state; Paper II 2020, 2021)
- **Basis**: local government exists by Acts of Parliament (no constitutional protection); Parliament can create or abolish councils (the Greater London Council was abolished in 1986 and London government recreated in 2000).
- **Structure in England**: a mix of **two-tier** (county councils and district councils) and **single-tier unitary authorities** (metropolitan boroughs, London boroughs, unitary councils); parish and town councils at the lowest level. Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland have their own systems under devolved governments.
- **Directly elected mayors**: London (since 2000) and **combined-authority "metro mayors"** (Greater Manchester since 2017, where Andy Burnham was mayor before becoming Prime Minister in July 2026).
- **Functions**: education (schools, though academies have reduced council control), social care, housing, planning, waste, roads, libraries, leisure, environmental health.
- **Finance**: central grants, **council tax** (on property), business rates (partly retained).
- **Laws**: Local Government Act 1972; Local Government Act 2000 (leader-and-cabinet or mayor-and-cabinet models); **Localism Act 2011** (general power of competence; community rights); devolution deals; the English Devolution White Paper (December 2024) planned strategic authorities and reorganisation into unitary councils [verify progress].
- **Central control**: ultra vires rule (historically), audit, grant dependence, rate-capping; councils' financial distress (several issued "section 114" bankruptcy notices, e.g. Birmingham in 2023).
- **How it works under a unitary system**: powers are granted, not guaranteed; the centre sets frameworks and funding; local authorities implement and adapt.

### 2.2 United States
- Local government is a creature of the **states**, not the federal constitution. **Dillon's Rule** (local governments have only powers expressly granted by the state) vs **home rule** (broad self-government charters).
- About 90,000 local governments: counties, municipalities (cities, towns), townships, **school districts**, special districts (water, transit).
- Forms of city government: mayor–council (strong or weak mayor), council–manager (professional city manager), commission.
- Strong local property tax base; elected sheriffs, judges, school boards.

### 2.3 France
- Historically centralised (Napoleonic **prefects**). About **35,000 communes** (each with a mayor and council), 101 **departments**, 18 **regions** (13 in metropolitan France since 2016).
- **Decentralisation laws of 1982–83** (Defferre laws) transferred executive power from prefects to elected councils; 2003 constitutional amendment declared the Republic's organisation "decentralised".
- Prefects still supervise legality.

### 2.4 Germany
- **Article 28** of the Basic Law guarantees municipalities (*Gemeinden*) the right to regulate all local affairs on their own responsibility, within the law.
- Tiers: municipalities, districts (*Kreise*), city-states (Berlin, Hamburg, Bremen) as Länder.
- Directly elected mayors in most Länder; strong role in implementing federal and Land laws (administrative federalism).

### 2.5 India
- **73rd Amendment (1992)**: three-tier **Panchayati Raj** (village, block, district) in rural areas; **74th Amendment (1992)**: urban local bodies (municipal corporations, councils, nagar panchayats).
- Constitutional status (Parts IX and IX-A); five-year terms; **State Election Commissions** and **State Finance Commissions**; one-third of seats reserved for women (many states 50%); reservations for Scheduled Castes and Tribes; Eleventh and Twelfth Schedules list 29 and 18 functions.
- Problems: "3Fs" (functions, funds, functionaries) not fully devolved.

### 2.6 China
- Party-state hierarchy: provinces, prefectures, counties, townships.
- **Village committees** elected directly since the Organic Law of Village Committees (trial 1987; formal 1998); urban residents' committees; the village party secretary usually dominates.

### 2.7 Lessons for Pakistan
Constitutional protection (India's 73rd/74th), fixed tenure and independent election and finance commissions; directly elected metropolitan mayors (UK, US); a guaranteed share of revenue; reserved seats; capacity-building.

## 3. LOCAL GOVERNMENT IN PAKISTAN (2018, 2022)

### 3.1 Colonial roots
Lord Ripon's Resolution of **1882** (the "father of local self-government in India"); municipal and district boards; the 1935 Act. Local bodies were controlled by British officials (deputy commissioners).

### 3.2 Development after 1947
| Period | System | Features |
|---|---|---|
| 1947–58 | Colonial-era municipal committees and district boards | Rarely elected; bureaucratic control |
| **1959 Ayub Khan: Basic Democracies** (Basic Democracies Order, 1959) | Four tiers in West Pakistan [verify: five in some accounts]: union councils (rural) / town and union committees (urban), tehsil (thana) councils, district councils, divisional councils | **80,000 Basic Democrats** (40,000 per wing) elected; acted as the **electoral college** for the presidential referendum (1960), the presidential election (1965) and assemblies under the 1962 Constitution; official members and bureaucrats dominated; collapsed with Ayub (1969) |
| 1972–77 Bhutto | People's Local Government Ordinance (1972) | Never implemented; no local elections |
| **1979 Zia-ul-Haq** | Local Government Ordinances (provincial) | **Non-party** elections (1979, 1983, 1987); used for legitimacy; strengthened biradari politics; elected councils dissolved after 1988 |
| 1990s | Elections in some provinces; councils suspended (1993) | Neglect under elected governments |
| **2001 Pervez Musharraf: Devolution of Power Plan** (Local Government Ordinance 2001, National Reconstruction Bureau) | Three tiers: **district** (zila) government headed by an elected **nazim**; **tehsil/town**; **union council** | Deputy commissioner post abolished (district coordination officer under nazim); **33% seats for women**; seats for peasants, workers, minorities; non-party elections (2001, 2005); provincial fund transfers through Provincial Finance Commissions; police reforms (Police Order 2002) |
| **2010 18th Amendment: Article 140A** | "Each Province shall, by law, establish a local government system and devolve political, administrative and financial responsibility and authority to the elected representatives"; elections by the **Election Commission** (140A(2)) | Local government made a provincial subject with a constitutional mandate |
| 2013 provincial Acts | Punjab, Sindh, KP, Balochistan LG Acts | Supreme Court orders (2014–15) forced elections |
| 2015 elections | Party-based elections held in all provinces (Balochistan 2013; Punjab, Sindh, KP 2015) | First local elections under civilian rule nationwide |
| 2019 Punjab | PTI's Punjab Local Government Act 2019 **dissolved** elected councils a year early | Supreme Court restored them in March 2021 [verify] |
| 2021–23 | KP elections (Dec 2021, Mar 2022); Balochistan (2022); Sindh (2022–23; Karachi mayor elected June 2023) | Islamabad elections repeatedly postponed |
| 2025–26 | Punjab passed a new Local Government Act (2025); polls now scheduled for **February 2027** in two phases (as of Nov 2026); KP's councils' term ended, new polls pending amid governor's rule (Oct 2026); a stronger Art. 140A is part of the proposed 28th Amendment | See Current Affairs pack |

### 3.3 The paradox
Local government was strongest under **military rulers** (Ayub, Zia, Musharraf), who used it to build a non-party base and bypass national parties, and weakest under **elected governments**, whose MNAs and MPAs see local councils as rivals for development funds and patronage.

### 3.4 Reasons for repeated failure (2018)
1. **Lack of constitutional protection** until 2010, and even now no fixed tenure or deadline for elections.
2. **Political use by military regimes** gave local government a legitimacy problem.
3. **Provincial capture**: provinces after the 18th Amendment centralised powers rather than devolving them ("provincial centralism").
4. **MNA/MPA development funds** compete with local bodies.
5. **Financial dependence**: weak own-source revenue; irregular or absent Provincial Finance Commission awards.
6. **Bureaucratic resistance**: deputy commissioners and provincial departments retain control.
7. **No continuity**: every government rewrites the law and dissolves predecessors' councils.
8. **Non-party elections** (Zia, Musharraf) strengthened biradari and weakened accountability.
9. **Elite capture** and corruption; weak capacity.
10. **Delays** through delimitation, litigation and postponement by election commissions.

### 3.5 Remedies
Amend Art. 140A for fixed five-year terms, elections within a set period, and a minimum list of local functions; mandatory Provincial Finance Commission awards with a formula share; transfer of property tax and municipal agencies to city governments; directly elected mayors for metropolitan cities; independent local government commissions; capacity-building; e-governance and transparency; party-based elections with reserved seats.

## 4. PUBLIC ADMINISTRATION

### 4.1 Meaning
The implementation of government policy and the academic study of how governments are organised and managed. (CSS 2025 MCQ: the study of government affairs.)
- **Woodrow Wilson**, "The Study of Administration" (1887): "Administration is the most obvious part of government; it is government in action." Proposed the **politics–administration dichotomy** (politicians decide; administrators implement neutrally).
- **Luther Gulick** (1937): **POSDCORB**: Planning, Organising, Staffing, Directing, Co-ordinating, Reporting, Budgeting.

### 4.2 Approaches
| Approach | Key ideas | Thinkers |
|---|---|---|
| Classical / scientific management | Efficiency, one best way, hierarchy | Frederick Taylor (1911), Henri Fayol (14 principles), Weber's bureaucracy |
| Human relations | Social and psychological factors, motivation | Elton Mayo (Hawthorne studies, 1920s–30s) |
| Behavioural | Decision-making, "bounded rationality", satisficing | Herbert Simon (*Administrative Behavior*, 1947) |
| New Public Administration | Equity, social justice, citizen participation | Minnowbrook Conference (1968) |
| **New Public Management** (NPM) | Market mechanisms, performance targets, privatisation, citizens as customers | Hood (1991); Osborne and Gaebler, *Reinventing Government* (1992) |
| New Public Service | Citizens, not customers; serve the public interest | Denhardt and Denhardt (2000) |
| Governance / networks | Steering through partnerships with private sector and civil society | Rhodes |
| Digital governance | E-government, data | — |

### 4.3 Pakistan
Colonial structure; generalist civil service (CSS) and specialists; reform commissions (Cornelius 1962, Bhutto 1973, NRB 2001, Ishrat Husain's Task Force 2018–20); problems: politicisation, transfers, red tape, corruption, low pay, weak use of technology; reforms: e-Khidmat centres, Citizen Portal, FBR digitisation, Special Investment Facilitation Council (2023) as a one-window body.

## 5. PUBLIC POLICY

### 5.1 Meaning
- **Thomas Dye**: "whatever governments choose to do or not to do".
- **James Anderson**: "a purposive course of action followed by an actor or set of actors in dealing with a problem or matter of concern". (CSS 2025 MCQ: a set of **actions** adopted by government.)
- **David Easton**: the authoritative allocation of values.

### 5.2 Policy cycle
1. **Agenda-setting** (problem recognition; John Kingdon's "policy windows" where problem, policy and politics streams meet).
2. **Formulation** (options developed by bureaucrats, experts, think tanks).
3. **Adoption / decision-making** (cabinet, legislature).
4. **Implementation** (bureaucracy; "top-down" vs "bottom-up"; Pressman and Wildavsky's *Implementation*, 1973).
5. **Evaluation** (impact assessment, audit).
6. **Feedback / termination or revision**.

### 5.3 Models of policy-making
| Model | Idea |
|---|---|
| Rational-comprehensive | Identify all options, choose the best (an ideal) |
| **Incremental** | Charles Lindblom, "The Science of 'Muddling Through'" (1959): small adjustments to existing policy |
| Elite | Policy reflects elite preferences |
| Group (pluralist) | Policy is the balance of group pressures |
| Institutional | Policy shaped by institutions and rules |
| Public choice | Self-interested politicians and bureaucrats |
| Garbage can | Organised anarchy: problems and solutions meet by chance (Cohen, March, Olsen, 1972) |
| Systems | Easton's input–output model |

### 5.4 Policy-making in Pakistan
Actors: Prime Minister's Office, Cabinet and its committees, Planning Commission (Five-Year Plans; "Uraan Pakistan" 2024–29), Economic Coordination Committee, Council of Common Interests, National Economic Council, provinces after the 18th Amendment, military (security and, through SIFC, economic policy), IMF conditionality, donors, think tanks (PIDE, SDPI), courts. Problems: discontinuity with each change of government, weak data and evaluation, elite capture, centralisation, implementation gaps.
