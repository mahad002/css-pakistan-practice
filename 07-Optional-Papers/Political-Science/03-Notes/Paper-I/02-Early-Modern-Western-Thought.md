# 02. Early Modern Western Thought: Machiavelli, Hobbes, Locke, Montesquieu, Rousseau

Asked: social contract 2017, 2018, 2020, 2021, 2022 (Hobbes), 2025 (Rousseau); Machiavelli 2016, 2024; Montesquieu 2020, 2024. This is the most-asked block of the whole subject.

**The big picture.** Between 1500 and 1800 Europe moved from feudalism and Church authority to sovereign territorial states, commerce and science. The Renaissance (Machiavelli) freed politics from theology. The Reformation and religious wars (1517–1648) made order the first question (Hobbes). The rise of Parliament and property (Locke) made limits on rulers the next question. The Enlightenment asked how to arrange institutions for liberty (Montesquieu) and how to make the people sovereign (Rousseau).

## PART 1: NICCOLÒ MACHIAVELLI (1469–1527)

### 1.1 Life and context

- Born in **Florence**. Served the Florentine republic as Secretary of the Second Chancery and diplomat (1498–1512). Met Cesare Borgia, Louis XII of France, Pope Julius II and Emperor Maximilian.
- Italy was divided into five main states (Naples, the Papal States, Milan, Venice, Florence) and invaded repeatedly by France and Spain from 1494. Mercenary armies were treacherous; the Pope played politics.
- In 1512 the **Medici** returned to power with Spanish help. Machiavelli was dismissed, then imprisoned and tortured (1513) on suspicion of conspiracy. Released, he retired to his farm near Florence and wrote.
- His dream: an Italy united and free of foreign "barbarians".

### 1.2 Key works

| Work | Notes |
|---|---|
| *The Prince* (written 1513, published 1532) | Advice to a new prince on gaining and keeping power. Dedicated to Lorenzo de' Medici |
| *Discourses on the First Ten Books of Livy* (c. 1513–19, published 1531) | Republicanism, liberty, Rome's mixed constitution |
| *The Art of War* (1521) | Citizen militias |
| *Florentine Histories* | Commissioned by the Medici |

### 1.3 Method

- **Empirical and historical**: lessons drawn from history (Rome) and his own diplomatic experience.
- **Realist**: "how men live is so far from how they ought to live" that he who studies what ought to be will ruin himself.
- He studies politics as it is, not as it should be. This is why he is called the **father of modern political science**.

### 1.4 Core ideas

**(a) Human nature.** Men are "ungrateful, fickle, liars, deceivers, fearful of danger and greedy for gain". They forget the death of a father sooner than the loss of their property. Politics must start from this.

**(b) Separation of politics from ethics and religion.** The ruler's actions are judged by their results for the state, not by Christian morality. He did not say "the end justifies the means" in those words; he said that in the actions of princes "one looks to the result". This is **dual morality**: one standard for private persons, another for rulers.

**(c) Fear or love.** It is best to be both loved and feared; if one must choose, it is safer to be **feared than loved**, because love depends on others while fear depends on you. But the prince must avoid being **hated**: never touch subjects' property or women.

**(d) The lion and the fox.** The prince must be a fox to recognise traps and a lion to frighten wolves. He should appear merciful, faithful, humane, honest and religious, and be able to act otherwise when needed.

**(e) Virtù and fortuna.** *Fortuna* (chance) controls about half of our actions; *virtù* (energy, skill, boldness) controls the rest. Fortune is like a flooding river: the able ruler builds dykes in calm times.

**(f) Arms.** "Good laws and good arms" are the foundations of the state, and there cannot be good laws without good arms. Mercenaries and auxiliaries are useless and dangerous; a citizen army is essential.

**(g) Religion as an instrument.** Religion is valuable for unity and obedience (Roman religion). He blamed the Papacy for keeping Italy divided and Christianity for making men humble and weak.

**(h) The state.** He popularised *lo stato* for a territorial power structure, the modern sense of "state".

**(i) Republicanism in the *Discourses*.** He prefers republics for liberty and long life. Rome's greatness came from a **mixed constitution** (consuls, senate, tribunes) and from healthy **conflict between nobles and people**, which produced laws favouring liberty. The people are wiser and more constant than a prince.

**Reconciling *The Prince* and the *Discourses*:** a prince is needed to found or restore a corrupt state; a republic is best to preserve it. *The Prince* addresses an emergency (Italy in 1513).

### 1.5 Was Machiavelli an apostle of power politics? (asked 2016)

**Yes:** power as the end; amoral means; fear; deception; force; raison d'état.
**But:** power was a means to a higher end—a united, secure, free Italy; he valued liberty and republics; his "immoralism" applied to emergencies; he despised cruelty used for its own sake (praising "well-used" cruelty that is done once and ends).

### 1.6 "A citizen of all states and a contemporary of all ages"? (asked 2024)

Arguments for:
- His view of human nature and power fits all times.
- Modern **realism** in IR (Morgenthau, Kissinger, Mearsheimer) restates him: states pursue power and survival.
- **Raison d'état** (reasons of state) still guides security decisions: intelligence, covert action, alliances of convenience.
- His advice on image management fits modern politics: spin, PR, media.
- Citizen armies and national security are still central.
- His secular, empirical approach is the method of political science.

Arguments against:
- Modern states rest on constitutions, rights and courts that limit the "prince".
- Democracies punish leaders who lie (Watergate).
- International law and institutions constrain power.
- His world was small Italian states, not nuclear powers and global markets.

**Balanced judgement:** his diagnosis is timeless; his remedies are tied to 16th-century Italy.

### 1.7 Critique

| Critic | View |
|---|---|
| Cardinal Pole, the Church | Work of the devil; *The Prince* placed on the Index (1559) |
| Frederick the Great | *Anti-Machiavel* (1740): a ruler is the first servant of the state |
| Leo Strauss | A "teacher of evil" |
| Rousseau | *The Prince* is "a book for republicans": it exposes how tyrants work |
| Gramsci | *The Modern Prince*: the revolutionary party is the new prince |
| Isaiah Berlin | His originality: political and Christian values are both real and cannot be reconciled |
| Quentin Skinner | A civic humanist republican |

### 1.8 Comparison: Machiavelli and Kautilya

Kautilya (Chanakya), *Arthashastra* (c. 4th century BC), adviser to Chandragupta Maurya: realism, spies, the mandala theory of neighbours, the ruler's welfare duty. Often called "the Indian Machiavelli". Kautilya places the king under dharma and welfare more than Machiavelli does.

### 1.9 Relevance and Pakistan

- Realism in Pakistan's foreign policy: alliances of necessity (SEATO, CENTO, the post-9/11 alliance, China).
- Civil–military relations: "good arms" and the security state.
- Leaders' image-building and use of religion for legitimacy (Zia).

## PART 2: THOMAS HOBBES (1588–1679)

### 2.1 Life and context

- Born at Malmesbury, England, prematurely, it is said, when news came of the Spanish Armada: "fear and I were born twins".
- Oxford; tutor to the Cavendish family; met Galileo; admired Euclid's geometry.
- **English Civil War** (1642–51) between King and Parliament; Charles I executed in 1649. Hobbes fled to Paris (1640–51) and tutored the future Charles II in mathematics.
- Returned under Cromwell; after the Restoration (1660) was pensioned by Charles II but accused of atheism.

### 2.2 Key works

*The Elements of Law* (1640); *De Cive* (1642); **Leviathan** (1651); *Behemoth* (history of the Civil War, written c. 1668).

### 2.3 Method

**Materialist and mechanical.** Everything is matter in motion, including human beings. He reasons like a geometer: from definitions to conclusions. The "resolutive–compositive" method: break society into its parts (individuals) and rebuild it logically.

### 2.4 Human nature

- Humans are driven by **appetites** (desire) and **aversions** (fear). The strongest passion is fear of violent death.
- A "perpetual and restless desire of power after power, that ceaseth only in death".
- **Equality**: humans are roughly equal in strength and wit; the weakest can kill the strongest by trick or alliance. Equality of ability breeds equality of hope, hence conflict.
- Three causes of quarrel: **competition** (for gain), **diffidence** (distrust, for safety), **glory** (for reputation).

### 2.5 State of nature

- A condition of **war of every man against every man** (not constant fighting, but a constant known disposition to fight).
- No industry, agriculture, navigation, arts, letters or society; "continual fear and danger of violent death"; life is "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short".
- No property, no justice and injustice ("where there is no common power, there is no law; where no law, no injustice").
- Hobbes offered examples: civil war, international relations between kings, and (wrongly) the "savage people in many places of America".

### 2.6 Right of nature and laws of nature

- **Right of nature**: liberty to use one's power for self-preservation, by any means.
- **Laws of nature** are rules of reason (he lists 19). The first three:
  1. Seek peace and follow it; if you cannot, defend yourself by all means.
  2. Be willing, when others are too, to lay down the right to all things, and be content with as much liberty against others as you allow them.
  3. Perform covenants made. (This is the origin of **justice**.)
- Laws of nature bind only in conscience until a sovereign enforces them: "covenants, without the sword, are but words".

### 2.7 The social contract

- Every man covenants with every other man: "I authorise and give up my right of governing myself to this man, or to this assembly of men, on this condition, that thou give up thy right to him, and authorise all his actions in like manner."
- The **sovereign is not a party** to the contract, so he cannot break it.
- The result is the **Leviathan**, the "mortal god", one person made from many (the famous frontispiece shows a giant crowned figure made of many small people).
- Commonwealth by **institution** (agreement) or by **acquisition** (conquest); both rest on consent motivated by fear.

### 2.8 Sovereignty

Absolute, indivisible, inalienable, permanent. The sovereign's rights include: making law, judging, war and peace, choosing officials, rewarding and punishing, censoring doctrines, controlling the Church. Subjects cannot change the form of government, accuse the sovereign of injustice or punish him. Hobbes prefers **monarchy** (one interest, consistent counsel, no faction) but his theory works for an assembly too.

### 2.9 The limited right of resistance (asked 2022)

Even under absolute sovereignty, some rights cannot be given up, because the whole point of the contract was **self-preservation**:
- A subject may resist if the sovereign tries to kill, wound or imprison him.
- He need not kill himself, accuse himself, or (sometimes) fight in war if he can find a substitute; a "naturally timorous" man may refuse battle.
- "The obligation of subjects to the sovereign is understood to last as long, and no longer, than the power lasteth by which he is able to protect them." If the sovereign can no longer protect, obedience ends; subjects may submit to a new protector.
- Subjects also have liberty wherever the law is silent.

This is limited: there is no collective right of revolution, no right to resist for religion or good government, and the sovereign remains unjudged.

### 2.10 Individualism within absolutism

Paradox: Hobbes's starting point is the free, equal individual who consents; government exists for the individual's security. Hence Michael Oakeshott and others see him as a founder of liberalism, and C.B. Macpherson calls him the theorist of "possessive individualism".

### 2.11 Critique

- **Pessimistic** and one-sided view of human nature; ignores sympathy and cooperation.
- **Contract is unhistorical**; and if people are so selfish and distrustful, how can they trust each other to make the first covenant?
- **Absolutism**: Locke: it is foolish to guard against "polecats or foxes" but be content to be devoured by "lions".
- Confuses state with government; no right to rebel against tyranny.
- Prefers order to liberty and justice.

### 2.12 Relevance

- **Realism in IR**: the international system is a Hobbesian state of nature with no world sovereign (Waltz's anarchy).
- **Failed states**: Somalia in the 1990s, Afghanistan in civil war, Libya after 2011: life without a common power.
- **Security versus liberty** debates after 9/11; emergency powers.
- **Pakistan**: arguments for a strong centre and a security state; the trade-off between order and rights during military rule; the logic of "the end of obedience is protection" when the state fails to protect citizens from terrorism.

## PART 3: JOHN LOCKE (1632–1704)

### 3.1 Life and context

- Born in Somerset to a Puritan family. Studied at Oxford; trained as a physician.
- From 1667 physician, secretary and friend of **Lord Shaftesbury**, founder of the Whig opposition to Charles II and his Catholic brother James.
- Fled to Holland (1683–89) after a failed plot. Returned with William and Mary after the **Glorious Revolution** (1688), which established parliamentary supremacy and the Bill of Rights (1689).

### 3.2 Key works

*Two Treatises of Government* (1689, title page dated 1690): the First refutes Robert Filmer's *Patriarcha* (divine right of kings descending from Adam); the Second sets out his own theory. *A Letter Concerning Toleration* (1689). *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (1690): the mind at birth is a blank slate (*tabula rasa*).

### 3.3 State of nature

- A state of **perfect freedom and equality**, governed by the **law of nature**, which is reason: no one should harm another in life, health, liberty or possessions.
- It is a state of peace, goodwill and mutual help—not war (though a state of war can arise within it).
- Everyone has the right to punish breaches of natural law.
- **Three inconveniences**: (1) no established, settled, known law; (2) no known and impartial judge; (3) no power to enforce just sentences. Men are judges in their own cases, so disputes escalate.

### 3.4 Natural rights and property

- Natural rights: **life, liberty and estate**, which he groups as "property" in a broad sense.
- **Labour theory of property**: the earth was given to mankind in common; a person owns his body and labour; whatever he "mixes his labour with" becomes his.
- Limits: (1) take only what you can use before it spoils; (2) leave "enough, and as good" for others.
- **Money** removes the spoilage limit, permitting unequal accumulation by consent.

### 3.5 The contract and government

- People unite into a **civil society** by consent to protect their property. This first agreement creates the community; the community then sets up a government as a **trust** (fiduciary relationship).
- **Consent**: express (for full members) or **tacit** (anyone who enjoys property or travels on the roads in a state consents).
- **Majority rule**: once society is formed, the majority acts for the whole.
- Government is **limited**: it holds only the powers given to it, for the public good; it cannot take property without consent (no taxation without consent); it must rule by known, standing laws and impartial judges.

### 3.6 Separation of powers (early version)

- **Legislative**: supreme, but still a trust; cannot be transferred.
- **Executive**: enforces law; can be in different hands; holds the **prerogative** to act for the public good where law is silent.
- **Federative**: war, peace, treaties, foreign affairs (usually with the executive).
- No independent judiciary in his scheme (Montesquieu added it).

### 3.7 Right of revolution

- If government breaches its trust (legislative alters the laws arbitrarily, executive misuses power, government is dissolved by conquest or tyranny), power returns to the people, who may set up a new government.
- Revolution is the "appeal to heaven". He denies it causes disorder: people bear "great mistakes" patiently and rise only after "a long train of abuses".
- This justified 1688 and inspired 1776.

### 3.8 Toleration

The state's role is civil interests (life, liberty, property), not souls; belief cannot be forced. Toleration for all Protestant sects—but not for **Catholics** (loyal to a foreign prince, the Pope) or **atheists** (cannot be bound by oaths).

### 3.9 Critique

- **Tacit consent** is too weak: staying in one's country is not real consent (Hume).
- State of nature is vague: peaceful and yet full of inconveniences.
- **Class bias**: Macpherson argues he justified unlimited capitalist accumulation and in effect limited full membership to property-owners.
- **Majority tyranny**: majority rule can oppress minorities.
- When exactly a revolution is justified is left to the people's judgement—a recipe for disorder (critics) or for liberty (supporters).
- Limited toleration.

### 3.10 Relevance

- **US Declaration of Independence** (1776): "Life, Liberty and the pursuit of Happiness"; government by consent; right to alter or abolish government.
- **Constitutionalism and limited government**; fundamental rights chapters (Pakistan's Articles 8–28, including property, Arts 23–24).
- "No taxation without representation"; parliamentary control of budgets (Art. 73, 80–84 in Pakistan).
- Liberal democracy as such: Fukuyama's "end of history" is in effect Locke's victory.

## PART 4: HOBBES, LOCKE AND ROUSSEAU COMPARED (asked 2017, 2018, 2020, 2021)

| | Hobbes | Locke | Rousseau |
|---|---|---|---|
| Era and event | English Civil War | Glorious Revolution | Pre-revolutionary France |
| Human nature | Selfish, fearful, power-seeking | Rational, sociable, moral | Naturally good, compassionate; corrupted by society |
| State of nature | War of all against all | Peace and goodwill, with three inconveniences | Idyllic, simple, solitary; later corrupted by property |
| Reason for contract | Fear of death; security | Protect property (life, liberty, estate) | Overcome inequality and dependence; regain freedom through community |
| Parties | Each with each; sovereign outside | Each with each to form society, then trust with government | Each with all; the community itself |
| Rights surrendered | All, except self-preservation | Only the right to judge and punish | All, to the community (total alienation), and received back as citizen rights |
| Sovereign | Absolute monarch or assembly | Limited government; ultimately the people | The people as a whole via the general will |
| Sovereignty | Absolute, indivisible | Limited, divided (legislative supremacy) | Absolute, inalienable, indivisible, cannot be represented |
| Right of revolution | Only self-defence; none collectively | Yes, when trust is broken | People can change government at will; government is only their agent |
| Ideal form | Monarchy | Constitutional, representative government | Direct democracy in a small state |
| Label | Absolutism | Liberalism, constitutionalism | Popular sovereignty, democracy (or "totalitarian democracy") |
| Influence | Realism, security state | US/UK constitutionalism | French Revolution, participatory democracy |

**Common ground:** all three begin with individuals in a state of nature, use consent to explain political obligation, and see the state as man-made (CSS 2026 MCQ: the central point of social contract theory is that the state is a man-made institution).

**General critique of social contract theory**
1. **Unhistorical**: no such contract ever happened (Hume, Bentham called natural rights "nonsense on stilts").
2. **Logically flawed**: a contract presumes the law (that promises must be kept) it is supposed to create.
3. **Wrong view of the state**: Hegel and Burke see the state as an organic, historical growth, not a business deal; Maine: societies moved "from status to contract", not the reverse.
4. **Dangerous**: can justify revolution (Locke) or tyranny (Hobbes).

**Value today:** consent as the basis of legitimacy; government as a trust; Rawls revived the contract as a thought experiment (*A Theory of Justice*, 1971).

## PART 5: MONTESQUIEU (1689–1755)

### 5.1 Life and context

- Charles-Louis de Secondat, **Baron de La Brède et de Montesquieu**, a French noble and magistrate; president of the *parlement* (high court) of Bordeaux.
- Lived under the absolute monarchy of Louis XIV and Louis XV.
- Travelled through Europe; lived in **England** (1729–31) and admired its constitution after 1688.

### 5.2 Key works

*Persian Letters* (1721): satire of French society through two Persian visitors. *Considerations on the Causes of the Greatness of the Romans and Their Decline* (1734). **The Spirit of the Laws** (1748): his masterpiece, put on the Catholic Index in 1751.

### 5.3 Method: why "the Aristotle of the 18th century" (asked 2020, 2024)

- **Empirical, comparative and historical**, like Aristotle: he compared the laws of many societies (ancient, European, Asian, American) to find general causes.
- **Classification of governments** in the manner of Aristotle.
- **Relativism**: laws must fit the people—like Aristotle's "best practicable" state.
- **Moderation and the mixed constitution**: like Aristotle's polity.
- **Causes of decline and revolution** (Romans) like Aristotle's Book V.
- **Founder of sociology of law** and of comparative politics in the modern era.

Differences: Montesquieu thinks of large nation-states and commerce, not the polis; he stresses liberty, not virtue.

### 5.4 The "spirit of the laws"

Laws are "the necessary relations arising from the nature of things". Each nation's laws must suit its **physical** causes (climate, soil, size) and **moral** causes (religion, commerce, customs, manners, history). Together these form the nation's **general spirit**.

**Climate theory**: hot climates make people passive and prone to despotism; cold climates make them vigorous and free; temperate climates suit moderate government. (Heavily criticised as determinist and ethnocentric.)

### 5.5 Classification of governments

| Form | Nature (who rules) | Principle (what moves it) | Corruption |
|---|---|---|---|
| Republic: democratic | The people as a whole | **Virtue** (love of country and equality) | Loss of equality or extreme equality |
| Republic: aristocratic | Part of the people | **Moderation** (a lesser virtue) | Arbitrary power of the nobles |
| Monarchy | One person ruling by fixed laws, through intermediate powers (nobility, courts) | **Honour** | Ruler destroys the intermediate bodies |
| Despotism | One person ruling by will and caprice | **Fear** | Corrupt by nature |

Republics suit small states, monarchies medium-sized ones, despotism large empires.

### 5.6 Liberty and the separation of powers

- **Political liberty** is "the right to do whatever the laws permit"; for the citizen, it is "tranquillity of mind arising from the opinion each person has of his safety".
- Liberty exists only in **moderate** governments where power is not abused. "Every man invested with power is apt to abuse it." Therefore "power must check power".
- Three powers: **legislative**, **executive** and **judicial**.
  - When legislative and executive are united in one person or body, there is no liberty: tyrannical laws will be executed tyrannically.
  - If judicial power is joined to legislative, the judge becomes legislator: arbitrary control over life and liberty.
  - If judicial power is joined to executive, the judge may act with the violence of an oppressor.
  - If all three are united, "all would be lost".
- **Checks and balances**: a bicameral legislature (nobles and commons); the executive has a veto; the legislature examines how laws are executed and can impeach ministers; judges are drawn from the people (juries) and are "the mouth that pronounces the words of the law".

### 5.7 Influence

- **US Constitution** (1787): James Madison in *Federalist No. 47* calls him "the celebrated Montesquieu" and the "oracle" on separation of powers; three articles for three branches; presidential veto, Senate confirmation, impeachment, judicial review.
- French Declaration of the Rights of Man (1789), Article 16: a society without separation of powers "has no constitution".
- **Pakistan**: the trichotomy of powers is part of the constitution's "salient features" doctrine; the courts have relied on it (e.g. in *Government of Sindh v. Sharaf Faridi*, 1994, separating the judiciary from the executive) [verify case details]; debates on the 26th and 27th Amendments (judicial appointments, the Federal Constitutional Court) are debates about Montesquieu.

### 5.8 Critique

- He **misread England**: by the 18th century the cabinet system fused executive and legislature (Walter Bagehot, *The English Constitution*, 1867: the cabinet is "the efficient secret").
- **Complete separation is impossible** and may cause **deadlock** (US government shutdowns).
- Powers are not equal: the legislature usually dominates in parliamentary systems, the executive in modern states.
- **Climate determinism** and Eurocentric views of "Asian despotism".
- Rousseau: sovereignty is indivisible.

### 5.9 Relevance

- Every democratic constitution rests on his ideas: independent courts, checks on executives.
- Concerns about "executive aggrandisement" (Turkey's 2017 referendum, Hungary) are Montesquieu's concerns.
- Pakistan: judicial independence, parliamentary oversight, military role.

## PART 6: JEAN-JACQUES ROUSSEAU (1712–1778)

### 6.1 Life and context

- Born in **Geneva**, a small Protestant republic; mother died soon after his birth; little formal education. Wandered; moved to Paris (1742); knew Diderot and the *Encyclopédie* circle but later broke with them.
- Won the Dijon Academy prize (1750) arguing that the arts and sciences had corrupted morals.
- His books were burned in Paris and Geneva (1762); he fled; quarrelled with Hume; died near Paris. His remains were moved to the Panthéon in 1794 by the revolutionaries.

### 6.2 Key works

*Discourse on the Arts and Sciences* (1750); *Discourse on the Origin of Inequality* (1755); *Discourse on Political Economy* (1755); **The Social Contract** (1762); *Emile* (1762, on education); *Confessions*; *Considerations on the Government of Poland* (1772).

### 6.3 Human nature and the state of nature

- Natural man is **good**, simple, solitary, free, with two instincts: **self-love** (*amour de soi*, healthy self-preservation) and **pity** (compassion). (The phrase "noble savage" is not his, but expresses the idea.)
- Society corrupted him: **amour-propre** (vanity, comparison with others) grew with settled life.
- **Private property** is the root of inequality: "The first man who, having enclosed a piece of ground, bethought himself of saying 'This is mine', and found people simple enough to believe him, was the real founder of civil society" (paraphrase). Property led to rich and poor, then to a fraudulent contract by which the rich created law and government to protect their wealth.

### 6.4 The problem of the *Social Contract*

"Man is born free, and everywhere he is in chains." The problem: "to find a form of association which will defend and protect with the whole common force the person and goods of each associate, and in which each, while uniting himself with all, may still obey himself alone, and remain as free as before".

### 6.5 The contract

- "Each of us puts his person and all his power in common under the supreme direction of the **general will**, and, in our corporate capacity, we receive each member as an indivisible part of the whole."
- **Total alienation**: each gives all to all, so no one gives himself to anyone in particular, and conditions are equal for all.
- Result: a **moral and collective body**, the sovereign people. Natural liberty is replaced by **civil liberty** and **moral liberty** ("obedience to a law we prescribe to ourselves is liberty").

### 6.6 The general will

- The **general will** aims at the **common good**; it is the will each person has as a citizen.
- It differs from the **will of all**, which is the sum of private interests. Take away the pluses and minuses that cancel out, and the general will remains.
- The general will "is always right" (always aims at the public good), though the people can be deceived.
- **Factions** and partial associations distort it; if they exist, there should be many and equal.
- Whoever refuses to obey the general will "shall be **forced to be free**".

### 6.7 Sovereignty

Rousseau's sovereignty belongs to the people and is:
- **Inalienable**: cannot be transferred.
- **Indivisible**: cannot be split among branches.
- **Infallible**: the general will cannot err in aim.
- **Absolute** over the common concerns.
- **Unrepresentable**: deputies are only agents. "The English people think they are free; they are free only during the election of members of Parliament."

This is the theory of **popular sovereignty** (CSS 2024 MCQ).

### 6.8 Government, legislator, civil religion

- **Government** is distinct from the sovereign: an intermediary body that executes the general will. Its form (democracy, aristocracy, monarchy) depends on size. Periodic assemblies should ask whether to keep the form and the officials.
- **The Legislator**: an extraordinary figure (like Lycurgus or Moses) who frames the first laws, persuading by divine authority rather than force.
- **Civil religion**: a few simple dogmas (God, afterlife, sanctity of the contract and laws, tolerance) required of citizens for social unity.
- Ideal: a **small state** where citizens can assemble (Geneva, Corsica).

### 6.9 Critique

- **Vagueness** of the general will: who decides what it is? It can be claimed by a dictator or a party.
- **Totalitarian potential**: J.L. Talmon (*The Origins of Totalitarian Democracy*, 1952) and Isaiah Berlin saw in "forced to be free" the seed of the Jacobin Terror and modern totalitarianism.
- Unworkable in large states; ignores representation.
- Self-contradictions: a lover of freedom who requires civil religion and banishment of unbelievers.
- Romantic, unhistorical state of nature.

Defence: he aimed at self-government and equality; he gave democracy its moral foundation (Kant, Hegel and Marx all built on him).

### 6.10 Do you agree with his rationale? (asked 2025)

Strong points: legitimacy needs the people's will; equality is a condition of freedom; citizens' duty to the common good; warnings about factions and inequality fit today. Weak points: unrepresentability; danger of majoritarian or leader-claimed "general will"; total alienation. A balanced answer accepts popular sovereignty and the moral idea of citizenship but insists on rights, pluralism and representation as checks.

### 6.11 Relevance

- **French Revolution**: Declaration of 1789, Art. 6: "Law is the expression of the general will."
- **Referendums and direct democracy** (Switzerland; Brexit 2016).
- **Populism**: leaders who claim to embody "the people's will" against courts and parliaments.
- **Pakistan**: the Objectives Resolution (1949) places sovereignty in Allah, exercised by the people "within the limits prescribed by Him": a contrast with Rousseau's purely popular sovereignty. Local government and participatory democracy echo his ideas.
