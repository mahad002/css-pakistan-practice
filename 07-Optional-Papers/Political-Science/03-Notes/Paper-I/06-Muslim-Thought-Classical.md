# 06. Classical Muslim Political Thought: al-Farabi, al-Mawardi, Ibn Rushd, al-Ghazali, Ibn Taymiyyah, Nizam ul Mulk Tusi, Ibn Khaldun

Asked: Ibn Khaldun 2017, 2018, 2020 (note), 2021, 2025, 2026 (note); al-Mawardi 2016, 2022; al-Ghazali 2023, 2026; al-Farabi 2022; Tusi 2024. Ibn Rushd and Ibn Taymiyyah: MCQs only so far. Muslim thought gives you **one long question every year**: it is safe ground if prepared.

**Three traditions to keep in mind** (Rosenthal, Enayat, Sherwani):

| Tradition | Starting point | Thinkers here |
|---|---|---|
| **Philosophical** (*falsafa*) | Greek philosophy (Plato, Aristotle) harmonised with Islam | al-Farabi, Ibn Rushd |
| **Juristic / theological** (*fiqh*, *kalam*) | Quran, Sunnah, consensus: the caliphate's legal rules | al-Mawardi, al-Ghazali, Ibn Taymiyyah |
| **Statecraft / "mirrors for princes"** (*adab*, *nasihat al-muluk*) | Practical advice to rulers, Persian court tradition | Nizam ul Mulk Tusi (and al-Ghazali's *Nasihat al-Muluk*) |
| **Historical-sociological** | Empirical study of society and dynasties | Ibn Khaldun |

**Background.** The Rashidun caliphate (632–661) is the ideal. Then Umayyad (661–750) and Abbasid (750–1258) dynasties turned the caliphate into hereditary kingship. From the mid-10th century, the Abbasid caliph in Baghdad lost real power to military dynasties: the Shia **Buyids** (945–1055), then the Sunni **Seljuk Turks** (1055 onwards). The Shia **Fatimids** in Cairo (909–1171) claimed a rival caliphate. The **Mongols** sacked Baghdad in 1258. Muslim thinkers had to answer: how can an institution be legitimate when the real power lies elsewhere?

## 1. AL-FARABI (c. 870–950)

### Life and context
- Abu Nasr Muhammad al-Farabi, probably born near Farab (Otrar) in Central Asia, of Turkic origin (some say Faryab, Afghanistan). Studied in **Baghdad** under Christian Aristotelian teachers. Spent his last years at the court of the Hamdanid ruler **Sayf al-Dawla** in Aleppo; died in Damascus (950).
- Known as **al-Mu'allim al-Thani**, "the **Second Teacher**" (after Aristotle, the First), for preserving and explaining Aristotle (CSS 2026 MCQ).
- Lived during the fragmentation of the Abbasid state and the rise of the Buyids.

### Key works
**Ara Ahl al-Madina al-Fadila** (*The Opinions of the People of the Virtuous City*); **al-Siyasa al-Madaniyya** (*The Political Regime*); *Tahsil al-Sa'ada* (*Attainment of Happiness*); *Ihsa al-Ulum* (*Enumeration of the Sciences*); *Fusul al-Madani* (*Aphorisms of the Statesman*).

### Core ideas
- **Man is social**: no one can reach perfection alone; cooperation is needed for survival and for happiness.
- **Kinds of society**:
  - *Perfect* (complete): **great** (the whole inhabited world), **medium** (a nation), **small** (a city).
  - *Imperfect*: village, quarter, street, household.
  - The city is the smallest unit that can reach true happiness.
- **The virtuous city** (*al-madina al-fadila*): its members cooperate to achieve true happiness (*sa'ada*). Like a healthy body, each part has a function, and the **heart** (the ruler) governs all. A virtuous nation and a virtuous world are possible.
- **The first ruler**: a **philosopher-prophet** who receives knowledge from the **Active Intellect** through reason (as a philosopher) and through imagination (as a prophet). He is the source of law and religion.
- **Twelve qualities of the ruler** (natural dispositions): sound body; good understanding; good memory; intelligence; eloquence; love of learning; moderation in food, drink and sex; love of truth and hatred of lies; magnanimity (greatness of soul); indifference to wealth; love of justice and hatred of oppression; firmness and courage.
- If all qualities are not found in one person: rule by **two** (one with wisdom, one with the other qualities) or by a **council** of virtuous persons. Later rulers ("rulers of the sunna") follow the laws of the first ruler and use reasoning to apply them.
- **Non-virtuous cities**:
  - **Ignorant cities** (*jahiliyya*), which do not know true happiness: city of **necessity** (bare needs); of **wealth** (meanness, *nadhala*); of **pleasure** (baseness, *khissa*); of **honour** (*karama*, like timocracy); of **domination/conquest** (*taghallub*); and the **democratic** or "collective" city (*jama'iyya*), where each does as he likes. He thought the democratic city the most pleasant and varied, and the one from which a virtuous city could most easily emerge, but also the most disordered.
  - **Wicked (immoral) city** (*fasiqa*): knows true happiness but acts like an ignorant city.
  - **Changed city** (*mubaddala*): was once virtuous but changed.
  - **Erring city** (*dalla*): has false beliefs about happiness, led by a deceiver.

### Critique
- Too Greek: Plato's philosopher-king in Islamic dress; prophecy explained philosophically, which theologians (including al-Ghazali) attacked.
- Utopian and elitist; says almost nothing about the actual caliphate.
- Still: the first systematic Muslim political philosophy; influenced Ibn Sina, Ibn Rushd and Maimonides.

### Relevance
Rule by the virtuous and knowledgeable; the idea of a world community; politics aimed at happiness, not just order.

## 2. AL-MAWARDI (972–1058)

### Life and context
- Abu al-Hasan Ali al-Mawardi, born in **Basra**; a **Shafi'i** jurist; chief judge (*qadi*) in Baghdad; diplomat for the Abbasid caliphs al-Qadir and al-Qa'im with the **Buyid** amirs and the Seljuks.
- The caliph had become a figurehead under Shia Buyid amirs; the Fatimids in Cairo and the Umayyads of Cordoba claimed caliphates. Mawardi's aim: **restore the caliph's legal authority** and give a legal place to the real power-holders.

### Key works
**al-Ahkam al-Sultaniyya** (*The Ordinances of Government*): the first systematic treatise on the constitutional law of the caliphate (CSS 2025 MCQ: he "collected all the ordinances relating to public law… in one volume"). Also *Adab al-Dunya wa al-Din*, *Qawanin al-Wizara*, *Nasihat al-Muluk*.

### Theory of the Imamate (asked 2016)
1. **Necessity**: the Imamate (caliphate) is necessary by **revelation (shari'a)**, not just by reason (against the Mu'tazila). It is a collective obligation (*fard kifaya*).
2. **Purpose**: "succession to prophethood in protecting religion and managing worldly affairs".
3. **Methods of appointment**:
   - **Election** by the **electors** (*ahl al-hall wa al-'aqd*, "those who loose and bind"). He discusses how many are needed: some say five (as with Abu Bakr), some three, some even **one** (as al-Abbas offered to give bay'a to Ali).
   - **Nomination** (*'ahd*) by the reigning Imam, as Abu Bakr nominated Umar; Umar's council of six is cited as a valid form.
4. **Qualifications of electors** (3): justice (*'adala*), knowledge to judge who is fit, wisdom and judgement.
5. **Qualifications of the Imam** (7): (i) justice; (ii) knowledge enabling *ijtihad*; (iii) sound hearing, sight and speech; (iv) sound limbs; (v) sound judgement for administration and public interest; (vi) courage to defend the territory and fight enemies; (vii) descent from **Quraysh**.
6. **Ten duties of the Imam**: (1) protect the faith as established by consensus; (2) enforce legal judgments and settle disputes; (3) maintain public order and security; (4) enforce the *hudud* (prescribed penalties); (5) defend the frontiers; (6) wage jihad against those who resist Islam after invitation; (7) collect *fay'* and *zakat* lawfully; (8) pay salaries and allowances from the treasury fairly and on time; (9) appoint trustworthy and competent officials; (10) **personally supervise** affairs rather than rely wholly on delegates.
7. **Only one Imam** at a time (he rejected two simultaneous caliphs).
8. **Deposition / loss of office**: through loss of **justice** (moral corruption, following passions, heresy) or **physical defects** (loss of mind, blindness, etc.), or **captivity**. But he gives **no procedure** for removing an Imam.
9. **The vizierate**: *wizarat al-tafwid* (delegated vizier with broad authority) and *wizarat al-tanfidh* (executive vizier who only carries out orders).
10. **Amirate by seizure** (*imarat al-istila*): when a governor seizes a province by force, the caliph may confirm him if he recognises the caliph, upholds the shari'a and religion and keeps unity. This **legalised the de facto power** of the Buyid and other amirs.

### The "executive" in Mawardi
The Imam is the chief executive, judge and commander, bound by the shari'a; he delegates to viziers, governors and judges. Power is legitimised by contract (*bay'a*) between the Imam and the community through the electors.

### Critique
- **Legitimises the status quo**: one elector, nomination and amirate by seizure make almost any ruler legal.
- **Quraysh condition** is not Quranic and excludes most Muslims.
- **No procedure to depose** an unjust ruler: the people have no real role.
- Theory written to bolster a weak caliph rather than describe reality.
- Defence: realism; a constitutional framework; duties of rulers and accountability under shari'a; separation of executive offices.

### Relevance
Concepts of *bay'a* (contract), *ahl al-hall wa al-'aqd* (an elite electoral college, compared with parliaments), duties of the state (security, justice, welfare, revenue, defence) still match modern state functions. Pakistan's Islamic provisions (Articles 2A, 31, 227) draw on this tradition.

## 3. IBN RUSHD (AVERROES) (1126–1198)

### Life and context
- Abu al-Walid Muhammad ibn Rushd, born in **Cordoba**, Andalusia, to a family of Maliki judges. Himself chief judge (*qadi*) of Seville and Cordoba, and court physician to the **Almohad** caliphs.
- Commissioned by the Almohad caliph Abu Ya'qub Yusuf to write commentaries on Aristotle: hence "**the Commentator**" in medieval Europe.
- Fell from favour in 1195: books burned, exiled to Lucena; later restored; died in Marrakesh (1198).

### Key works
*Fasl al-Maqal* (*The Decisive Treatise* on the harmony of religion and philosophy); **Tahafut al-Tahafut** (*The Incoherence of the Incoherence*, a reply to al-Ghazali); *Commentary on Plato's Republic* (Aristotle's *Politics* was not available to him); *Bidayat al-Mujtahid* (comparative fiqh).

### Core ideas
- **Harmony of religion and philosophy**: "truth does not contradict truth". The Quran commands reflection; where a text seems to contradict demonstrated truth, it should be **interpreted** (*ta'wil*).
- **Three classes of people** by ways of assent: the **demonstrative** (philosophers, by proof), the **dialectical** (theologians, by argument) and the **rhetorical** (the masses, by persuasion and imagery). Each should be addressed in its own way.
- **Political ideas** (from the *Republic* commentary):
  - Accepts Plato's ideal state, with the philosopher, lawgiver and Imam as one figure.
  - Applies Plato's decline of states to Islamic history: the early (Rashidun) caliphate approached the virtuous state; **Mu'awiya** turned it into a timocracy/hereditary rule; later dynasties in Andalusia moved towards rule for wealth and tyranny.
  - **Women**: women can do the same tasks as men, including government and war; their confinement to childbearing and domestic roles makes them a burden and is a cause of poverty in Muslim cities. Remarkable for his time.
  - The shari'a is the best law, as it combines what serves the masses with what serves the elite.
  - Justice and the rule of law; condemns tyranny.

### Critique
Elitist; Greek-dependent; little influence in the Muslim world after his death compared with his vast influence in Europe (Latin Averroism, Thomas Aquinas, the Renaissance).

### Relevance
Reason and revelation; tolerance of interpretation; women's role in society; critique of hereditary and tyrannical rule.

## 4. AL-GHAZALI (1058–1111)

### Life and context
- Abu Hamid Muhammad al-Ghazali, born in **Tus**, Khorasan. A Shafi'i jurist and Ash'ari theologian. Appointed in 1091 by the vizier **Nizam ul Mulk** as professor at the **Nizamiyya** madrasa in Baghdad, the most prestigious post in the Sunni world.
- In 1095 a spiritual crisis left him unable to speak; he gave up his post, lived as a Sufi in Damascus and Jerusalem, made the Hajj, returned to Tus. Titled **Hujjat al-Islam** ("Proof of Islam").
- Context: **Seljuk sultans** held real power; the Abbasid caliph al-Mustazhir was a figurehead; the **Batinis** (Nizari Isma'ilis) threatened Sunni order and assassinated Nizam ul Mulk (1092); the **First Crusade** took Jerusalem (1099).

### Key works
**Ihya Ulum al-Din** (*Revival of the Religious Sciences*); *Tahafut al-Falasifa* (*The Incoherence of the Philosophers*, 1095); *al-Munqidh min al-Dalal* (*Deliverance from Error*, autobiography); *Fada'ih al-Batiniyya* (also called *al-Mustazhiri*); *al-Iqtisad fi al-I'tiqad*; **Nasihat al-Muluk** (*Counsel for Kings*, in Persian); *Kimiya-yi Sa'adat*.

### Origin of the state (asked 2026)
1. **Man is social by nature**: created needing food, clothing and shelter, he cannot meet these needs alone.
2. This creates **cooperation** and **division of labour**: basic crafts are agriculture, weaving, building and **politics** (the art of coordinating the others).
3. Living together produces **competition and conflict** over scarce goods.
4. Therefore a **ruler** and **law** are needed to keep order, settle disputes and enforce justice.
5. Religion needs the state: "**Religion and temporal power are twins**: religion is the foundation and the sultan is the guardian; what has no foundation collapses, and what has no guardian is lost" (paraphrase of the famous line).
6. Order in this world is a precondition for religious life and salvation in the next; so the state is necessary by the **shari'a** (as a religious necessity), not merely by reason.

**Features of the state** in his view: natural and necessary; moral and religious purpose; based on cooperation; ruler as guardian of religion; justice as its foundation; aims at welfare in this world and the hereafter.

### Theory of the caliphate and qualifications (asked 2023)
- The caliphate is necessary; it is the symbol of Sunni unity and the source of legitimacy.
- **Realism**: since real power (*shawka*) lies with the Seljuk sultan, the caliph is appointed by the **bay'a of the holder of power** (the sultan). The caliph legitimises the sultan; the sultan protects the caliph. A tripartite arrangement: caliph (legitimacy), sultan (power), ulama (law).
- **Ten qualifications** of the caliph: maturity (adulthood); sanity; freedom (not a slave); male sex; **Qurayshi** descent; sound hearing and sight; **power** (*najda/shawka*); **administrative capacity** (*kifaya*); **piety** (*wara'*); **knowledge** (*'ilm*).
- He **relaxed** the knowledge and ijtihad conditions: if the caliph lacks learning, he may consult the ulama; if he lacks power, the sultan supplies it. Better an imperfect caliph than none.
- **Obedience** even to an unjust ruler, to avoid *fitna* (civil strife): "the tyranny of a sultan for a hundred years causes less damage than one year's tyranny of the subjects over one another" (paraphrase of a common saying he endorses in spirit). Revolt is allowed only against open unbelief.

### Duties of the ruler (*Nasihat al-Muluk*)
Justice above all ("the sultan is God's shadow on earth" and must be just); fear of God; seek the company of pious ulama and hear their advice; listen to the complaints of the oppressed; avoid pride, anger and luxury; supervise officials; do not seize property; be gentle; know that power is a trust for which he will answer on the Day of Judgement.

### Critique
- **Legitimises de facto power** and despotism; quietism; no right of revolt.
- His attack on the philosophers (*Tahafut*) is blamed by some for the decline of rationalism in the Muslim world (a disputed claim; others say he made logic part of mainstream theology).
- Quraysh condition.
- Defence: realism in a crisis; preserved unity; insisted on justice and accountability before God; methodic doubt anticipates Descartes.

### Relevance
Religion–state relationship in Muslim countries (Pakistan's Objectives Resolution, Council of Islamic Ideology); the order-versus-justice dilemma; ethics of rulers.

## 5. IBN TAYMIYYAH (1263–1328)

### Life and context
- Taqi al-Din Ahmad ibn Taymiyyah, born in **Harran** (now south-east Turkey). His family fled the **Mongols** to Damascus in 1269. A **Hanbali** jurist and theologian.
- Lived under the **Mamluk** sultanate (Egypt and Syria). The Mongol Ilkhanids invaded Syria repeatedly; though they had converted to Islam (Ghazan Khan, 1295), they ruled partly by the Mongol *Yasa* code. Ibn Taymiyyah issued fatwas declaring war against them lawful and took part in the battle of Shaqhab (1303).
- Imprisoned several times in Cairo, Alexandria and Damascus for his views; died in the Damascus citadel prison (1328).

### Key works
**al-Siyasa al-Shar'iyya fi Islah al-Ra'i wa al-Ra'iyya** (*Governance According to God's Law in Reforming Both the Ruler and the Ruled*); *Minhaj al-Sunna*; *al-Hisba fi al-Islam*; *Majmu' al-Fatawa*.

### Core ideas
- **The purpose of the state** is to enforce the shari'a, establish justice, command good and forbid evil (*al-amr bi al-ma'ruf wa al-nahy 'an al-munkar*). Government is a religious duty and "one of the greatest obligations of religion"; without it religion cannot be upheld.
- **Form does not matter; shari'a does.** The true caliphate lasted thirty years after the Prophet (a hadith he relies on); later rule is kingship (*mulk*). He did not insist on a single universal caliph or on Qurayshi descent; a plurality of Muslim states, each upholding the shari'a, is acceptable.
- **Governance as a trust** (*amana*): based on Quran 4:58–59: "render trusts to whom they are due, and when you judge, judge with justice… obey God, the Messenger and those in authority among you". Rulers must give offices to the **most competent**, measured by **strength** (*quwwa*, ability) and **trustworthiness** (*amana*, integrity); appointing for kinship or bribes betrays God.
- **Those in authority** (*ulu al-amr*) are both the **rulers** and the **ulama**; they must cooperate.
- **Obedience** to the ruler is required except in sin; rebellion causes worse harm (he quoted that sixty years under an unjust imam is better than a night without a ruler).
- **Shura** (consultation) is required of rulers.
- **Hisba**: an officer (*muhtasib*) supervises markets, morals and public order.
- **Return to the Quran and Sunnah**; **ijtihad** over blind imitation (*taqlid*); opposition to innovations (*bid'a*) and certain Sufi practices.
- **Jihad** against aggressors and those who refuse to apply the shari'a.

### Critique
Literalism and rigidity; hostility to philosophy and Sufism; his fatwas (e.g. on Mongols and the "Mardin fatwa" on a mixed abode) have been misused by modern militant groups (scholars at a 2010 conference in Mardin argued these misread him).

### Relevance
Major influence on **Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab** (18th c.), the **Salafi** movement and some modern Islamist thinkers; concepts of competence and integrity in public appointment (merit-based civil service) and accountability are widely cited.

## 6. NIZAM UL MULK TUSI (1018–1092)

### Life and context
- Abu Ali Hasan ibn Ali Tusi, born near **Tus**. Grand vizier of the **Seljuk** sultans **Alp Arslan** (r. 1063–72) and **Malik Shah** (r. 1072–92) for about 30 years: the real administrator of a vast empire from Central Asia to Anatolia.
- Founded the **Nizamiyya madrasas** (Baghdad, 1067, and others) to train Sunni scholars and officials against Fatimid/Isma'ili propaganda; appointed al-Ghazali.
- Assassinated in 1092 near Nahavand by a member of the **Nizari Isma'ili** (Assassin) order.

### Key work
**Siyasatnama** (*Book of Government*, also *Siyar al-Muluk*), 50 chapters, written in Persian around 1091–92 at Malik Shah's request: a classic "mirror for princes".

### Theory of kingship (asked 2024)
1. **Divine selection**: "In every age God chooses one of the people and, having endowed him with kingly virtues, entrusts him with the world." The king's authority comes **directly from God** (the Persian idea of divine grace, *farr-i izadi*), not from the caliph or the people.
2. **Moral causation**: when people disobey God's law, God punishes them by removing the good king; disorder, bloodshed and anarchy follow until God chooses a new just king.
3. **Justice** is the foundation: a kingdom can survive unbelief but not oppression (a saying he relays). The king must hold court (*mazalim*) to hear grievances in person, twice a week.
4. **Religion**: the king must follow orthodox (Sunni) religion, honour the ulama, and suppress heresy—especially the Batinis (Isma'ilis), the Mazdakites and other "rebels".
5. **Administration**:
   - Appoint **honest, competent officials**; never give two posts to one man or one post to two men; avoid hereditary office without merit.
   - **Supervise** officials and tax collectors; *iqta'* holders (land-grant holders) have rights only to revenue, not over the peasants' persons and property.
   - **Intelligence**: a network of spies and postmasters (*sahib-i barid*) to report on officials and the provinces.
   - **Army** drawn from many races (Turks, Daylamis, Khurasanis, Georgians…) so that no single group can conspire.
   - Consultation with wise and experienced men; a capable **vizier**.
   - Keep women and favourites out of state affairs (a view now rightly criticised).
   - Avoid haste in punishment; keep the treasury full but not by oppression.

### Critique
- **Absolutism**: the king answers only to God; no institutional limits.
- **Sectarian**: hostile to Shias and Isma'ilis.
- **Misogynistic**.
- Persian (Sassanid) court traditions more than Islamic law (the caliph hardly appears).
- Strength: practical administrative wisdom; anticipates bureaucracy, intelligence services, accountability of officials.

### Comparison
- With **Machiavelli**: both practical advisers; Tusi grounds kingship in God and morality, Machiavelli separates politics from ethics.
- With **Kautilya**: spies, administration, treasury.
- With **al-Mawardi**: Mawardi derives authority from the caliph and shari'a; Tusi from God directly to the king.

### Relevance
Merit-based civil service, intelligence and accountability mechanisms, grievance redress (today's ombudsmen; Pakistan's Wafaqi Mohtasib and Prime Minister's Citizen Portal echo the *mazalim* court).

## 7. IBN KHALDUN (1332–1406)

### Life and context
- Abd al-Rahman ibn Khaldun, born in **Tunis** to an Arab family that had emigrated from **Seville**. Lost both parents to the **Black Death** (1348–49).
- A turbulent career in the courts of North Africa and Spain: secretary and minister at Fez (Marinids), envoy from Granada to Pedro the Cruel of Castile (1364), chamberlain in Bougie; often imprisoned or in flight as dynasties rose and fell.
- Retired to the castle of **Qal'at Ibn Salama** (in present-day Algeria) where he wrote the **Muqaddimah** (first draft completed **1377**).
- Moved to **Cairo** (1382), taught and served several times as **Maliki chief judge**. In 1401 he met **Timur** (Tamerlane) outside besieged Damascus.
- He saw at first hand the cycle of rise and fall of North African dynasties.

### Key work
*Kitab al-'Ibar* (*Book of Lessons*, a universal history). Its introduction, the **Muqaddimah** (*Prolegomena*), is his political and social theory.

### Method: a "new science"
- **'Ilm al-'umran**: the science of human social organisation and civilisation. History must be judged by the **nature of society**, not just by the reliability of narrators.
- **Sources of error** in historians: partisanship; overconfidence in sources; failure to understand what is intended; mistaken belief in the truth; ignorance of context; the wish to win favour with the powerful; and, above all, **ignorance of the nature of social organisation**.
- Hence he is called a founder of **sociology**, **historiography** and **economics** (Arnold Toynbee called the Muqaddimah "undoubtedly the greatest work of its kind that has ever yet been created by any mind").

### Origin of the state
- Man is social; he needs cooperation for food and defence.
- Human aggressiveness makes a **restraining authority** (*wazi'*) necessary; this develops into **royal authority** (*mulk*).
- The group with the strongest **asabiyya** wins royal authority.

### Asabiyya (asked 2018, 2020, 2026)
- **Definition**: group feeling, social solidarity, the cohesion that makes a group act together, defend each other and pursue power.
- **Sources**: blood kinship (most natural), clientship and alliance, long common life; strengthened by **religion**, which removes rivalry and envy and focuses the group on one goal ("religion adds to the power of asabiyya"). The early Arab Muslims are his example.
- **Strongest among nomads** (*badawa*): desert life makes people brave, self-reliant, simple, and dependent on each other. **Sedentary** life (*hadara*) in cities brings luxury, specialisation, softness and reliance on walls and mercenaries, which erode asabiyya.
- **Role**: asabiyya is the engine of state formation; its decline is the cause of state decay. The leader must keep it alive; once he tries to rule alone, it weakens.

### The cycle of dynasties (asked 2017, 2021, 2025)

**Lifespan**: a dynasty usually lasts about **three generations** (roughly **120 years**, about 40 years each):
1. **The builders**: keep desert virtues and strong asabiyya; share power with the group.
2. **The maintainers**: lived with the builders; imitate them, but by tradition rather than experience.
3. **The destroyers**: born in luxury, lose asabiyya entirely; think they deserve power by birth; the dynasty falls.

**Five stages**:
1. **Success and conquest**: the ruler is one of the group; shares glory and power.
2. **Autocracy**: the ruler gains complete control, excludes his kinsmen, relies on clients and mercenaries.
3. **Leisure and tranquillity**: enjoyment of the fruits of power: tax collection, building, monuments, generosity.
4. **Contentment and peacefulness**: imitation of predecessors; conservative, no innovation.
5. **Waste and squandering**: extravagance, bad advisers, oppressive taxes, alienation of the army and people; senility; the state is overthrown by a new group with fresh asabiyya, usually from the periphery.

*Diagram in words:* draw a circle with five arrows: Conquest → Autocracy → Leisure → Contentment → Waste, and an arrow from Waste back to "New group with strong asabiyya" → Conquest. Mark "asabiyya high" at the top-left and "asabiyya low" at the bottom-right.

### Internal versus external factors (asked 2021)
Ibn Khaldun admits that a dynasty can be conquered from outside, but this happens only when it is already **weak inside**. Internal factors:
- Decline of asabiyya (luxury, sedentary life, exclusion of the original group).
- **Luxury** and rising expenditure.
- **Oppressive taxation**: at the start, low rates on many people yield high revenue; at the end, high rates on a shrinking economy yield little (an idea later compared to the **Laffer curve**).
- Injustice (*zulm*) "ruins civilisation": seizing property kills the incentive to work.
- Reliance on **mercenaries** and clients.
- The ruler's isolation and suspicion of his own kin.
- The state's entry into trade, competing with subjects.
- Senility: "a natural lifespan like that of an individual".

### State and government (asked 2025)
**Three kinds of rule**:
| Type | Basis | Aim |
|---|---|---|
| **Natural kingship** (*mulk tabi'i*) | The ruler's desires and force | Ruler's interest; tyrannical |
| **Political / rational rule** (*siyasa 'aqliyya*) | Laws based on reason | Worldly interests of subjects (like Persian kingship) |
| **Caliphate** (*khilafa*) | The shari'a | Welfare in **this world and the next**; the best form |

- The Rashidun caliphate was the true caliphate; it gradually turned into kingship under the Umayyads, first while keeping religious aims, then losing them.
- The **state** (*dawla*) is the dynasty and its apparatus; the **city** and **civilisation** depend on it. When the state falls, its cities decline.
- **Functions of government**: defence, justice, protection of property, public works, coinage, the vizierate, finance, the chancery (*diwan*), police (*shurta*), army.

### Economic ideas
Labour as the source of value and wealth; division of labour raises productivity; supply and demand; low taxes encourage enterprise; state commerce harms the economy; large cities are richer because of division of labour.

### Critique
- **Cyclical determinism**: not every state follows three generations; modern bureaucratic and constitutional states have lasted centuries.
- Built on **North African** Berber and Arab dynasties; may not generalise.
- **Environmental determinism** (climate shapes character).
- Little room for ideas and individuals beyond asabiyya and religion.
- Strength: empirical, causal, sociological analysis four centuries before Montesquieu, Vico and Comte.

### Relevance
- **Pakistani politics**: dynastic parties rise on group solidarity and decline in the third generation; the military's institutional cohesion as a form of asabiyya; elite luxury and tax burdens on a narrow base; overtaxation and the IMF; Ibn Khaldun's "injustice ruins civilisation" fits debates on rule of law and investment.
- **Nation-building**: national cohesion as modern asabiyya (Ernest Gellner and Peter Turchin used his ideas).
- **Arab Spring** and state collapse; the Taliban's comeback from the periphery (2021) as a fresh asabiyya defeating a weak, aid-dependent state.
- **Economics**: taxation and the Laffer curve.
