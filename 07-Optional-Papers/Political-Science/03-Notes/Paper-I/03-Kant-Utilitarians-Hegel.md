# 03. Kant, the Utilitarians (Bentham and Mill) and Hegel

Asked: Hegel 2019, 2020 (same question), 2025 (Hegel vs Marx); Bentham 2023; utilitarianism 2026. Kant and Mill have not yet been the subject of a long question; both are strong candidates for 2028.

**Context.** These thinkers wrote after the Enlightenment and during the French Revolution (1789), the Napoleonic wars and the Industrial Revolution. The questions changed: how should reason ground morality and law (Kant)? How should law and policy produce welfare (Bentham)? How much may society interfere with the individual (Mill)? Does history have a direction (Hegel)?

---

## PART 1: IMMANUEL KANT (1724–1804)

### 1.1 Life and context

- Born, lived and died in **Königsberg**, East Prussia (now Kaliningrad, Russia). Never travelled far. Professor of logic and metaphysics.
- Admired Rousseau ("Rousseau set me right") and welcomed the American and French revolutions in principle, while denying any right to revolt.
- Wrote under Frederick the Great, an "enlightened despot", and suffered censorship under his successor.

### 1.2 Key works

*Critique of Pure Reason* (1781); "What is Enlightenment?" (1784); *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* (1785); *Critique of Practical Reason* (1788); **Perpetual Peace** (1795); *The Metaphysics of Morals* (1797, includes the *Doctrine of Right*).

### 1.3 Core ideas

**(a) Enlightenment.** "Man's emergence from his self-imposed immaturity." Motto: *Sapere aude* ("dare to know"). Freedom of public reason is its condition.

**(b) Moral philosophy: the categorical imperative.** Morality comes from reason, not consequences or feelings. Duty must be done for its own sake (**deontology**).
- *Formula of universal law*: act only on a rule you could will to become a universal law.
- *Formula of humanity*: treat humanity, in yourself or others, always as an end and never merely as a means.
- *Autonomy*: a rational being gives the moral law to himself.

**(c) Right and the state.** The purpose of the state is not happiness but **freedom under law**: "the restriction of each individual's freedom so that it harmonises with the freedom of everyone else". The state rests on an "original contract", not as history but as an **idea of reason**: a test that a law is legitimate if the whole people *could* have agreed to it.

**(d) Republicanism.** The best constitution is **republican**: freedom of members as men, dependence of all on a single common legislation as subjects, and equality as citizens. Executive and legislative must be separated. Republic ≠ democracy: Kant feared direct democracy as despotic. Only independent property-owning adults are "active citizens" with the vote.

**(e) No right of revolution.** Citizens may criticise (freedom of the pen) but must obey; rebellion destroys the legal order. Yet once a revolution succeeds, the new order must be obeyed.

**(f) Perpetual Peace (1795).** A "philosophical sketch" in the form of a treaty.

Six *preliminary* articles (steps):
1. No peace treaty with secret reservations for a future war.
2. No independent state may be acquired by another by inheritance, exchange, purchase or gift.
3. Standing armies shall gradually be abolished.
4. No national debts for external wars.
5. No forcible interference in another state's constitution and government.
6. No acts of war that make future trust impossible (assassins, poisoners, breach of surrender, incitement to treason).

Three *definitive* articles:
1. The civil constitution of every state shall be **republican** (citizens who bear the costs of war will be cautious about it).
2. The law of nations shall be founded on a **federation of free states** (*foedus pacificum*), not a world state.
3. **Cosmopolitan right** shall be limited to universal **hospitality**: a right to visit without hostility, not a right to settle.

Nature itself, through commerce and the destructiveness of war, pushes humanity towards peace ("unsocial sociability").

### 1.4 Critique

- Hegel: his ethics is **empty formalism**; it gives no concrete content.
- Rigid: no lying even to a murderer at the door.
- Restricted citizenship (excludes women, servants, dependants).
- Denying the right of revolution sits awkwardly with his support for republican freedom.
- Realists: perpetual peace is utopian; federations collapse (League of Nations).

### 1.5 Relevance

- **Democratic peace theory**: Michael Doyle (1983) and others found that liberal democracies rarely, if ever, fight each other, citing Kant.
- **League of Nations, UN, EU**: federations of free states.
- **Human dignity**: Article 1 of Germany's Basic Law ("Human dignity shall be inviolable"); human rights law.
- Cosmopolitan right and the refugee debate.
- Rawls is a Kantian.

## PART 2: JEREMY BENTHAM (1748–1832)

### 2.1 Life and context

- Born in London; child prodigy (Oxford at 12); trained as a lawyer but turned to legal reform after hearing William Blackstone's lectures on English common law, which he found confused and complacent.
- Led the **Philosophical Radicals**, whose reforms influenced the Great Reform Act (1832), the Poor Law (1834) and prison and legal reform.
- Designed the **Panopticon**, a circular prison where a hidden inspector can see every cell.
- Left his body to science; his preserved skeleton, dressed and seated (the "auto-icon"), is at University College London.

### 2.2 Key works

*A Fragment on Government* (1776); **An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation** (printed 1780, published 1789); *Anarchical Fallacies* (attack on the French Declaration of Rights); *Constitutional Code* (1830).

### 2.3 Core ideas

**(a) Psychological hedonism.** "Nature has placed mankind under the governance of two sovereign masters, pain and pleasure." They determine what we do and what we ought to do.

**(b) The principle of utility.** Actions, laws and institutions are good to the extent that they increase happiness and reduce pain. "It is the greatest happiness of the greatest number that is the measure of right and wrong" (*Fragment*, 1776). The phrase predates him (Francis Hutcheson used a similar form), but Bentham made it a system.

**(c) The felicific (hedonic) calculus.** Pleasures and pains can be measured by seven dimensions: **intensity, duration, certainty, propinquity (nearness), fecundity** (chance of producing more pleasure), **purity** (chance of not being followed by pain) and **extent** (number of people affected).

**(d) Quantity, not quality.** All pleasures are of one kind; "prejudice apart, the game of push-pin is of equal value with the arts and sciences of music and poetry" (paraphrase).

**(e) Each to count for one.** "Everybody to count for one, nobody for more than one" (attributed via Mill).

**(f) Against natural rights and contract.** Natural rights are "simple nonsense: natural and imprescriptible rights, rhetorical nonsense, nonsense upon stilts". Rights are created by law. The social contract is a fiction. Government is justified by utility alone.

**(g) Law and sovereignty.** Law is the **command** of a sovereign backed by sanctions. His follower **John Austin** built analytical jurisprudence and the legal theory of sovereignty on this (see `09-Political-Concepts.md`).

**(h) Ends of legislation.** Subsistence, abundance, security and equality; security comes first.

**(i) Punishment.** Punishment is itself a pain, justified only if it prevents greater pain (deterrence, reform, incapacitation), not as retribution.

**(j) Democracy.** At first a reformer of laws under any government; later a radical democrat: since rulers pursue their own interest, only the people's control makes their interest coincide with the general interest. Supported wide suffrage (eventually including women in principle), secret ballot, annual parliaments, abolition of the monarchy and House of Lords in his *Constitutional Code*.

**(k) Role of the state.** Generally **laissez-faire** in economics (the individual knows his own interest best), but an active, reforming state in law, prisons, health and education. (Utilitarians are sometimes described as favouring limited state action and sometimes the opposite; the honest answer is that utility decides case by case, and later utilitarians moved towards more state action.)

### 2.4 Critique

- Pleasure cannot be measured or compared between persons.
- **Tyranny of the majority**: the happiness of the many can justify sacrificing a minority (e.g. punishing an innocent to calm a mob).
- Ignores **justice and rights** (Rawls: utilitarianism "does not take seriously the distinction between persons").
- Thomas Carlyle called it a "pig philosophy".
- Psychological hedonism is false: people act from duty, love and principle.
- Mill had to revise it (qualitative pleasures).

### 2.5 Explaining "the greatest happiness of the greatest number" (asked 2023)

Structure: meaning → origins (Hutcheson, Helvetius, Beccaria, Priestley) → basis in hedonism → calculus → applications (law, punishment, democracy, Poor Law) → Mill's modification → criticisms (Rawls, minority rights, measurement) → present uses (cost–benefit analysis).

## PART 3: JOHN STUART MILL (1806–1873)

### 3.1 Life and context

- Son of James Mill, Bentham's close ally. Hothouse education: Greek at three, Latin at eight, political economy at 13.
- Mental crisis at 20; recovered by reading Wordsworth: concluded that feelings and culture matter, and happiness is found by not aiming at it directly.
- Worked for the **East India Company** (1823–58), rising to chief examiner of Indian correspondence.
- Long partnership with **Harriet Taylor** (married 1851), whom he credited as co-author of *On Liberty*.
- MP for Westminster (1865–68); in 1867 moved an amendment to give women the vote (defeated).

### 3.2 Key works

*A System of Logic* (1843); *Principles of Political Economy* (1848); **On Liberty** (1859); **Considerations on Representative Government** (1861); **Utilitarianism** (1861, book 1863); **The Subjection of Women** (1869).

### 3.3 Revised utilitarianism

- Pleasures differ in **quality**: intellectual and moral pleasures are higher than bodily ones. "It is better to be a human being dissatisfied than a pig satisfied; better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied."
- The judge of quality: those who have experienced both.
- Justice and rights are the most important parts of utility, protected because security is vital to happiness.

### 3.4 On Liberty

- **Danger**: in democracies the threat is no longer the king but the **tyranny of the majority**, both through law and through social pressure and opinion (borrowed from Tocqueville).
- **The harm principle**: "The only purpose for which power can be rightfully exercised over any member of a civilised community, against his will, is to prevent harm to others." His own good is not a sufficient warrant.
- **Self-regarding** actions (affecting only oneself) are free; **other-regarding** actions can be regulated.
- **Liberty of thought and discussion**, four arguments:
  1. The silenced opinion may be **true**; to deny this is to assume infallibility.
  2. It may be **partly true**; truth emerges from collision of views.
  3. Even if the received view is wholly true, unless contested it is held as a **prejudice**, without understanding.
  4. Its meaning becomes **dead dogma** and loses its effect on conduct.
- **Individuality**: a source of progress; "the despotism of custom" stifles it.
- Limits: liberty applies to adults in "civilised" societies; "despotism is a legitimate mode of government in dealing with barbarians, provided the end be their improvement".

### 3.5 Representative Government

- The ideally best form is **representative government**: it protects people's interests and improves their character by participation.
- Dangers: low intelligence of the legislature and **class legislation** by the majority.
- Remedies:
  - **Proportional representation** (Thomas Hare's single transferable vote) to represent minorities.
  - **Plural voting**: extra votes for the educated.
  - Literacy and tax-paying qualifications for the vote.
  - **Open (public) voting**, because voting is a public trust (he opposed the secret ballot).
  - A small expert commission to draft laws; Parliament to debate and approve.
  - Votes for women.
- Representative government needs preconditions: people willing to accept it, able to preserve it and to do what it requires.

### 3.6 The Subjection of Women (1869)

The legal subordination of women is wrong in itself and a chief obstacle to human improvement; women's supposed nature is the product of education and circumstance. Calls for equal rights in law, marriage, property, education, professions and suffrage.

### 3.7 Critique

- The self/other-regarding line is unclear: almost every act affects others.
- **Elitism**: plural voting and educational qualifications.
- **Imperialism**: despotism for "barbarians" justified the East India Company's rule.
- Departs from utilitarianism: if pleasures differ in quality, utility is no longer the only standard.
- Marxists: his liberty is "bourgeois" liberty; ignores economic power.

### 3.8 Relevance

- **Free speech debates**: hate speech, blasphemy laws, social media regulation. Pakistan's PECA 2016 and its 2025 amendments and the X (Twitter) suspension of 2024–25 can be judged by the harm principle.
- Harm principle in criminal law reform (decriminalising private conduct).
- Proportional representation debates.
- Women's suffrage and equality law.

## PART 4: UTILITARIANISM AND ITS CONTEMPORARY APPLICATIONS (asked 2026)

**Definition.** A consequentialist theory: the right act or policy is the one that produces the greatest overall well-being.

**Types.** *Act* utilitarianism (judge each act); *rule* utilitarianism (follow rules that generally maximise utility); *preference* utilitarianism (Peter Singer: satisfy preferences); *negative* utilitarianism (Popper: minimise suffering).

**Applications today**

| Area | How utilitarian reasoning is used |
|---|---|
| Public policy | **Cost–benefit analysis** of projects and regulations (US OMB rules; Pakistan's PC-1 appraisal and Planning Commission evaluations) |
| Health | Cost per **QALY** (quality-adjusted life year) to decide which drugs to fund (UK NICE); triage; vaccine priority and lockdown decisions during COVID-19 |
| Economics | Welfare economics, GDP, Pareto efficiency; "greatest good" in budget allocation |
| Criminal justice | Deterrence-based sentencing; prison reform; the Panopticon as a model of surveillance (Foucault) |
| Environment | Carbon pricing; discounting future harms; climate cost–benefit models |
| Technology | Self-driving car and AI ethics ("trolley problems"); utility-maximising algorithms |
| Philanthropy | **Effective altruism**: give where it does most good |
| Development | Poverty programmes targeted by impact (BISP's targeting, conditional cash transfers) |
| War | Proportionality and "lesser evil" arguments |

**Critiques today:** Rawls (*A Theory of Justice*, 1971) as the alternative (CSS 2026 MCQ); Nozick (rights as side-constraints); Amartya Sen (capabilities, not utility); minority and rights concerns (e.g. surveillance justified by security); the difficulty of measuring well-being (the World Happiness Report is an attempt).

**Judgement:** utilitarianism is the hidden logic of modern governance and economics, but democracies temper it with rights, constitutions and justice.

## PART 5: G.W.F. HEGEL (1770–1831)

### 5.1 Life and context

- Born in Stuttgart; studied theology at the Tübingen seminary, where his roommates were the poet Hölderlin and the philosopher Schelling. They celebrated the French Revolution.
- Tutor, then journalist and headmaster. At Jena in 1806 he saw Napoleon after the battle and called him "the world-soul on horseback".
- Professor at Heidelberg, then **Berlin** (from 1818), where he became the most influential philosopher in Germany. Died in a cholera epidemic.
- Germany was divided into many states; Prussia was reforming after defeat by Napoleon. Hegel wanted a rational, unified state.

### 5.2 Key works

*Phenomenology of Spirit* (1807); *Science of Logic* (1812–16); *Encyclopaedia of the Philosophical Sciences* (1817); **Elements of the Philosophy of Right** (1820, dated 1821); *Lectures on the Philosophy of History* (published after his death).

### 5.3 Spirit (*Geist*)

- Reality is the self-development of **Spirit** (Mind, Reason, the Absolute Idea). Spirit comes to know itself through nature, human history, culture, religion and philosophy.
- "What is rational is actual, and what is actual is rational."
- History is "the progress of the consciousness of freedom".
  - **Oriental world**: only **one** is free (the despot).
  - **Greek and Roman world**: **some** are free (citizens, not slaves).
  - **Germanic-Christian world**: **all** are free (man as such is free).
- **World-historical individuals** (Alexander, Caesar, Napoleon) serve Spirit's purposes while pursuing their own passions: the **"cunning of reason"**.
- "The owl of Minerva spreads its wings only with the falling of dusk": philosophy understands an age only when it is ending.

### 5.4 The dialectic

- Every idea or form of life contains contradictions. Its **negation** emerges; the conflict is resolved in a higher unity that cancels, preserves and lifts both (*Aufhebung*, "sublation").
- Usually summarised as **thesis → antithesis → synthesis** (Fichte's terms; Hegel spoke of abstract → negative → concrete). The synthesis becomes a new thesis.
- Example: **Being → Nothing → Becoming**. In the *Phenomenology*: the **master–slave dialectic**, a struggle for **recognition** in which the slave, through labour, achieves a truer self-consciousness than the master.

### 5.5 The state (Philosophy of Right)

The development of freedom runs through:
1. **Abstract right**: property, contract, wrong (external freedom).
2. **Morality**: individual conscience and intention (inner freedom).
3. **Ethical life** (*Sittlichkeit*): freedom made concrete in institutions:
   - **Family** (thesis): unity based on love; particular.
   - **Civil society** (antithesis): the "system of needs", market, competition, division of labour; administration of justice, police and corporations. It creates wealth but also poverty and a "rabble".
   - **State** (synthesis): the "actuality of the ethical idea"; unites the universal and the particular; the individual finds his true freedom as a member of the state.

The state is "the march of God in the world" (from students' notes; often quoted as "the march of God on earth"). It is an end in itself, not a mere instrument for individuals.

**Constitution**: a **constitutional monarchy** with three moments: the **crown** (final decision, "dotting the i"), the **executive** (a professional civil service, the "universal class"), and the **legislature** of estates. Public opinion deserves "to be as much respected as despised".

**International relations**: states are sovereign and recognise no judge above them; relations are a state of nature; **war** preserves "the ethical health of peoples". He rejected Kant's perpetual peace.

### 5.6 "The Hegelian spirit is the evolution of human consciousness towards political maturity for global human coexistence" (asked 2019, 2020)

How to answer:
- **Agree in part**: Spirit is humanity's collective self-consciousness; history is the expansion of freedom from one to some to all; this is a story of growing political maturity; the rational state embodies mutual recognition; universal recognition implies ultimately the recognition of all humans.
- **Disagree in part**: Hegel's endpoint is the **nation-state**, not a global order; he accepted war and rejected world government; his scheme is Eurocentric (Africa "no historical part of the world").
- **Later extensions**: Alexandre Kojève's reading led to the idea of a "universal homogeneous state"; **Fukuyama** argued in 1989–92 that liberal democracy is the end point of this dialectic (see `05-Contemporary-Western-Thought.md`); the EU and the UN are sometimes read as institutions of mutual recognition beyond the state.
- **Conclusion**: Hegel gave the idea of history as a progress of consciousness; global coexistence is an extension his followers made, not a claim he held.

### 5.7 Critique

| Critic | Objection |
|---|---|
| **Marx** | Hegel had the dialectic "standing on its head"; it must be turned "right side up": material conditions, not ideas, drive history |
| **Karl Popper**, *Open Society*, vol. 2 | Historicism; worship of the state; apologist of Prussian absolutism; a source of fascism and communism |
| L.T. Hobhouse, *The Metaphysical Theory of the State* (1918) | The god-state destroys individual freedom |
| Bertrand Russell | Freedom reduced to the right to obey the state |
| Kierkegaard | The "system" swallows the existing individual |
| Defenders (Avineri, Pelczynski) | He was a liberal constitutionalist who defended rule of law, civil service, property and conscience |

### 5.8 Relevance

- **Marxism** grew from Hegel's dialectic.
- **Fukuyama's End of History** is Hegelian.
- **Politics of recognition** (Charles Taylor, Axel Honneth): identity movements, minority rights.
- **Civil society** as a sphere between family and state.
- The professional civil service (Weber builds on it).
- Nationalism and the idea of the state as the community's ethical life.

## PART 6: HEGEL'S AND MARX'S DIALECTICS COMPARED (asked 2025)

| | Hegel | Marx |
|---|---|---|
| Name | Idealist dialectic | **Dialectical and historical materialism** (the term "dialectical materialism" was popularised by Engels and Plekhanov) |
| Driving force | Ideas, Spirit, consciousness | Material forces: the mode of production |
| Motor of change | Contradiction between ideas | Contradiction between forces and relations of production; **class struggle** |
| Agents | World-historical individuals; nations | Classes |
| Stages | Oriental → Greco-Roman → Germanic world | Primitive communism → slavery → feudalism → capitalism → socialism → communism |
| End point | The rational state; freedom realised in the state | Classless, stateless communist society |
| Role of state | Highest ethical institution | Instrument of class rule that will "wither away" |
| Change | Gradual reconciliation | Revolution |
| Attitude to present | Reconciles with reality ("the real is rational") | Revolutionary critique of reality |
| Marx's own summary | Hegel's dialectic stands on its head | "Turn it right side up again" |

**Which appeals more?** Either is acceptable if argued. A balanced line: Marx's materialism explains economic and class conflict well (inequality, labour, globalisation); Hegel's emphasis on ideas, recognition and institutions explains nationalism, identity, religion and the durability of states better. Both are criticised for historicism (Popper) and for claiming to know history's end.
