Asked: political parties 2016, 2017 (vs interest groups), 2024 (and public opinion), 2025 (engines of change and recruitment); public opinion 2016 (note); modes of participation 2026. Parties are the single most-asked item in Paper I, Part B.

## 0. POLITICAL PARTICIPATION (2026)

**Definition**: activities by citizens aimed at influencing government and public policy, directly or indirectly (Verba and Nie).

**Modes**
| Mode | Examples |
|---|---|
| Electoral | Voting, campaigning, donating, standing for office |
| Party activity | Membership, meetings, internal elections |
| Contacting officials | Petitions, letters, Citizen Portal complaints |
| Collective action | Interest groups, NGOs, community associations |
| Protest | Demonstrations, sit-ins (dharnas), strikes, long marches, boycotts |
| Digital | Online campaigns, hashtags, crowdfunding |
| Unconventional / violent | Riots, civil disobedience, insurgency |

**Milbrath's hierarchy** (1965): **gladiatorial** activities (holding office, candidacy, fundraising; about 1–3% of people), **transitional** (attending meetings, donating), **spectator** (voting, discussing, wearing a badge), and the **apathetic** at the bottom.

**Factors**: education, income, age, gender, urban residence, party identification, political efficacy, trust, mobilisation, institutional rules (compulsory voting in Australia).

**Pakistan**: turnout 1970 about 63%; 1997 about 35%; 2013 about 55%; 2018 about 52%; 2024 about 47–48% [verify]. Low women's participation; protest politics strong (2014 PTI dharna, 2022–23 long marches, TLP sit-ins, 9 May 2023 riots).

## 1. POLITICAL CHANGE AND REVOLUTION

### 1.1 Political change
Change in the structure, institutions, rules, leadership or policies of a political system. **Modes**: evolutionary/gradual (reform, constitutional amendment, elections); revolutionary (sudden, fundamental, usually violent); coups (change of rulers, not system); "revolution from above" (Meiji Japan; Atatürk); transition by negotiation (Spain 1975–78; South Africa 1990–94).

### 1.2 Revolution: meaning
A rapid, fundamental, usually violent change in the political, social and economic structure of a society, carried out with mass participation. Distinguish from **rebellion** (failed or limited), **coup d'état** (elite change at the top), **reform** (gradual).

### 1.3 Theories
| Theory | Thinker | Cause of revolution |
|---|---|---|
| Inequality | **Aristotle** | The passion for equality (see file 01) |
| Class struggle | **Marx** | Contradiction between forces and relations of production |
| J-curve | **James Davies** (1962) | A long period of rising expectations followed by a sharp reversal |
| Relative deprivation | **Ted Gurr** (1970) | Gap between expectations and capabilities |
| Natural history | **Crane Brinton**, *The Anatomy of Revolution* (1938) | Stages like a fever: old regime crisis, moderates, radicals and terror, Thermidor (reaction) |
| Structural | **Theda Skocpol**, *States and Social Revolutions* (1979) | State breakdown (fiscal and military crisis, international pressure) plus peasant revolt; France, Russia, China |
| Political institutions | **Huntington** (1968) | Participation outruns institutionalisation |
| Islamic thought | Ibn Khaldun | New group with fresh asabiyya overthrows a decadent dynasty |

### 1.4 Major revolutions
| Revolution | Year | Result |
|---|---|---|
| English (Glorious) | 1688 | Parliamentary supremacy |
| American | 1776–83 | Republic, written constitution |
| French | 1789 | End of absolute monarchy; liberty, equality, fraternity; nationalism |
| Russian | 1917 | First communist state |
| Chinese | 1949 | Communist PRC |
| Cuban | 1959 | Castro's socialist state |
| Iranian | 1979 | Islamic Republic, *velayat-e faqih* |
| East European | 1989 | End of communism |
| "Colour" revolutions | 2003–05 | Georgia, Ukraine, Kyrgyzstan |
| Arab Spring | 2010–12 | Tunisia's transition (later reversed in 2021); Egypt's coup (2013); wars in Libya, Syria, Yemen |
| Bangladesh | 2024 | Student uprising ended Sheikh Hasina's rule; BNP won the February 2026 election |

### 1.5 Coup d'état (CSS 2025 MCQ contrasts coups with elections)
Pakistan: 1958 (Ayub), 1969 (Yahya, transfer within the army), 1977 (Zia), 1999 (Musharraf).

## 2. ELECTIONS

### 2.1 Functions
Choose representatives and governments; confer **legitimacy**; hold rulers **accountable** (remove them peacefully: Popper); recruit leaders; aggregate interests; educate and mobilise citizens; register public opinion; peaceful change of government (CSS 2025 MCQ: "fresh elections").

### 2.2 Free and fair elections: criteria
Universal adult suffrage; secret ballot; periodic elections; independent election commission; freedom to form parties and campaign; equal media access; accurate voter rolls; impartial counting and transparent results; effective dispute resolution; absence of intimidation and fraud.

### 2.3 Pakistan
- **Election Commission of Pakistan** (Arts. 213–221): Chief Election Commissioner and four members (one per province).
- **Elections Act 2017**: unified law; result forms (Form 45 at polling station; Form 47 consolidated); 5% women tickets; 10% women turnout rule.
- **Controversies**: 1977 (rigging led to Zia's coup); 1990 (Asghar Khan case, 2012 judgment, found ISI funding); 2013 ("35 punctures"; judicial commission 2015 found no organised rigging); 2018 (RTS failure; "selected"); **2024** (PTI's symbol withdrawn, candidates ran as independents and won the most seats; Form 45 vs 47 disputes; mobile internet shut down on polling day).
- **Electronic voting machines and overseas voting**: approved in 2021 amendments, reversed in 2022.

## 3. ELECTORAL SYSTEMS

### 3.1 Main families

| System | How it works | Used in | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **First-past-the-post (FPTP)** / simple plurality | Single-member districts; candidate with most votes wins | UK (Commons), USA (most), India (Lok Sabha), Canada, **Pakistan (general seats)** | Simple; strong single-party government; constituency link | Disproportional; wasted votes; minority governments of votes win majorities; favours big parties |
| **Two-round system** (majority run-off) | If no majority in round one, a second round | **France** (President and National Assembly) | Winner has majority support | Costly; tactical behaviour |
| **Alternative vote** (preferential) | Rank candidates; lowest eliminated and votes transferred until one has 50% | Australia (House of Representatives) | Majority winner, single-member seats | Still disproportional |
| **List proportional representation (PR)** | Parties win seats in proportion to votes; party lists | Netherlands, Israel, Spain, South Africa, **Turkey**; Pakistan's **reserved seats** (women, minorities) allocated in proportion to general seats won | Fair to parties; representation of minorities and women | Coalitions; fragmentation; weak constituency link; party bosses control lists |
| **Single transferable vote (STV)** | Multi-member districts; ranked preferences; quota | Ireland, Malta; Indian Rajya Sabha and presidential elections (via legislators); **Pakistan's Senate** (by provincial assemblies) [verify method] | Proportional and candidate-centred | Complex counting |
| **Mixed-member proportional (MMP)** | One vote for constituency, one for party; list seats top up to proportionality | **Germany**, New Zealand | Combines constituency link and proportionality | Two classes of MPs; complex |
| **Parallel / mixed** | FPTP and PR seats counted separately | Japan, South Korea | Balance | Less proportional than MMP |

### 3.2 Duverger's law
**Maurice Duverger** (*Political Parties*, 1951): plurality single-member systems tend to produce **two-party** systems (mechanical effect: third parties under-rewarded; psychological effect: voters avoid "wasting" votes); PR tends to produce **multi-party** systems.

### 3.3 Which suits Pakistan?
Arguments for PR or mixed: FPTP rewards electables and feudal power, wastes votes, produces disproportional results and disputes. Arguments for FPTP: simple, gives clear winners, keeps the constituency link. A mixed system is a common recommendation.

## 4. PUBLIC OPINION (2016 note; 2024, 2025 contexts)

### 4.1 Definitions
- **Lord Bryce**: "the aggregate of the views men hold regarding matters that affect or interest the community" (CSS 2026 MCQ).
- **A. Lawrence Lowell**: opinion is "public" when the minority accepts the majority's view not from fear but from conviction.
- **Walter Lippmann**, *Public Opinion* (1922): people act on "pictures in their heads"; opinion can be manufactured.

### 4.2 Agencies forming public opinion
Press, television, radio, social media; political parties; educational institutions; religious institutions; legislatures and debates; pressure groups; cinema and literature; family; opinion leaders (Katz and Lazarsfeld's "two-step flow of communication", 1955).

### 4.3 Hindrances to sound public opinion
Illiteracy and ignorance; poverty; a biased, sensational or "unscrupulous" press; lack of civil liberties; communalism and sectarianism; superstition and conservatism; propaganda and disinformation; apathy. (CSS 2026 MCQ: **educational institutions** are *not* a hindrance; they help.)

### 4.4 Role in democracy
"Democracy is government by public opinion." It guides legislators, checks government, shapes policy agendas, protects rights, legitimises decisions. In foreign policy (2025, Paper II): see `../Paper-II/16-Pakistan-Foreign-Policy.md`.

### 4.5 Measurement
Elections, referendums, opinion polls (Gallup Pakistan, Gilani Research), media analysis, social media analytics, letters to editors, protests.

## 5. PROPAGANDA

### 5.1 Meaning
The deliberate, systematic attempt to shape perceptions and behaviour to achieve a desired response, usually by selective or emotional messages. From the Catholic Church's *Congregatio de Propaganda Fide* (1622), "for propagating the faith". The word acquired its **negative meaning during World War I** (CSS 2024 MCQ), when governments used atrocity stories and posters.

### 5.2 Thinkers and practitioners
Harold **Lasswell**, *Propaganda Technique in the World War* (1927); Edward **Bernays**, *Propaganda* (1928), father of public relations; Joseph **Goebbels** (Nazi Ministry of Propaganda, 1933); Herman and **Chomsky**, *Manufacturing Consent* (1988): the "propaganda model" of mass media (ownership, advertising, sources, flak, anti-communism as filters).

### 5.3 Techniques (Institute for Propaganda Analysis, 1937)
Name-calling; glittering generalities; transfer (borrowing prestige of symbols); testimonial; plain folks; card-stacking (selective facts); bandwagon. Also fear appeals, repetition ("big lie"), scapegoating, demonisation of the enemy.

### 5.4 Propaganda vs public opinion and education
Education encourages critical thinking; propaganda discourages it. Propaganda shapes public opinion, often bypassing reason.

### 5.5 Today
Disinformation, "fake news", deepfakes, bots and troll farms; "fifth-generation" or hybrid warfare (a term used heavily in Pakistan); the India–Pakistan media war during the **May 2025** conflict; state narratives and censorship; EU DisinfoLab's reports on Indian networks (2019–20) [verify].

## 6. POLITICAL PARTIES (2016, 2017, 2024, 2025)

### 6.1 Definitions
- **Edmund Burke** (1770): "a body of men united for promoting by their joint endeavours the national interest upon some particular principle in which they are all agreed".
- **Gettell**: a group of citizens, more or less organised, who act as a political unit and, by using their voting power, aim to control the government and carry out their policies.
- **Anthony Downs**: a team seeking to control government by winning office in elections.

### 6.2 Essentials
Organisation; common principles or programme; aim of winning power by constitutional means; national outlook; leadership.

### 6.3 Functions
1. **Interest aggregation**: combine many demands into programmes.
2. **Political recruitment** and leadership training.
3. **Contesting elections** and forming governments.
4. **Opposition**: criticise and offer alternatives ("government in waiting").
5. **Political socialisation and education**.
6. **Mobilisation** of voters and public opinion (2024).
7. **Link** between people and government.
8. **Policy formulation**.
9. **National integration** (national parties bridging regions).
10. **Social change**: parties carry reform agendas (Labour and the welfare state; PPP and land reform/"roti, kapra, makaan"; Congress and independence).

### 6.4 Why democracy cannot function without parties (2016)
Without parties: no organised choice for voters; no stable majority to govern; no organised opposition; no aggregation of interests; legislature fragmented into individuals; elections become contests of personalities and money. Pakistan's **1985 non-party elections** under Zia produced patronage politics, the "lotas" (turncoats) and weak legislatures, a case in point. Even critics (Washington's farewell warning against "faction"; Rousseau's dislike of partial associations) could not prevent parties from appearing.

### 6.5 Types of parties
- **Cadre (elite) parties** and **mass parties** (Duverger).
- **Catch-all parties** (Otto Kirchheimer, 1966): broad, ideology-light, aim at all voters.
- **Cartel parties** (Katz and Mair, 1995): established parties use state resources to protect themselves.
- Ideological, programmatic, religious, ethnic/regional, personalist, single-issue.
- Revolutionary vanguard (Lenin).

### 6.6 Party systems
| System | Example |
|---|---|
| One-party | China (CPC); former USSR |
| Dominant-party | South Africa (ANC until 2024), Japan (LDP), India (Congress then; BJP now partly) |
| Two-party | USA, UK (historically) |
| Multi-party | Germany, France, India, **Pakistan** |

### 6.7 Problems of parties
- **Iron law of oligarchy** (Robert Michels, *Political Parties*, 1911): "who says organisation says oligarchy"; leaders entrench themselves.
- Factionalism, corruption, dynastic control, money politics, weak ideology.

### 6.8 Parties in Pakistan
| Party | Founded | Base and features |
|---|---|---|
| Pakistan Muslim League (N) | 1988 (as IJI component; PML-N from 1993) | Punjab, trading and industrial classes; Sharif family |
| Pakistan Peoples Party | 1967 (Zulfikar Ali Bhutto) | Sindh; Bhutto–Zardari family; social democratic origins |
| Pakistan Tehreek-e-Insaf | 1996 (Imran Khan) | Urban middle class, youth, KP; populist anti-corruption; lost its symbol in 2024 |
| MQM | 1984 (Altaf Hussain) | Urdu-speaking Karachi; split after 2016 |
| Jamiat Ulema-e-Islam (F) | 1945 (JUI); F faction 1980s | Deobandi; KP and Balochistan |
| Jamaat-e-Islami | 1941 (Maududi) | Cadre-based Islamist party |
| ANP | 1986 (successor of NAP) | Pashtun nationalist, secular |
| TLP | 2015 | Barelvi; blasphemy issues; street power |
| BNP, NP, BAP | — | Balochistan |

**Weaknesses**: dynastic leadership; little internal democracy (Elections Act requires intra-party elections; PTI lost its "bat" symbol in January 2024 over flawed intra-party polls); personality cults; electables; weak policy capacity; history of state engineering (IJI 1988–90; King's parties: Convention Muslim League, PML-Q 2002, BAP 2018, IPP 2023); defections (Art. 63-A).

### 6.9 Parties vs interest groups (2017)

| Point | Political party | Interest (pressure) group |
|---|---|---|
| Aim | Win power and form government | Influence policy without seeking office |
| Scope | Broad programme on all issues | Narrow, specific interests |
| Membership | Open; mass appeal | Specific (business, labour, profession, cause) |
| Methods | Elections, legislature | Lobbying, campaigns, litigation, protests, funding |
| Accountability | To voters | To members |
| Responsibility | Answerable for government | No formal responsibility |
| Role in policy | Formulates and enacts | Supplies information, pressure, expertise |

Overlaps: groups may become parties (Greens; TLP from a movement); parties have allied groups (unions with Labour; business with PML-N).

## 7. PRESSURE GROUPS AND LOBBIES

### 7.1 Definitions
- **Pressure group / interest group**: an organised group that tries to influence government policy without seeking to govern. "The unofficial government" or "the anonymous empire" (S.E. Finer).
- **Lobby/lobbying**: the practice of directly persuading legislators and officials (the term from the lobbies of legislatures). Professional lobbyists in the US register under the Lobbying Disclosure Act (1995).

### 7.2 Types
- **Interest (sectional)** groups: protect members' interests (chambers of commerce, trade unions, bar councils, farmers' groups).
- **Promotional (cause)** groups: promote a cause for all (Amnesty, Greenpeace, Edhi).
- **Insider** (consulted by government) vs **outsider** (protest from outside) (Wyn Grant).
- **Institutional** groups: bodies inside the state that act as interest groups (military, bureaucracy, judiciary).
- **Anomic** groups: spontaneous (riots), and **associational** and **non-associational** (Almond).

### 7.3 Methods
Lobbying; expert evidence to committees; campaign finance and PACs (US); media campaigns; litigation (public interest cases); strikes and protests; personal contacts; co-option on advisory bodies.

### 7.4 Role: arguments for and against
**For** (pluralism: Dahl, Truman): represent interests between elections; supply information; widen participation; check government.
**Against**: unequal access (business stronger than the poor); secrecy and corruption; "regulatory capture"; narrow interests over the public good (Olson, *The Logic of Collective Action*, 1965: small concentrated groups organise better).

### 7.5 Pakistan
FPCCI and chambers of commerce; APTMA (textiles); sugar and wheat mill owners (2020 sugar inquiry report); real-estate developers; bar councils (Lawyers' Movement); doctors' and teachers' associations; ulama and religious groups (Tahaffuz-e-Namoos-e-Risalat movements; TLP); farmers' groups (Kissan Ittehad); traders (Anjuman-e-Tajiran, resisting tax documentation); the military as the strongest institutional interest; foreign lobbies in Washington.
