# 09. Political Concepts (Western and Islamic): Sovereignty, Justice, Law, Liberty, Freedom, Equality, Rights and Duties, Human Rights, Authority and Power

## 1. SOVEREIGNTY

### 1.1 Meaning
From Latin *superanus* (supreme). The supreme power of the state over all persons and associations within its territory (**internal sovereignty**) and its independence from external control (**external sovereignty**).

- **Jean Bodin** (*Six Books of the Commonwealth*, 1576): "supreme power over citizens and subjects, unrestrained by law" (though bound by divine and natural law).
- **Grotius**: "the supreme political power vested in him whose acts are not subject to any other".
- **Burgess**: "original, absolute, unlimited power over the individual subject and over all associations of subjects".

### 1.2 Characteristics (monistic view)
Absoluteness; permanence; universality (all persons and bodies in the territory); indivisibility; inalienability; exclusiveness (only one sovereign).

### 1.3 Kinds

| Kind | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Titular vs real** | Nominal holder (British monarch, Pakistan's President) vs actual exerciser (Cabinet/PM). CSS 2025 MCQ: in a parliamentary democracy the President is a **titular** sovereign |
| **Legal sovereignty** | The authority that makes law the courts enforce: in the UK, the King-in-Parliament (Dicey) |
| **Political sovereignty** | The power behind the legal sovereign: the electorate and public opinion (Dicey's term) |
| **Popular sovereignty** | The people as the ultimate source of power (Rousseau; Locke in a sense; CSS 2024 MCQ) |
| **De jure vs de facto** | Lawful vs actual power (a usurper or military regime is de facto; courts' "doctrine of necessity" in Pakistan validated de facto regimes: *Dosso* 1958, *Nusrat Bhutto* 1977, *Zafar Ali Shah* 2000) |
| **Internal vs external** | Supremacy within; independence without |

(CSS 2025 MCQ asked who gave the concept of "political sovereignty"; it is usually credited to **A.V. Dicey**, not Bodin, Rousseau or Montesquieu, so "None of these" would be correct for that set of options.)

### 1.4 Austin's theory of legal (monistic) sovereignty
**John Austin**, *The Province of Jurisprudence Determined* (1832): "If a determinate human superior, not in a habit of obedience to a like superior, receive habitual obedience from the bulk of a given society, that determinate superior is sovereign in that society, and the society (including the superior) is a society political and independent."

Elements: (1) a **determinate** person or body; (2) receives **habitual obedience** from the bulk of society; (3) is not habitually obedient to anyone else; (4) its commands are **law**; (5) sovereignty is **indivisible** and **unlimited**.

**Criticism**
- **Determinate superior** is hard to locate in federations and democracies (Maine: Ranjit Singh was absolute yet never made a law; customs ruled).
- Ignores **custom** and **public opinion** (political sovereignty).
- Ignores **international law** and treaties.
- Indivisibility is contradicted by **federalism** (US: powers divided between Union and states; sovereignty is in the amending process).
- Law is not merely command: much law grants powers (H.L.A. Hart, *The Concept of Law*, 1961).

### 1.5 The pluralist attack: why the monistic concept was abandoned (2020)
**Pluralists**: Harold **Laski**, R.M. **MacIver**, J.N. **Figgis**, G.D.H. **Cole**, Léon **Duguit**, Hugo **Krabbe**, Ernest **Barker**.

Arguments:
1. Society contains many associations (churches, unions, universities, professional bodies) with their own authority; the state is one association among many, perhaps "first among equals" (MacIver: the state is an "association of associations").
2. Sovereignty in practice is divided: federalism, separation of powers, local government.
3. **Law** comes from many sources, not only the state's command (Duguit: social solidarity; Krabbe: sense of right).
4. **International law** and organisations limit states.
5. **Rights** of individuals limit the state (constitutional rights, judicial review).
6. In democracy, power rests on consent, not command.
7. Laski: "It would be of lasting benefit to political science if the whole concept of sovereignty were surrendered" (paraphrase).

**Today**: globalisation, the EU, WTO, IMF conditionality, human-rights treaties and R2P further limit sovereignty. Yet legal sovereignty survives as a concept in constitutional law (who has the final word) and in international law (UN Charter Art. 2(1): sovereign equality).

**Criticism of pluralism**: someone must settle conflicts between associations: that is the state; pluralism risks anarchy; Laski himself moved back towards the state later.

### 1.6 Islamic concept of sovereignty (2024)
- **Sovereignty belongs to Allah alone** (*hakimiyya*): "The command (*hukm*) is only for Allah" (12:40); "Blessed is He in whose hand is dominion (*al-mulk*)" (67:1); "Say: O Allah, Owner of all sovereignty, You give sovereignty to whom You will…" (3:26).
- **Humans are vicegerents** (*khalifa*): they exercise a **delegated authority** as a trust (*amanah*) within the limits of the shari'a.
- **The community (Ummah)** holds this vicegerency collectively; rulers are chosen through **bay'a** and **shura** and remain accountable.
- **Legal sovereignty** = the shari'a (Quran and Sunnah); **political authority** = the community and its chosen rulers.
- **Maududi**: legal sovereignty belongs to God; the state is a "theo-democracy" in which the people's representatives make laws within divine limits; no human being is sovereign.
- **Pakistan**: the Objectives Resolution: "sovereignty over the entire universe belongs to Allah Almighty alone, and the authority which He has delegated to the State of Pakistan, through its people for being exercised within the limits prescribed by Him is a sacred trust".

### 1.7 Western and Islamic concepts compared

| Point | Western (Bodin, Hobbes, Austin; popular sovereignty) | Islamic |
|---|---|---|
| Holder | Monarch, parliament or the people | Allah alone; humans hold delegated authority |
| Nature | Absolute, unlimited (monists); limited by constitution (liberals) | Limited by divine law; a trust |
| Source of law | Will of the sovereign or the people | Revelation (Quran, Sunnah) + ijtihad |
| Changeability | Laws can be changed by the sovereign | Core principles cannot be changed; details by ijtihad |
| Accountability | To electorate (democracy); none (absolutism) | To God and the community |
| Rights | Granted or recognised by the state | Granted by God; inviolable |
| Purpose | Order, security, will of the people | Justice, welfare, obedience to God |
| Similarities | Supreme authority needed for order; legitimacy linked to the people's consent in democratic versions; rule of law | |

### 1.8 Parliamentary sovereignty in Pakistan (2019, 2021)
**In the UK** (Dicey): Parliament can make or unmake any law; no body can override it; no Parliament can bind its successor. (Modified by EU membership until 2020, the Human Rights Act 1998 and devolution.)

**In Pakistan, Parliament is supreme only within the Constitution.** Constraints:

*Legal constraints*
1. **Written, rigid constitution**: amendments need a two-thirds majority in each House (Art. 239).
2. **Islamic provisions**: no law repugnant to Quran and Sunnah (Art. 227); Federal Shariat Court can strike down laws (Art. 203-D); Council of Islamic Ideology advises.
3. **Fundamental rights**: laws inconsistent with them are void (Art. 8).
4. **Judicial review**: Supreme Court and High Courts (Arts. 184(3), 185, 199); after the 27th Amendment (Nov 2025), the **Federal Constitutional Court** decides constitutional questions.
5. **Federalism**: Federal and Concurrent Lists (after the 18th Amendment only a Federal Legislative List, Parts I and II); provincial autonomy; the **Senate**'s equal provincial representation; the **Council of Common Interests**.
6. **Basic structure / salient features**: in *District Bar Association Rawalpindi v. Federation* (2015, on the 18th and 21st Amendments), a majority of the Supreme Court held that the Constitution has salient features (democracy, parliamentary form, federalism, independence of judiciary, fundamental rights, Islamic provisions) and that amendments could in principle be reviewed, though it upheld the amendments [verify the precise split]. Article 239(5)–(6) says amendments cannot be questioned in court.
7. **Presidential and procedural limits**: money bills, ordinances, emergency provisions (Arts. 232–237).

*Political constraints*
1. **Military's role** (four coups; "hybrid" arrangements); the 27th Amendment created the Chief of Defence Forces post.
2. **Judicial activism** (2005–2022): disqualification of PMs (Gilani 2012, Nawaz Sharif 2017), suo motu actions.
3. **Executive dominance** and party discipline (Art. 63-A defection clause): legislators follow party leaders.
4. **Coalition politics** and fragile majorities.
5. **External actors**: IMF conditions on budgets; foreign pressure.
6. **Public opinion and media**.

**2019 question**: judicial activism vs parliamentary sovereignty → argue that both are under the Constitution; activism filled governance gaps but sometimes encroached (disqualifications, policy decisions such as the Karachi demolitions, dam fund); Parliament replied with the 26th Amendment (Oct 2024: constitutional benches, Special Parliamentary Committee appointing the Chief Justice for three years) and the 27th (Nov 2025: Federal Constitutional Court). Judge whether the balance has now tilted too far towards the executive.

## 2. JUSTICE

### 2.1 Western theories
| Theory | Thinker | Idea |
|---|---|---|
| Justice as harmony and functional specialisation | **Plato** | Each doing his own work |
| Distributive and corrective justice | **Aristotle** | Proportion to merit; restoring equality |
| Natural justice / natural law | Stoics, Cicero, Aquinas | Universal law of reason |
| Utility | **Bentham**, Mill | Greatest happiness |
| Justice as fairness | **Rawls** | Equal liberty; fair opportunity; difference principle |
| Entitlement | **Nozick** | Just acquisition and transfer; minimal state |
| Class justice | **Marx** | Bourgeois justice serves capital; true justice needs end of exploitation |
| Capabilities | **Sen**, Nussbaum | What people can do and be |

**Dimensions**: legal (equal laws, fair trial, due process—Pakistan Art. 10-A), political (equal rights to participate), social (no discrimination by caste, sex, religion), economic (fair distribution, minimum needs).

### 2.2 Islamic concept
- **'Adl** (balance, putting things in their right place) and **qist** (equity). Justice is the purpose of sending prophets: "We sent Our messengers… that people may uphold justice" (57:25).
- "Stand firm for justice as witnesses for Allah, even against yourselves, your parents or kin" (4:135); "let not hatred of a people make you swerve from justice" (5:8); "Allah commands justice and kindness" (16:90).
- Includes legal justice (equality before law, independent qadis), economic justice (zakat, prohibition of riba and hoarding, inheritance shares), social justice (equality, care for the weak).
- Examples: Umar's accountability; Qadi Shurayh's ruling against Caliph Ali.

## 3. LAW

### 3.1 Meaning and definitions
- The word comes from the Teutonic root "**lag**", meaning something fixed or laid down (CSS 2025 MCQ: "Lag").
- **Austin**: law is a command of the sovereign backed by a sanction.
- **Holland**: "a general rule of external human action enforced by a sovereign political authority".
- **Woodrow Wilson**: "that portion of the established habit and thought of mankind which has gained distinct and formal recognition in the shape of uniform rules backed by the authority and power of government".
- **Historical school** (Savigny, Maine): law grows from the *Volksgeist*, the spirit of the people, like language.
- **Sociological school** (Duguit, Roscoe Pound): law as a means of social engineering and social solidarity.
- **Natural law school**: law must conform to reason and morality; "an unjust law is no law" (Augustine/Aquinas).

### 3.2 Sources of law
Custom; religion; judicial decisions (precedent); equity; scientific commentaries (jurists); legislation (the main modern source); constitution; international treaties.

### 3.3 Kinds
Natural and positive; public (constitutional, administrative, criminal) and private (contract, property, family); constitutional and ordinary; municipal (national) and international; statute and common law; substantive and procedural.

### 3.4 Law and morality
Overlap (murder, theft) but differ: law concerns external acts, is enforced by the state, is precise; morality concerns motives too, is enforced by conscience and society.

### 3.5 Islamic law
- **Shari'a** (the divine path) and **fiqh** (human understanding of it).
- **Sources**: Quran, Sunnah, Ijma (consensus), Qiyas (analogy); secondary: istihsan, maslaha mursala, 'urf (custom), istishab.
- **Objectives** (*maqasid*): protection of religion, life, intellect, lineage, property.
- **Categories**: obligatory, recommended, permissible, disliked, forbidden.
- Ruler is under the law; legislation by humans is limited to areas not fixed by the texts.

## 4. LIBERTY AND FREEDOM

### 4.1 Meaning
From Latin *liber* (free). **Negative**: absence of restraint. **Positive**: presence of conditions to develop oneself.
- **Laski**: "the eager maintenance of that atmosphere in which men have the opportunity to be their best selves".
- **Isaiah Berlin**, "Two Concepts of Liberty" (1958): **negative liberty** (freedom from interference; Hobbes, Locke, Mill) and **positive liberty** (freedom to be one's own master; Rousseau, Hegel, T.H. Green). Berlin warned that positive liberty can be twisted into tyranny ("forcing people to be free").

**Liberty and freedom**: generally used as synonyms. Where distinguished, *liberty* is social and legal (freedom within law, protected by the state), while *freedom* is broader and can be inner or absolute (freedom of will). Some use "freedom" for the absence of external control of a nation (national freedom).

### 4.2 Kinds of liberty
Natural (in the state of nature, a myth); **civil** (personal: life, speech, religion, movement, association, property); **political** (vote, contest, criticise government, hold office); **economic** (work, fair wages, freedom from want; Laski: "political liberty without economic equality is a myth"); **national** (independence from foreign rule); **moral** (self-mastery).

### 4.3 Safeguards of liberty
Written constitution and fundamental rights; **rule of law** (Dicey); **separation of powers** (Montesquieu); **independent judiciary** and judicial review; democracy and elections; free press; decentralisation; strong opposition and civil society; economic security; "eternal vigilance" of citizens (a saying often attributed to Jefferson; origin uncertain).

### 4.4 Liberty and law
- **Opposed** (individualists, anarchists): every law restricts liberty.
- **Complementary** (Locke: "where there is no law, there is no freedom"; idealists): law protects liberty from others' interference.
- Balanced view: good laws protect liberty; bad laws destroy it.

### 4.5 Islamic view
Freedom of belief ("no compulsion in religion", 2:256), of expression within limits of truth and decency, of movement, of property and of dignity; Umar's famous words: "Since when have you enslaved people whom their mothers bore free?" True freedom is servitude to God alone, which frees humans from servitude to other humans.

## 5. EQUALITY

### 5.1 Meaning
Not identical treatment, but absence of special privileges and equal opportunities (Laski: "equality means first of all the absence of special privilege… secondly that adequate opportunities are laid open to all").

### 5.2 Kinds
Natural (all humans equal in dignity); **legal/civil** (equality before law and equal protection: Pakistan Art. 25); **political** (one person, one vote); **social** (no caste, race, sex discrimination); **economic** (fair distribution, minimum standards); **equality of opportunity** (Rawls).

### 5.3 Equality and liberty
- **Opposed**: de Tocqueville and Lord Acton feared that the passion for equality would destroy liberty; Hayek: economic equality requires coercion.
- **Complementary**: Laski, Tawney, Rousseau: liberty without equality benefits only the strong; real liberty requires a minimum of equality.

### 5.4 Islamic view
"O mankind, We created you from a male and a female and made you nations and tribes that you may know one another; the most honoured of you with Allah is the most righteous" (49:13). The Farewell Sermon: no Arab over non-Arab, no white over black, except by piety. Equality before law (the Makhzumi woman case), equal spiritual worth of men and women; differences in roles in some areas (inheritance, testimony in certain transactions) are explained by jurists in terms of differing responsibilities.

## 6. RIGHTS AND DUTIES (2023)

### 6.1 Rights
- **Laski**: "Rights are those conditions of social life without which no man can seek, in general, to be himself at his best."
- **T.H. Green**: "a power claimed and recognised as contributory to a common good".
- **Theories**: natural rights (Locke); legal (Bentham, Austin: rights are created by law); historical (rights grow from custom); social welfare (rights justified by social good); idealist (rights as conditions of moral development); Marxist (rights reflect class interests).
- **Kinds**: moral and legal; **civil** (life, liberty, property, equality, religion, speech, association); **political** (vote, contest, petition, office); **economic** (work, fair wages, rest, social security); human rights (see §7).

### 6.2 Duties
Moral and legal; to the state (allegiance, obey law, pay taxes, defend the country, vote honestly, serve when called); to society and others (respect others' rights).

Pakistan: **Article 5** (loyalty to the state is the basic duty of every citizen; obedience to the Constitution and law is the inviolable obligation of every citizen).

### 6.3 Why "two sides of the same coin"
1. **My right is your duty**: my right to life is everyone's duty not to kill me.
2. **Rights imply duties for the holder**: my right to free speech implies my duty to respect others' reputation.
3. **Rights against the state imply duties to the state**: protection requires loyalty and taxes.
4. **Rights exist in society**: they are recognised because they serve the common good, so they carry social responsibility (Green, Laski).
5. Gandhi: "the true source of rights is duty… if we all discharge our duties, rights will not be far to seek".

### 6.4 Islamic view
- **Huquq Allah** (rights of God: worship, obedience) and **huquq al-'ibad** (rights of people: life, property, honour, family, neighbours, the poor).
- Islam speaks more of duties (obligations, *fara'id*) than of rights: one person's duty secures another's right. The Prophet: "Each of you is a shepherd and each is responsible for his flock."
- Rights in the Farewell Sermon: sanctity of life, property and honour.
- Rulers' duties = subjects' rights (justice, protection, welfare); subjects' duties = obedience in good, advice (*nasiha*), participation.

### 6.5 Pakistan's Constitution
Fundamental rights in **Articles 8–28**: security of person (9), safeguards on arrest (10), fair trial (10-A, added 2010), slavery and forced labour banned (11), dignity (14), movement (15), assembly (16), association (17), trade (18), speech (19), information (19-A), religion (20–22), property (23–24), equality (25), free education (25-A), access to public places (26), non-discrimination in services (27), culture and language (28). Principles of Policy: Articles 29–40.

## 7. HUMAN RIGHTS (2025 note)

### 7.1 Meaning
Rights that belong to every human being by virtue of being human: universal, inalienable, indivisible and interdependent.

### 7.2 Development
Magna Carta (1215); English Bill of Rights (1689); US Declaration of Independence (1776) and Bill of Rights (1791); French Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen (1789); abolition of slavery; Geneva Conventions (1864 onward; four conventions of 1949); UN Charter (1945).

**International Bill of Human Rights**:
- **Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR)**: adopted by the UN General Assembly on **10 December 1948** (Human Rights Day), 30 articles; 48 in favour, 0 against, 8 abstentions. (CSS 2026 MCQ: adopted in 1948 — "None of these" for the options given there.)
- **ICCPR** and **ICESCR** (1966; in force 1976).

**Core treaties**: CERD (1965), CEDAW (1979), CAT (1984), CRC (1989), CMW (1990), CRPD (2006), CPED (2006).

**Institutions**: UN **Human Rights Council** (2006, replaced the Commission on Human Rights; 47 members; Universal Periodic Review); **OHCHR** (High Commissioner); treaty bodies (e.g. the **Human Rights Committee** monitors the ICCPR — CSS 2026 MCQ); regional courts (European Court of Human Rights).

**Generations** (Karel Vasak, 1977): first (civil and political: liberty), second (economic, social and cultural: equality), third (solidarity: development, peace, environment).

### 7.3 Islamic perspective
- Rights are God-given, so no ruler can abolish them. Farewell Sermon (632) as an early charter.
- **Cairo Declaration on Human Rights in Islam** (OIC, 1990): rights subject to shari'a. A revised **OIC Declaration on Human Rights** was adopted in 2020 [verify].
- Debates: freedom of religion (apostasy), women's rights, punishments.

### 7.4 Debates
- **Universalism vs cultural relativism**: the "Asian values" debate (1990s); critics call human rights a Western imposition.
- **Selectivity and double standards**: great powers apply them selectively (Gaza, Kashmir, Guantanamo).
- **Sovereignty vs intervention**: R2P (2005); Libya (2011).

### 7.5 Pakistan
Fundamental rights (Arts. 8–28); ratified ICESCR (2008) and ICCPR and CAT (2010) [verify years]; National Commission for Human Rights (Act of 2012); EU **GSP+** status tied to 27 conventions; concerns: enforced disappearances, blasphemy-law misuse, minorities, women's rights, press freedom, the death penalty.

## 8. POLITICAL AUTHORITY AND POWER

### 8.1 Definitions
- **Power**: the ability to make others do what they would not otherwise do. Robert **Dahl**: "A has power over B to the extent that he can get B to do something that B would not otherwise do." Harold **Lasswell**: politics is "who gets what, when, how".
- **Authority**: **legitimate power**: the recognised right to command and be obeyed.
- **Influence**: the ability to affect decisions without command (persuasion, prestige).
- **Legitimacy**: the belief that power is rightful.

**Formula**: authority = power + legitimacy.

### 8.2 Max Weber's three types of legitimate authority
| Type | Basis | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Traditional** | Custom, inheritance, "eternal yesterday" | Monarchies, tribal chiefs, sardars, pirs |
| **Charismatic** | Extraordinary personal qualities of a leader | Prophets, Jinnah, Bhutto, Gandhi, Imran Khan's appeal; unstable, faces "routinisation" after the leader |
| **Legal-rational** | Rules and offices established by law | Modern bureaucratic states, elected officials |

### 8.3 Dimensions of power
- **Steven Lukes**, *Power: A Radical View* (1974): three dimensions:
  1. **Decision-making** (Dahl): who wins open conflicts.
  2. **Non-decision-making / agenda-setting** (Bachrach and Baratz, 1962): keeping issues off the agenda.
  3. **Shaping preferences**: manipulating what people want (ideology; compare Gramsci's hegemony).
- **Foucault**: power as relational and productive (see `05`).
- **Joseph Nye**: **hard power** (coercion, payments), **soft power** (attraction of culture, values, policies), **smart power** (combination).
- Forms: political, economic, military, ideological; national power (Morgenthau's elements: geography, resources, industry, military, population, national character, morale, diplomacy, government).

### 8.4 Islamic view
Power belongs to Allah; human authority is a trust (*amanah*) and test; rulers must be just and consult; obedience to "those in authority" (4:59) is conditional on obedience to God ("no obedience to the creature in disobedience to the Creator", hadith). Legitimacy comes from bay'a, competence, justice and adherence to shari'a.

### 8.5 Relevance to Pakistan
Pakistan shows all three Weberian types at once: legal-rational institutions; traditional authority (feudal and tribal elites, pirs); and charismatic leadership (Jinnah, Bhutto, Imran Khan). The gap between formal authority (elected government) and real power (military, establishment) is often called a **hybrid regime**.
