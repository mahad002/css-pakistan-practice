# 08. State System: The Modern Nation-State, Origin of the State, the Islamic State and the Ummah

Asked: development of the modern nation-state 2022; origin of the state (force theory) 2019; nationality vs citizenship 2019; Islamic concept of state and modern challenges 2017; Islam as a complete code for managing political power 2023. MCQs often ask the elements of the state (2024: four).

---

## 1. The state: definitions and elements

**Definitions**
- **Max Weber**: a human community that successfully claims the **monopoly of the legitimate use of physical force** within a given territory.
- **J.W. Garner**: a community of persons, more or less numerous, permanently occupying a definite territory, independent of external control, with an organised government to which the great body of inhabitants render habitual obedience.
- **Woodrow Wilson**: "a people organised for law within a definite territory".
- **Montevideo Convention (1933), Art. 1**: a state should possess a permanent population, a defined territory, a government, and the capacity to enter into relations with other states.

**Four essential elements** (CSS 2024 MCQ: "four"):

| Element | Notes |
|---|---|
| Population | No fixed size: Vatican (about 800) to India (over 1.4 billion). Plato wanted 5,040 households; Aristotle "neither too large nor too small" |
| Territory | Land, territorial waters (12 nautical miles), airspace. Boundaries may be disputed (Kashmir) |
| Government | The agency through which the state's will is formulated and executed |
| Sovereignty | Supreme internal authority and external independence. The element that distinguishes the state from other associations |

Some add **recognition** by other states (declaratory vs constitutive theories of recognition).

**State, government, nation, society**

| | Meaning | Key difference |
|---|---|---|
| State | Population + territory + government + sovereignty | Permanent, abstract, sovereign |
| Government | The machinery (executive, legislature, judiciary) | Changes; agent of the state |
| Nation | A people bound by common identity (history, culture, language, religion) and political consciousness | Psychological and cultural; may lack a state (Kurds, Palestinians) |
| Society | All social relations and associations | Wider; not necessarily sovereign or territorial |

## 2. Theories of the origin of the state (2019)

| Theory | Claim | Exponents | Criticism |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Divine origin** | The state is created by God; kings rule by divine right and answer only to God | Old Testament; medieval Christian thinkers; James I (*Trew Law of Free Monarchies*, 1598); Robert Filmer (*Patriarcha*) | Unhistorical; justifies tyranny; rejected by Locke. (The Islamic view is different: sovereignty belongs to God, but rulers are accountable trustees, not divine) |
| **Force theory** | The state arose from conquest: the strong subdued the weak; "war begat the king" | Ancient sophists (Thrasymachus); Treitschke and Bernhardi (Germany); Franz Oppenheimer (*The State*, 1908); Marxists (state as instrument of class rule); Ibn Khaldun partly (domination by the group with strongest asabiyya) | Force is one factor, not the only one; "force is not the basis of the state, will is" (T.H. Green); a state resting on force alone cannot last; justifies might-is-right and imperialism |
| **Patriarchal** | The state grew from the family headed by the father | Henry Maine (*Ancient Law*, 1861); Aristotle (household → village → polis) | Some early societies were matriarchal; families alone do not explain territorial sovereignty |
| **Matriarchal** | Early kinship traced through the mother | McLennan, Morgan, Jenks | Limited evidence |
| **Social contract** | The state was created by agreement among individuals in a state of nature | Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau (see `02-Early-Modern-Western-Thought.md`) | No historical evidence; presupposes law it creates |
| **Evolutionary / historical** (accepted view) | The state grew gradually from many factors: **kinship**, **religion**, **force/war**, **economic activity** (property, agriculture), **political consciousness** | Burgess, Garner, MacIver, Gettell. "The state is a growth, not a make" (Garner) | Most widely accepted |

**Answer to "how far is it true that the origin of the state lies in force?"** Force played a real part (conquest states, empires, early kingdoms, colonial borders, Ibn Khaldun's dynasties) and the state still rests on a monopoly of force (Weber). But force alone cannot explain why people obey over time: kinship, religion, economic needs and consent all contributed. A state that rests only on force (Nazi Germany, colonial rule) eventually collapses. Conclusion: force was a factor in the emergence of states, not their sole origin or justification.

## 3. Emergence and nature of the modern nation-state system (2022)

### 3.1 Before the nation-state
- **City-states** (Greek polis), **empires** (Roman, Persian, Mughal, Ottoman), **feudal kingdoms** with overlapping loyalties (lord, king, Church, Holy Roman Emperor).
- In medieval Europe authority was layered: the **Pope** claimed spiritual supremacy; the **Holy Roman Emperor** claimed universal temporal authority; nobles held fiefs.

### 3.2 Stages in the rise of the modern state
1. **Decline of feudalism** (late Middle Ages): the Black Death (1347–51), gunpowder, standing armies and royal taxation strengthened kings over nobles.
2. **Renaissance**: secular politics (Machiavelli's *lo stato*).
3. **Reformation** (Luther, 1517): broke the Church's universal authority; princes chose their realm's religion (*cuius regio, eius religio*, Peace of Augsburg 1555).
4. **Theory of sovereignty**: Jean Bodin (*Six Books of the Commonwealth*, 1576) and Hobbes (1651).
5. **Peace of Westphalia (1648)**: ended the Thirty Years' War and the Eighty Years' War. Established the principles of **territorial sovereignty**, **non-interference** in internal affairs and **legal equality of states**. The "**Westphalian system**" is the start of the modern international state system.
6. **Nationalism**: the American (1776) and French (1789) revolutions turned dynastic states into **nation-states** where sovereignty belongs to the nation. Napoleon's wars spread nationalism. Unifications of Italy (1861) and Germany (1871).
7. **Collapse of empires after World War I** (Austro-Hungarian, Ottoman, Russian, German) and Wilson's principle of **self-determination** (1918).
8. **Decolonisation** after 1945: from 51 founding UN members to **193** today. Pakistan (1947) is part of this wave.
9. **End of the Cold War**: break-up of the USSR and Yugoslavia created new nation-states.

### 3.3 Features of the modern nation-state
- Defined, recognised **territory** and borders.
- **Sovereignty**, internal and external.
- **Monopoly of legitimate force** (army, police).
- **Impersonal, bureaucratic administration** and rule of law.
- **Nation** as the basis of legitimacy; citizenship and national identity.
- **Taxation** and a national economy, currency, markets.
- **Membership** in an international system of equal states (UN Charter, Art. 2(1) and 2(7)).

### 3.4 Challenges to the nation-state today
- **Globalisation**: capital, trade, information and migration cross borders; states lose control over economies (see `../Paper-II/05-Global-and-Regional-Integration.md`).
- **Supranational bodies**: EU law overrides national law; WTO rules; IMF conditionality (Pakistan's IMF programmes).
- **Non-state actors**: multinational corporations, NGOs, terrorist networks (al-Qaeda, ISIS, TTP), tech platforms.
- **Sub-national movements**: secession and autonomy (Catalonia, Scotland, Kurdistan, Balochistan).
- **Failed and fragile states**: Somalia, Yemen, Libya, Sudan.
- **Responsibility to Protect (R2P, 2005)**: sovereignty as responsibility, limiting non-interference.
- **Transnational threats**: climate change, pandemics, cyber.

**Yet the nation-state survives**: COVID-19 border closures, the return of tariffs and industrial policy, Brexit and rising nationalism show it remains the main unit of politics.

## 4. Nation, nationality and citizenship (2019)

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Nationality** (in political science) | A people with a sense of common identity based on race, language, religion, history, culture, common interests and a shared will to live together, **not necessarily organised as a state**. "A nationality is a nation in the making" |
| **Nation** | A nationality that has achieved political consciousness and usually its own state ("nationality + statehood", Burgess/Bryce). Ernest Renan (1882): a nation is "a daily plebiscite", a shared memory and will |
| **Citizenship** | The **legal status** of membership in a state, giving civil and political rights (vote, office, protection) and duties (allegiance, taxes, obeying law) |
| **Nationality (in law)** | Often used as a synonym for citizenship (e.g. on a passport) |

**Elements of nationality**: common descent (not essential), language, religion, history and traditions, geographical unity, common political aspirations, common economic interests, and above all the **psychological will** to be one people.

**Differences between nationality and citizenship**

| Nationality | Citizenship |
|---|---|
| Cultural and psychological | Legal and political |
| Can exist without a state | Requires a state |
| One person feels one nationality | Dual citizenship possible (Pakistan allows it with listed countries) |
| Cannot be granted by law | Granted or withdrawn by law |
| Several nationalities can live in one state (multinational state: UK, India, Pakistan) | All share one citizenship |

**Acquisition of citizenship**: by birth (**jus soli**, place of birth: USA; Pakistan's Citizenship Act 1951, s. 4, with exceptions); by descent (**jus sanguinis**, parentage); by **naturalisation**; by marriage; by registration; by incorporation of territory. **Loss**: renunciation, deprivation, acquiring another citizenship (where barred), long residence abroad.

## 5. The Islamic concept of the state (2017, 2023)

### 5.1 Sources
Quran, Sunnah, the practice of the Prophet's state in Madinah and the Rightly Guided Caliphs (632–661), ijma and ijtihad.

### 5.2 The first Islamic state: Madinah (622)
- **Charter of Madinah** (*Mithaq-i-Madinah*, c. 622): often called the **first written constitution**. It created one *ummah* of the Muslims (Muhajirin and Ansar) and recognised the **Jewish tribes** as part of the community with their own religion; set mutual defence obligations; made the Prophet (PBUH) the final arbiter; guaranteed rights of protected groups; declared Yathrib a sanctuary.
- The Prophet was head of state, chief judge, commander and lawgiver (conveying revelation). Consultation (*shura*) was practised (e.g. before Uhud, the battle of the Trench).

### 5.3 Basic principles

| Principle | Meaning | Quranic basis (examples) |
|---|---|---|
| **Sovereignty of Allah** (*hakimiyya*) | Ultimate sovereignty belongs to God; human authority is delegated | "Command belongs only to Allah" (12:40); "Say: O Allah, Owner of sovereignty…" (3:26) |
| **Khilafah** (vicegerency) | Humans, especially the community of believers, are God's vicegerents, exercising authority as a trust (*amanah*) | 2:30; 24:55 |
| **Risalat** | The Prophet's Sunnah as the model and second source of law | 4:59; 33:21 |
| **Shura** (consultation) | Rulers must consult; the community runs its affairs by consultation | "their affairs are by mutual consultation" (42:38); "consult them in matters" (3:159) |
| **'Adl** (justice) | Justice for all, even against oneself or kin; justice towards enemies | 4:135; 5:8; 16:90 |
| **Equality** | No superiority by race, colour or wealth; superiority only by piety | 49:13; the Farewell Sermon |
| **Rule of law** | The ruler is under the shari'a; no one is above the law | The Prophet: "had Fatima stolen…" |
| **Accountability** | Rulers answer to God and to the people (Abu Bakr's first address: "obey me as long as I obey God…") | |
| **Rights of minorities** (*dhimmis*) | Protection of life, property, worship; Charter of Madinah; Umar's covenant with Jerusalem | 2:256 "no compulsion in religion" |
| **Welfare** | *Bayt al-mal*, zakat, care for the poor, orphans, travellers | 9:60 |
| **Amr bil ma'ruf, nahi 'anil munkar** | The state promotes good and prevents evil | 3:104; 22:41 |

### 5.4 Functions of the Islamic state
Protection of religion, life, intellect, lineage and property (the five *maqasid al-shari'a* of al-Ghazali and al-Shatibi); justice; defence; welfare and economic justice (zakat, ban on *riba*); education; promoting good; peaceful relations with others (treaties, *Sulh al-Hudaybiyyah*).

### 5.5 Islamic state versus Western (secular) state

| Point | Islamic state | Western liberal state |
|---|---|---|
| Sovereignty | Allah; people exercise it as trust | The people / parliament |
| Law | Shari'a as supreme; legislation within its limits | Man-made; constitution supreme |
| Religion and state | Integrated (Iqbal: no Church–State dualism) | Separated |
| Purpose | Welfare in this world and the hereafter | Worldly welfare, rights |
| Basis of community | Ideology (faith) | Nation, territory |
| Legislature | Shura; ijtihad within shari'a | Sovereign legislature |
| Rights | Granted by God; cannot be withdrawn by state | Natural or legal rights |

**Not a theocracy**: no priestly class rules; the ruler is chosen and accountable (Maududi used the term "theo-democracy"; Iqbal rejected theocracy).

### 5.6 Issues and challenges to the Islamic concept of state in modern times (2017)
1. **Nation-state versus Ummah**: Islam's community is universal; the modern world is divided into 57 Muslim nation-states (OIC members).
2. **Sovereignty**: reconciling God's sovereignty with popular sovereignty and parliaments (Pakistan's solution: Objectives Resolution and Article 2A).
3. **Legislation and ijtihad**: who interprets shari'a—ulama or elected legislators? (Iqbal: the assembly; Council of Islamic Ideology; Federal Shariat Court.)
4. **Form of government**: no single prescribed form; monarchies (Saudi Arabia), republics (Pakistan, Iran), velayat-e faqih (Iran), military rule.
5. **Sectarian differences** (Sunni–Shia; schools of fiqh) over which interpretation the state applies.
6. **Rights of women and minorities** in line with modern human rights law.
7. **Islamic economics**: implementing interest-free banking (Pakistan's Federal Shariat Court judgement of April 2022 ordered riba to be eliminated by 2027; the 26th Amendment (2024) set 1 January 2028 as the target in Article 38(f)) [verify exact wording].
8. **Extremism**: militant groups (ISIS, TTP) claim to establish an "Islamic state" by violence, discrediting the idea.
9. **Western pressure and globalisation**: IMF/World Bank models, international human rights scrutiny, cultural globalisation.
10. **Weak institutions and governance**: corruption, dictatorship and elite capture contradict Islamic accountability.

**Way forward**: ijtihad through representative institutions; consensus on core principles (justice, shura, accountability, welfare); rule of law; education; constitutional guarantees for minorities and women; OIC cooperation.

### 5.7 Islam as a complete code for managing political power (2023)
Points to make: Islam defines the **source** of power (Allah), its **nature** (trust, *amanah*: "Allah commands you to render trusts to those worthy of them, and when you judge between people, judge with justice", 4:58), its **holders** (competent and trustworthy: Ibn Taymiyyah), its **method** (shura, bay'a), its **limits** (shari'a, rights), its **purpose** (justice and welfare), **accountability** (hisba, qadis independent of rulers—Qadi Shurayh's judgement against Caliph Ali in the case of the armour), and **succession** (Rashidun examples). Then note the gap between principle and Muslim history (hereditary kingship from 661) and modern practice; conclude that the code provides principles, leaving forms to ijtihad.

### 5.8 Pakistan's constitutional expression
Objectives Resolution (1949; Article 2A since 1985); Islam as state religion (Art. 2); Islamic way of life (Art. 31); no law repugnant to Quran and Sunnah (Art. 227); Council of Islamic Ideology (Arts. 228–231); Federal Shariat Court (Art. 203-C, created 1980); President and PM must be Muslim (Arts. 41, 91).

## 6. The Ummah

- **Meaning**: the community of believers, bound by faith rather than race, territory or language.
- **Quranic basis**: "You are the best community (*khayra ummatin*) raised for mankind; you enjoin right and forbid wrong" (3:110); "We made you a middle community (*ummatan wasatan*)" (2:143); "this community of yours is one community" (21:92; 23:52).
- **Features**: unity of belief (tawhid, risalat); brotherhood ("the believers are brothers", 49:10); equality; collective responsibility; universality; moderation.
- **Ummah and the Charter of Madinah**: the charter used *ummah* to include believers and, as allies, the Jews of Madinah: a political as well as religious community.
- **Ummah versus nation-state**: Iqbal criticised territorial nationalism but supported a Muslim homeland; modern Muslims live in nation-states, so the Ummah today is expressed through cooperation: the **OIC** (founded 1969 after the arson at Al-Aqsa; 57 members; HQ Jeddah), the Islamic Development Bank, solidarity on Palestine and Kashmir.
- **Challenges**: sectarian conflict, rivalry (Saudi Arabia–Iran), weak OIC, economic disparity, foreign alignments, the 2026 Iran war dividing Gulf and other states.
- **Pan-Islamism**: Afghani, the Khilafat Movement (1919–24), Iqbal's "League of Muslim Nations".
