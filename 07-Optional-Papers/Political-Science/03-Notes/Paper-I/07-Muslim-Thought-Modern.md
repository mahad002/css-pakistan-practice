# 07. Modern Muslim Political Thought: Shah Waliullah, Iqbal, Jamaluddin Afghani, Rashid Rida

Asked: Shah Waliullah 2021, 2022 (with Farabi and Mawardi on the executive); Iqbal 2018 (ijtihad), 2023 (vision for South Asian Muslims). Afghani and Rida: not yet asked (MCQs: 2025 on Iqbal's Ummah; Afghani's pan-Islamism is a staple MCQ). Iqbal also appears in Paper II (`../Paper-II/11-Muslim-Nationalism-and-Pakistan-Movement.md`); here the focus is his political philosophy.

**Context.** From the 18th century the Muslim world declined politically: the Mughals collapsed, the Ottomans and Safavids weakened, and European empires advanced (British India from 1757; Napoleon in Egypt 1798; French Algeria 1830; British Egypt 1882). Muslim thinkers asked: why did we decline, and how can we revive? Answers ranged from moral-religious reform (Shah Waliullah) to pan-Islamic anti-imperialism (Afghani), reformed caliphate theory (Rida) and a philosophy of selfhood and a homeland (Iqbal).

## 1. SHAH WALIULLAH (1703–1762)

### Life and context
- Qutb al-Din Ahmad, known as Shah Waliullah, born in **1703** in Delhi's region; his father **Shah Abdur Rahim** founded the **Madrasa Rahimiyya** in Delhi and helped compile the *Fatawa-i-Alamgiri*. He memorised the Quran by seven and succeeded his father as teacher at 17.
- Studied in the **Hijaz** (1730–32), especially hadith under Shaykh Abu Tahir al-Kurdi al-Madani; there he had a vision that he would help reorganise the Muslim community.
- **Context**: after **Aurangzeb's death (1707)** the Mughal empire crumbled: weak emperors, court intrigues, rise of the **Marathas**, Jats and Sikhs; **Nadir Shah's sack of Delhi (1739)**; economic collapse; sectarian strife; moral decay.
- Wrote letters to **Ahmad Shah Abdali** of Afghanistan, Najib-ud-Daulah and Mughal nobles urging them to act against the Marathas; Abdali defeated the Marathas at the **Third Battle of Panipat (1761)**. Died in 1762.

### Key works
**Hujjat Allah al-Baligha** (*The Conclusive Argument of God*): his masterwork on the rational basis of the shari'a and society; **Izalat al-Khafa 'an Khilafat al-Khulafa** (on the caliphate); *al-Budur al-Bazigha*; *al-Fawz al-Kabir fi Usul al-Tafsir*; *Fath al-Rahman*: a **Persian translation of the Quran** (c. 1738), so ordinary literate Muslims could read it; *al-Tafhimat al-Ilahiyya*; *Maktubat* (letters).

### Socio-political thought (asked 2021)

**(a) Irtifaqat: stages of social development.** *Irtifaq* means the means by which human life is made easier and better.

| Stage | Name | Content |
|---|---|---|
| First | Primitive | Basic needs: food, shelter, language, family, simple agriculture and cooperation; shared by all humans |
| Second | Refined social life | Household economics, crafts, trade, rules of social conduct, ethics, marriage, manners: the life of towns |
| Third | The city-state (*siyasat al-madina*) | Government, laws, courts, police, army, taxation, public works to manage conflicts that arise in complex society; a ruler is needed |
| Fourth | International / universal | A **supreme caliphate** (*khilafat al-kubra*) over many rulers, to prevent wars between states and ensure justice among them |

This is an early theory of the **evolution of the state**, comparable to Aristotle's household–village–polis and to Ibn Khaldun's growth of civilisation.

**(b) Caliphate.**
- **Necessity**: the caliphate is a collective obligation required by the shari'a to uphold religion and manage worldly affairs.
- Two kinds: **khilafat-i khassa** (special): the ideal caliphate of the four Rightly Guided Caliphs, combining spiritual and temporal leadership (he argued its legitimacy against Shia critics in *Izalat al-Khafa*); **khilafat-i 'amma** (general): ordinary rule of later Muslim sovereigns.
- **Qualifications**: Muslim, adult, sane, free, male, brave, wise and capable in judgement, sound in hearing, sight and speech, knowledgeable in shari'a, of good character, and of **Quraysh**.
- **Methods**: election by the *ahl al-hall wa al-'aqd*; nomination by the predecessor; a **council** (like Umar's six); or **seizure of power** (*istila'*) if the ruler then fulfils the duties.
- **Duties**: defence and jihad, administration of justice, enforcement of *hudud*, collection and fair distribution of revenue, appointment of honest officials, public welfare, protection of religion.
- **Removal**: open unbelief or clear abandonment of shari'a justifies removal.

**(c) Economic causes of decline (his most original point).**
- **Heavy taxation** on peasants, traders and artisans drove them to ruin.
- **Parasitic classes** lived on the treasury without productive work: jagirdars, courtiers, soldiers paid for nothing, some poets, Sufis and ulama on state stipends.
- Concentration of wealth and luxury of the rich.
- **Remedies**: light, fair taxation; payment according to work; encouragement of agriculture, trade and crafts; balanced distribution of wealth. He saw economic justice as the base of a sound polity, a century before Marx.

**(d) Religious and social reform.**
- **Return to the Quran and Sunnah**; Persian translation of the Quran.
- **Ijtihad** and a harmonising approach to the four Sunni schools (*tatbiq*), reducing blind imitation.
- **Sectarian moderation**: reconcile Sunni–Shia differences and Sufi–ulama tensions, though he defended the Rashidun caliphs against Shia critiques.
- Reform of Sufism, removal of un-Islamic customs.

**(e) Political action.** Letters calling on Abdali and Muslim nobles to restore Muslim power; his aim was to revive a strong Muslim government in India.

### Legacy
His sons **Shah Abdul Aziz** (whose fatwa declared India under the British *dar al-harb*), Shah Rafiuddin and Shah Abdul Qadir (Urdu translations of the Quran); the **Mujahideen movement** of **Syed Ahmad Barelvi** and Shah Ismail Shaheed; the **Deoband** seminary (1866); indirectly Aligarh. Iqbal called him "perhaps the first Muslim who felt the urge of a new spirit in him".

### Critique
- Inviting **Abdali** brought plunder of Delhi and Afghan raids, not lasting Muslim revival.
- Caliphate theory largely traditional (Quraysh, *istila'*).
- Appeal mainly to an elite.
- Strength: first serious diagnosis of decline in moral, religious **and economic** terms; bridge between medieval and modern Muslim thought in South Asia.

## 2. THE "EXECUTIVE" IN AL-FARABI, AL-MAWARDI AND SHAH WALIULLAH (asked 2022)

| Point | Al-Farabi | Al-Mawardi | Shah Waliullah |
|---|---|---|---|
| Title of executive | First ruler / Imam (philosopher-prophet) | Imam / Caliph | Khalifa (and, in the general caliphate, the ruler/sultan) |
| Source of authority | Knowledge from the Active Intellect; perfection of reason and imagination | Shari'a; contract (*bay'a*) through electors or nomination | Shari'a and the needs of the third and fourth *irtifaq*; election, nomination, council or seizure |
| Qualifications | 12 natural qualities (body, intellect, memory, eloquence, love of learning and truth, temperance, magnanimity, indifference to wealth, justice, courage) | 7: justice, ijtihad-level knowledge, sound senses, sound limbs, judgement, courage, Quraysh | Muslim, adult, sane, free, male, brave, wise, knowledgeable, sound in senses, Quraysh |
| If no ideal person | Two rulers or a council of the virtuous; later "rulers of the sunna" follow law | Lesser conditions tolerated; amirate by seizure confirmed | General caliphate; ruler by seizure accepted if he performs duties |
| Functions | Lead the city to happiness; legislate; educate; organise the parts like the heart of a body | 10 duties: religion, justice, order, hudud, frontiers, jihad, revenue, salaries, appointments, supervision | Defence, justice, hudud, revenue and its fair distribution, appointments, welfare, international peace (supreme caliphate) |
| Delegation | Assisted by ranked subordinates | Delegated and executive viziers, governors, judges | Officials, judges, army; supreme caliph over lesser rulers |
| Accountability | Moral: must remain virtuous | Loses office through loss of justice or capacity (no procedure) | Removal for open unbelief or abandoning shari'a |
| Character | Philosophical, ideal | Legal, constitutional, realistic | Socio-economic, reformist, practical |
| Greek influence | Strong (Plato) | Little | Some (rational arguments), mainly Islamic |

**Conclusion line:** Farabi's executive is a philosopher-king, Mawardi's a legal officer of the shari'a, Shah Waliullah's a manager of a developing society with an economic and international mission.

## 3. SAYYID JAMALUDDIN AFGHANI (1838/39–1897)

### Life and context
- Born probably at **Asadabad, Iran** (he claimed Afghan origin, perhaps to appear Sunni and to escape Iranian control). Educated in Shia seminaries; visited India (perhaps around 1857).
- A restless activist across the Muslim world:
  - **Afghanistan** (1866–68): adviser to rulers; expelled.
  - **Istanbul** (1869–71): expelled after a lecture that angered the ulama.
  - **Egypt** (1871–79): teacher of **Muhammad Abduh** and a generation of nationalists; agitation against foreign control; expelled in 1879.
  - **India** (Hyderabad and Calcutta, 1879–82): wrote *Refutation of the Materialists* (*al-Radd 'ala al-Dahriyyin*), attacking Sir Syed's "naturalist" (*naichari*) school as pro-British.
  - **Paris** (1883–84): with Abduh published the journal **al-'Urwa al-Wuthqa** (*The Firmest Bond*, 1884, 18 issues); debated **Ernest Renan** on Islam and science (1883).
  - **Iran** (1886–91): clashed with **Naser al-Din Shah**; expelled; his letters helped inspire the **Tobacco Protest** (1891–92) against a British concession.
  - **Istanbul** (1892–97): guest of Sultan **Abdul Hamid II**, who used pan-Islamism; virtually under house arrest. A disciple assassinated Naser al-Din Shah in 1896. Afghani died in 1897.

### Core ideas
- **Pan-Islamism**: unity of the Muslim world (the Ummah) across states and sects as the answer to **European imperialism**, especially British. He called on Muslim rulers to unite.
- **Anti-imperialism**: Britain was the main enemy; he supported resistance in India, Egypt and Sudan.
- **Reason, science and Islam**: Islam is compatible with reason and science; decline came from stagnation, blind imitation (*taqlid*) and despotic rulers, not from Islam. Muslims must reopen **ijtihad** and acquire modern science and technology.
- **Constitutionalism**: limit despots; rulers must be accountable; consultative government. He supported constitutional movements (Egypt, Iran).
- **Activism**: ideas must lead to political action; he used journalism, secret societies and speeches.
- **Tactical nationalism**: at times urged Hindu–Muslim unity in India against Britain and Egyptian patriotism, alongside pan-Islam.

### Critique
- **Inconsistent and opportunistic**: courted rulers he later attacked; mixed pan-Islamism with local nationalism.
- Unclear religious position (debate with Renan suggested scepticism); membership of a Masonic lodge in Egypt.
- Left no systematic theory or lasting organisation.
- Unfair to Sir Syed's educational project.

### Legacy
Father of modern **Islamic modernism** and **pan-Islamism**: Muhammad Abduh, **Rashid Rida**, the Young Turks' pan-Islamic wing, the **Khilafat Movement** in India, **Iqbal** (who made Afghani a guide in *Javid Nama*), the **Muslim Brotherhood**, and Iranian revolutionary thought.

## 4. MUHAMMAD RASHID RIDA (1865–1935)

### Life and context
- Born in **Qalamun**, near Tripoli (now Lebanon), then Ottoman Syria. Influenced by *al-'Urwa al-Wuthqa*.
- Moved to **Cairo** in 1897 to become **Muhammad Abduh's** disciple. In 1898 founded the journal **al-Manar** (*The Lighthouse*), which he edited until his death (1935); with Abduh, began **Tafsir al-Manar**.
- President of the **Syrian Congress** (1920) under Faisal's short-lived Arab Kingdom of Syria, ended by France.
- Context: collapse of the Ottoman empire; the Turkish Grand National Assembly separated the caliphate from the sultanate (November 1922) and **abolished the caliphate (3 March 1924)**; British and French mandates; Saudi rise.

### Key works
**al-Khilafa aw al-Imama al-'Uzma** (*The Caliphate, or the Supreme Imamate*, 1922–23); *al-Manar* articles; *Tafsir al-Manar*.

### Core ideas
- **Salafiyya**: return to the Quran, Sunnah and the practice of the pious predecessors (*salaf*); reject *taqlid* and later innovations; reopen **ijtihad**.
- **The caliphate**:
  - Necessary by shari'a; the true caliph should be a **mujtahid** from Quraysh, chosen by the community.
  - Distinguishes the **ideal caliphate** from a "**caliphate of necessity**" (*khilafat al-darura*) accepted when the ideal is impossible.
  - The **ahl al-hall wa al-'aqd** should be a **representative body**, chosen by the community, acting as a consultative and legislative assembly: he linked classical theory to modern representative government.
  - Proposed a practical plan: a caliph based in a neutral region between Arabs and Turks [verify exact location proposed], and an institute to train candidates in religious and modern sciences.
- **Shura** is obligatory; it is Islam's form of consultation and close to constitutional, representative government.
- **Islamic government** (*hukuma islamiyya*): a state that legislates through ijtihad within shari'a, considering public interest (*maslaha*). This idea bridges classical caliphate theory and modern "Islamic state" thought.
- **Opposed Kemalist secularism** and Western imperialism; supported Arab revival; later sympathised with the Saudi-Wahhabi state.

### Critique
- Utopian caliphate plan; Quraysh condition; tensions between modernism and Salafi conservatism; later sectarian and anti-Shia positions.

### Legacy
Shaped **Hasan al-Banna** (founder of the **Muslim Brotherhood**, 1928) and the modern **Islamic state** concept; influenced debates in Pakistan through Maududi's generation.

## 5. ALLAMA MUHAMMAD IQBAL (1877–1938)

### Life and context
- Born in **Sialkot** on 9 November 1877. Studied at Scotch Mission College, Sialkot (under **Mir Hasan**), and **Government College Lahore** (MA philosophy, 1899; influenced by Sir Thomas Arnold).
- In Europe (1905–08): BA at Trinity College, **Cambridge**; PhD at **Munich** (1907) with the thesis *The Development of Metaphysics in Persia*; barrister at Lincoln's Inn (1908).
- Returned convinced that Western nationalism and materialism were destructive and that Muslims needed spiritual revival.
- Member of the Punjab Legislative Council (1927–30) [verify years; he was elected in 1926]. President of the **All-India Muslim League session at Allahabad (29 December 1930)**. Attended the **2nd and 3rd Round Table Conferences** (1931, 1932). Letters to **Jinnah** (1936–37). Died in Lahore on **21 April 1938**.

### Key works
Persian poetry: *Asrar-i-Khudi* (1915), *Rumuz-i-Bekhudi* (1918), *Payam-i-Mashriq* (1923), *Zabur-i-Ajam* (1927), *Javid Nama* (1932). Urdu: *Bang-i-Dara* (1924), *Bal-i-Jibril* (1935), *Zarb-i-Kalim* (1936), *Armaghan-i-Hijaz* (1938). Prose: **The Reconstruction of Religious Thought in Islam** (lectures 1928–29; published 1930; seventh lecture added in the 1934 Oxford edition).

### Core political ideas

**(a) Khudi (selfhood).** The self is real and must be developed, not dissolved (against passive mysticism). Three stages: **obedience** to divine law (*ita'at*); **self-control** (*zabt-i nafs*); **divine vicegerency** (*niyabat-i ilahi*), producing the **Mard-i-Momin**, the perfect believer. A nation of strong selves can throw off slavery.

**(b) Bekhudi (the self in the community).** The individual finds fulfilment in the **millat** (community). The Muslim community rests on **Tawhid** (unity of God) and **Risalat** (prophethood and its finality), not on territory, race or language.

**(c) Critique of territorial nationalism.** In *Bang-i-Dara* ("Wataniyat") he condemned the homeland as "the biggest of these new gods", because European nationalism divides humanity and the Ummah. Yet he accepted that Indian Muslims were a **distinct nation** within India and needed a political home; he reconciled these by treating Muslim nationhood as cultural-religious, not racial.

**(d) The state in Islam.** "The state, according to Islam, is only an effort to realise the spiritual in a human organisation." Islam rejects the dualism of Church and State; the ultimate reality is spiritual and is realised in secular activity. Not a **theocracy** ruled by priests, but a state based on **equality, solidarity and freedom**.

**(e) Ijtihad (asked 2018).** In the *Reconstruction* (lecture 6, "The Principle of Movement in the Structure of Islam"):
- Ijtihad is "the principle of movement in the structure of Islam". Its closure after the fall of Baghdad was a defensive mistake that led to stagnation.
- **Sources of law**: Quran, Hadith, **Ijma** and **Qiyas**, all to be reinterpreted for modern conditions.
- **Transfer of ijtihad from individuals to a Muslim legislative assembly**: "The transfer of the power of Ijtihad from individual representatives of schools to a Muslim legislative assembly… is the only possible form Ijma can take in modern times." Ulama should participate and guide, but the elected assembly decides.
- He praised the **Turkish Grand National Assembly's** ijtihad in vesting the caliphate in an assembly (while criticising the later secularism).
- **How it can be applied today**: elected parliament as the body of collective ijtihad, with expert advice (Pakistan's **Council of Islamic Ideology**, Art. 228–230, and the **Federal Shariat Court**, Art. 203); reform of family law (Muslim Family Laws Ordinance 1961), Islamic banking, bioethics; education producing scholars who know both fiqh and modern sciences.

**(f) Democracy.** He welcomed spiritual democracy and equality as Islamic ideals but criticised Western democracy as materialist and quantitative: "a form of government in which men are counted, not weighed" (paraphrase of his Urdu verse in *Zarb-i-Kalim*). He also called for the election of a legislative assembly as the modern form of Ijma.

**(g) Critique of capitalism and communism.** Both are materialist; he admired communism's attack on exploitation ("Lenin before God" in *Bal-i-Jibril*) but rejected its atheism.

**(h) The Allahabad Address (29 December 1930).** "I would like to see the Punjab, North-West Frontier Province, Sind and Baluchistan amalgamated into a single State. Self-government within the British Empire, or without the British Empire, the formation of a consolidated North-West Indian Muslim State appears to me to be the final destiny of the Muslims at least of North-West India." Basis: Indian Muslims form a distinct cultural unit; Western democracy based on majority rule cannot work in India without recognising communal groups.

**(i) Letters to Jinnah (1936–37).** Urged a separate federation of Muslim provinces and argued that the shari'a's enforcement and Muslim economic problems required a free Muslim state.

### Vision for the rise of South Asian Muslims under colonialism (asked 2023)
1. **Self-awakening** (khudi) against the slave mentality created by colonial rule.
2. **Unity** of the Ummah; rejection of sectarian, regional and caste divisions.
3. **Dynamic Islam** through ijtihad, not blind imitation; criticism of mullahs and pirs who preached passivity.
4. **Education** and science, while rejecting Western materialism; youth as **shaheen** (falcons).
5. **Economic justice** (critique of feudalism and capitalism).
6. **Political organisation**: support for the Muslim League; a separate Muslim state in the north-west.
7. **Spiritual democracy** as the ideal polity.

### Critique
- Poetic language allows conflicting interpretations (pan-Islamist vs Muslim nationalist; modernist vs traditionalist).
- Allahabad: a state "within or without the British Empire"—some argue he meant a province within an Indian federation, not full partition; his letters to Jinnah suggest a separate federation.
- Ijtihad by elected legislators: are they qualified? Ulama objected; secularists also objected.
- Tension between condemning nationalism and supporting a national homeland.

### Relevance
Pakistan's ideology; constitutional Islamic provisions; the Council of Islamic Ideology as institutionalised ijtihad; youth and self-reliance; critique of dependency.

## 6. COMPARISON OF MODERN THINKERS

| | Shah Waliullah | Afghani | Rida | Iqbal |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Diagnosis of decline | Moral decay, economic exploitation, sectarianism, weak rulers | Stagnation, despotism, disunity, imperialism | Departure from the salaf, loss of caliphate, imitation | Loss of khudi, closure of ijtihad, slavery mentality |
| Remedy | Return to Quran/Sunnah, ijtihad, economic justice, strong Muslim rule | Pan-Islamic unity, science, constitutionalism, revolt against imperialism | Salafi reform, reformed caliphate, shura | Selfhood, ijtihad by assembly, Muslim homeland, spiritual democracy |
| Political unit | Caliphate and a supreme caliph over states | Ummah of united Muslim states | Caliphate with representative ahl al-hall | Muslim state(s) within a spiritual Ummah ("League of Nations of Muslims") |
| Method | Scholarship, letters to rulers | Agitation, journalism | Journalism, scholarship | Poetry, philosophy, politics |
