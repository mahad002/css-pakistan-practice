## PART 1: KARL MARX (1818–1883)

### 1.1 Life and context

- Born in **Trier**, Prussian Rhineland, to a Jewish family that had converted to Protestantism.
- Studied law and philosophy at Bonn and Berlin; joined the **Young Hegelians**. Doctorate (Jena, 1841) on Democritus and Epicurus.
- Edited the *Rheinische Zeitung* (1842–43) until it was banned. Moved to Paris (1843), where in 1844 he began his lifelong partnership with **Friedrich Engels**, son of a textile mill owner who had studied the Manchester working class.
- Expelled from Paris, moved to Brussels; wrote **The Communist Manifesto** (published February 1848, on the eve of the 1848 revolutions).
- From 1849 lived in **London** in poverty, supported by Engels; researched in the British Museum. Helped found the **First International** (1864). Died 1883; buried at Highgate.
- Context: the **Industrial Revolution**: factories, child labour, slums, 14-hour days, recurring crises, the failed revolutions of 1848.

### 1.2 Key works

| Work | Year | Content |
|---|---|---|
| *Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts* | 1844 (pub. 1932) | Alienation |
| *The German Ideology* (with Engels) | 1845–46 | Historical materialism |
| *The Communist Manifesto* (with Engels) | 1848 | Class struggle, programme |
| *The Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Bonaparte* | 1852 | Class and the state in France; "men make their own history, but not as they please" |
| *A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy*, Preface | 1859 | Base and superstructure in brief |
| *Das Kapital*, vol. 1 | 1867 | Surplus value, exploitation (vols 2–3 edited by Engels, 1885, 1894) |
| *The Civil War in France* | 1871 | The Paris Commune as a model |
| *Critique of the Gotha Programme* | 1875 | Two phases of communism |

### 1.3 Historical materialism (asked 2017)

- "The mode of production of material life conditions the general process of social, political and intellectual life. It is not the consciousness of men that determines their being, but their social being that determines their consciousness" (1859 Preface, paraphrased). This is the statement the 2017 paper quoted.
- **Base** (economic structure): **forces of production** (labour, tools, technology, raw materials) + **relations of production** (ownership, who works for whom).
- **Superstructure**: law, state, politics, religion, morality, art, philosophy. It reflects and protects the base.
- History moves when the forces of production outgrow the relations of production; the relations become "fetters"; "then begins an epoch of social revolution".
- Stages: **primitive communism → ancient slave society → feudalism → capitalism → socialism → communism** (Marx also mentioned an "Asiatic mode of production").

*Diagram in words:* a two-storey building. Ground floor "Base: forces + relations of production". Upper floor "Superstructure: state, law, religion, ideology". Arrows up (base shapes superstructure) and a thin arrow down (superstructure protects base; Engels later admitted reciprocal influence).

### 1.4 Class and class struggle (asked 2020, 2022)

- "The history of all hitherto existing society is the history of class struggles" (Manifesto). Freeman and slave, patrician and plebeian, lord and serf, guild-master and journeyman.
- A **class** is defined by its relation to the means of production. Under capitalism: the **bourgeoisie** (owners) and the **proletariat** (who sell their labour power). Also the petty bourgeoisie (shopkeepers, artisans) and the *lumpenproletariat*.
- "Class in itself" (objective position) must become "class for itself" (conscious, organised).
- Capitalism simplifies society into "two great hostile camps". Competition drives small owners into the proletariat; workers are concentrated in factories, which makes organisation possible.

### 1.5 Economics: surplus value and exploitation

- **Labour theory of value**: the value of a commodity is set by the socially necessary labour time needed to produce it.
- The worker is paid the value of his **labour power** (enough to live and reproduce) but works longer: the extra is **surplus value**, taken by the capitalist as profit. This is exploitation, even if the wage contract is "free".
- Laws of capitalism: accumulation and concentration of capital; falling rate of profit; recurring crises of overproduction; growing **immiseration** of workers; a "reserve army" of unemployed.
- **Commodity fetishism**: relations between people appear as relations between things (prices, markets).

### 1.6 Alienation (1844)

Under capitalism the worker is alienated from: (1) the **product** of his labour (owned by another); (2) the **process** of labour (forced, monotonous); (3) his **species-being** (creative human nature); (4) **other people** (competition).

### 1.7 The state (asked 2022)

- The state is not neutral. "The executive of the modern state is but a committee for managing the common affairs of the whole bourgeoisie" (Manifesto).
- Political power is "the organised power of one class for oppressing another".
- In the *Eighteenth Brumaire* he allowed the state some **relative autonomy** when classes are balanced (Bonapartism).
- After revolution: the **dictatorship of the proletariat** (a transitional state in which workers rule; modelled on the Paris Commune: elected, recallable officials paid workers' wages).
- Under communism the state, no longer needed for class rule, will **wither away** (Engels' phrase, *Anti-Dühring*, 1878).

### 1.8 Religion (asked 2022)

- "Religion is the sigh of the oppressed creature, the heart of a heartless world… It is the **opium of the people**" (1843–44, *Contribution to the Critique of Hegel's Philosophy of Right*).
- Religion is part of the superstructure: it consoles the poor and justifies the existing order. It reflects real suffering and a protest against it.
- Abolishing religion as illusory happiness requires abolishing the conditions that need illusions. He did not call for persecuting believers; criticism of religion must become criticism of law and politics.

### 1.9 Revolution and communism

- Capitalism produces "its own grave-diggers". "Workers of the world, unite! You have nothing to lose but your chains."
- **Two phases** (*Critique of the Gotha Programme*): a lower phase (socialism), where each receives according to work; a higher phase (communism), "**from each according to his ability, to each according to his needs**".
- Communism: no private ownership of the means of production, no classes, no state, no division between mental and manual labour.

### 1.10 Critique

| Point | Criticism |
|---|---|
| Predictions | Revolutions came in backward peasant countries (Russia 1917, China 1949), not industrial England or Germany; workers' living standards rose; a large middle class grew |
| Determinism | Economics does not determine everything: religion, nationalism, ideas and individuals matter (Weber's *Protestant Ethic*) |
| Labour theory of value | Rejected by mainstream economics (marginal utility); ignores entrepreneurship, risk, demand |
| State | The "withering away" never happened; Marxist states became the most powerful states in history |
| Practice | Soviet and Maoist regimes produced dictatorship, famine and repression |
| Scientific status | Popper: historicism; adjusts to every failure, so it cannot be falsified |
| Revisionists | **Eduard Bernstein** (*Evolutionary Socialism*, 1899): socialism through parliament and reform, not revolution |
| Islamic view | Rejects materialism and atheism, but shares concern for justice and against exploitation (zakat, ban on riba, criticism of hoarding) |

### 1.11 Relevance today

- **Inequality**: Thomas Piketty, *Capital in the Twenty-First Century* (2013): returns on capital outpace growth. Oxfam reports on billionaire wealth.
- **Globalisation**: the Manifesto's account of capital spreading worldwide reads like a description of today.
- **Dependency and world-systems theory** (Frank, Wallerstein), relevant to Pakistan's debt and IMF dependence.
- **Labour rights**, unions, the welfare state (partly a response to Marxism).
- **China**: the CPC still calls Marxism its guiding ideology, alongside "socialism with Chinese characteristics" and Xi Jinping Thought.
- **Pakistan**: analyses of feudal landlords, the military's economic role and the "overdeveloped state" (Hamza Alavi, 1972) are Marxist in method.

## PART 2: VLADIMIR ILYICH LENIN (1870–1924)

### 2.1 Life and context

- Born Vladimir Ulyanov in **Simbirsk**, Russia. His elder brother Alexander was hanged in 1887 for plotting against the Tsar.
- Exiled to Siberia (1897–1900), then lived abroad. At the 1903 congress of the Russian Social Democratic Labour Party the **Bolsheviks** (majority, led by Lenin) split from the **Mensheviks**.
- Returned to Russia in April 1917 (with German help) after the February Revolution; issued the **April Theses** ("All power to the Soviets"; "peace, land and bread").
- Led the **October Revolution** (25 October Old Style = 7 November 1917). Signed the **Treaty of Brest-Litovsk** with Germany (March 1918). Won the Civil War (1918–21) under "War Communism"; then the **New Economic Policy** (1921) allowed some private trade. USSR formed in December 1922. Died January 1924.

### 2.2 Key works

*What Is to Be Done?* (1902); *Imperialism, the Highest Stage of Capitalism* (written 1916, published 1917); **The State and Revolution** (1917); *"Left-Wing" Communism: An Infantile Disorder* (1920).

### 2.3 Core ideas

**(a) The vanguard party.** Left alone, workers develop only "trade-union consciousness" (better wages), not revolutionary consciousness. A **party of professional revolutionaries**, disciplined and centralised, must bring socialist consciousness "from outside" and lead the class.

**(b) Democratic centralism.** Free discussion before a decision; once the majority decides, iron discipline and no factions; lower bodies obey higher ones. (Still the organising principle of the Communist Party of China; CSS 2025 MCQ.)

**(c) Imperialism.** Capitalism has entered a monopoly stage. Five features: concentration of production into monopolies; merger of bank and industrial capital into **finance capital**; **export of capital** (not just goods); international cartels dividing the world; territorial division of the world among great powers. This explains World War I (a war for colonies) and why Western workers were "bought off" with colonial super-profits (a **labour aristocracy**). Revolution can therefore break out at the "**weakest link**" of the imperialist chain: Russia.

**(d) The State and Revolution.** The state is "a product of the irreconcilability of class antagonisms". The proletariat cannot simply take over the bourgeois state; it must **smash** it and build a new one: the **dictatorship of the proletariat**, democracy for the majority and repression of the exploiters. Then the state withers away.

**(e) Self-determination of nations**, including secession (in theory).

### 2.4 Critique

- **Rosa Luxemburg**: Lenin's centralism would lead to the dictatorship of a party, then of a few leaders; "freedom only for the supporters of the government… is no freedom at all".
- **Karl Kautsky** and the Mensheviks: Russia was not ready for socialism; a minority seizing power would need terror.
- The Red Terror, banning of other parties (1921), and the path to Stalin.
- Imperialism theory: capital mostly flowed between rich countries, not to colonies.

### 2.5 Relevance

The **party-state** model (USSR, China, Vietnam, Cuba, North Korea); anti-imperialist and national liberation movements in Asia and Africa; the vocabulary of "imperialism" in global South politics.

## PART 3: MAO ZEDONG (1893–1976)

### 3.1 Life and context

- Born to a peasant family in **Shaoshan, Hunan**. Librarian's assistant at Peking University; attended the founding congress of the **Communist Party of China** (Shanghai, 1921).
- After the Kuomintang (KMT) turned on the communists (1927), led peasant uprisings and rural bases (Jiangxi).
- **Long March** (1934–35), about 10,000 km [verify distance; commonly given as 9,000–12,500 km]; became leader at the Zunyi Conference (1935). Fought Japan (1937–45) and then the KMT.
- Proclaimed the **People's Republic of China** on **1 October 1949**.
- Hundred Flowers campaign (1956–57) followed by the Anti-Rightist purge; **Great Leap Forward** (1958–62) and a famine that killed tens of millions (estimates range from about 15 to 45 million); **Cultural Revolution** (1966–76). Died 1976.

### 3.2 Key works

*Report on an Investigation of the Peasant Movement in Hunan* (1927); *On Practice* and *On Contradiction* (1937); *On Protracted War* (1938); *On New Democracy* (1940); *Quotations from Chairman Mao* ("Little Red Book", 1964).

### 3.3 Core ideas

**(a) Peasants as the revolutionary force.** In semi-feudal, semi-colonial China the peasantry, not the small urban proletariat, would make the revolution, under party leadership. This was the key "sinification" of Marxism.

**(b) People's war.** "Political power grows out of the barrel of a gun." **Protracted war** in three stages: strategic defensive (guerrilla war from rural bases), strategic stalemate, strategic counter-offensive. "Encircle the cities from the countryside." Guerrillas must move among the people "as a fish swims in the sea".

**(c) The mass line.** "From the masses, to the masses": gather the people's scattered ideas, systematise them, return them as policy, test them in action.

**(d) On Contradiction.** Contradictions exist everywhere; there is a **principal contradiction** at each stage; contradictions are **antagonistic** (with enemies, resolved by force) or **non-antagonistic** (among the people, resolved by persuasion).

**(e) New Democracy.** A transitional stage: a coalition of four classes (workers, peasants, petty bourgeoisie, national bourgeoisie) under communist leadership, before socialism.

**(f) Continuous revolution.** Revolution must continue after the seizure of power to prevent a new bourgeoisie inside the party: the justification for the Cultural Revolution.

**(g) Three Worlds theory** (1974): the superpowers (first world), other developed states (second), and developing countries including China (third).

### 3.4 Critique

Catastrophic human costs (Great Leap famine, Cultural Revolution); personality cult; voluntarism (belief that will can overcome material limits); the CPC's own 1981 resolution judged Mao's record "70% right, 30% wrong" and condemned the Cultural Revolution.

### 3.5 Relevance

Guerrilla and insurgent movements (Vietnam, Nepal's Maoists, India's Naxalites); Deng Xiaoping's reforms (1978) kept the party but abandoned Maoist economics; today Mao Zedong Thought is still written into the CPC constitution. Pakistan: Mao-era friendship with China began the "all-weather" relationship (border agreement 1963).

## PART 4: ANTONIO GRAMSCI (1891–1937)

### 4.1 Life and context

- Born in **Sardinia**, poor and disabled from a childhood illness. Studied in **Turin**, Italy's industrial centre.
- Edited *L'Ordine Nuovo*; supported the factory councils of the "Red Biennium" (1919–20). Co-founded the **Communist Party of Italy** (1921) and became its leader and a deputy.
- Arrested by **Mussolini's** fascist regime in 1926. The prosecutor reportedly said, "We must stop this brain from working for twenty years." In prison he wrote about 30 notebooks (*Prison Notebooks*). Released, gravely ill, shortly before his death in 1937.
- His question: why did revolution succeed in Russia but fail in the West, where fascism won instead?

### 4.2 Core ideas

**(a) Hegemony.** The ruling class rules not only by **force** (coercion through the state: police, army, courts) but above all by **consent**: intellectual and moral leadership that makes its worldview seem like "**common sense**" to everyone. (CSS 2025 MCQ: the "theory of hegemony" — Gramsci.)

**(b) Political society and civil society.** The state in the narrow sense (political society) uses coercion; **civil society** (churches, schools, media, parties, unions, families, clubs) is where consent is manufactured. "State = political society + civil society, hegemony protected by the armour of coercion."

**(c) War of manoeuvre and war of position.** In Russia, the state was everything and civil society "primordial", so a frontal assault (war of manoeuvre) worked. In the West, civil society is a strong system of "trenches"; a revolutionary party must first fight a long **war of position** to win cultural and moral leadership before taking power.

**(d) Intellectuals.** **Traditional** intellectuals (clergy, academics) present themselves as independent but serve the existing order; **organic** intellectuals arise from and articulate the interests of a class. Every class needs its organic intellectuals.

**(e) Historic bloc.** An alliance of classes and social groups held together by a shared worldview, uniting base and superstructure.

**(f) Passive revolution.** Change "from above" in which the ruling class absorbs and neutralises pressures for change without mass participation (Italy's unification, the Risorgimento).

**(g) The Modern Prince.** The revolutionary party is today's Machiavellian prince: a collective will.

### 4.3 Critique

Fragmentary, coded prison writing open to many readings; downplays coercion and economics (some Marxists); used by both radical and moderate movements.

### 4.4 Relevance

- **Media and cultural studies**: how consent is produced through television, schools, social media.
- **Neo-Gramscian IR** (Robert Cox): US hegemony in the world order rests on consent through institutions (IMF, WTO) as well as power.
- **Soft power** (Nye) resembles hegemony.
- **Pakistan**: how a dominant "establishment" narrative becomes common sense through textbooks and media; the role of religious and intellectual elites; civil society as a site of contest.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Revolutionary agent | Industrial proletariat | Proletariat led by vanguard party | Peasantry led by party | Workers' historic bloc led by party and organic intellectuals |
