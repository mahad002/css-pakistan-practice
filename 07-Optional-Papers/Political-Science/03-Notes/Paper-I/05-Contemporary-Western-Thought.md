# 05. Modern and Contemporary Western Thinkers: Popper, Bourdieu, Rawls, Fukuyama, Foucault, Derrida, Kierkegaard, Sartre, Descartes

Asked: Fukuyama 2016 (note "End of History"), 2026 (long); Sartre and Descartes 2023 (notes). Rawls, Popper, Bourdieu, Foucault, Derrida and Kierkegaard so far only in MCQs (2025: Popper's *Open Society*; 2026: Theory of Justice as the alternative to utilitarianism). These names were added to the syllabus in 2016; expect one of them as a note or long question in any year.

The syllabus lists them in no historical order. Here they are in date order, which makes the ideas easier to connect: **Descartes** (17th c.) → **Kierkegaard** (19th c.) → **Sartre** → **Popper** → **Rawls** → **Foucault**, **Derrida**, **Bourdieu** → **Fukuyama**.

## 1. RENÉ DESCARTES (1596–1650)

### Life and context
- Born at La Haye (now Descartes), France. Educated by the Jesuits at La Flèche. Served as a gentleman soldier during the Thirty Years' War era. Lived about 20 years in the Netherlands, where thought was freer. Died in Stockholm (1650) while tutoring Queen Christina of Sweden.
- Lived through the scientific revolution (Galileo was condemned in 1633) and religious wars: old certainties were collapsing.

### Key works
*Discourse on the Method* (1637, in French: "je pense, donc je suis"); *Meditations on First Philosophy* (1641); *Principles of Philosophy* (1644, in Latin: "**cogito, ergo sum**"); *Passions of the Soul* (1649). Also founded analytic geometry (Cartesian coordinates).

### Core ideas
- **Methodic doubt**: doubt everything that can be doubted—senses (they deceive), the external world (I may be dreaming), even mathematics (an "evil demon" may deceive me)—to find a foundation that cannot be doubted.
- **Cogito, ergo sum** ("I think, therefore I am"): even if I doubt everything, the fact that I am doubting proves that I, a thinking thing, exist. This is the first certain truth.
- From the cogito he argues to God (a perfect being who would not deceive) and then to the reliability of clear and distinct ideas and the external world.
- **Four rules of method**: accept only what is clear and distinct; divide problems into parts; proceed from simple to complex; review completely.
- **Mind–body dualism**: mind (thinking substance) and body (extended substance) are distinct.
- **Provisional morality**: while doubting, obey the laws and customs of one's country and religion, be firm in action, master oneself rather than fortune.

### Political significance
Descartes wrote little politics and was cautious. His importance is indirect:
- Founds **modern rationalism** and the autonomous individual subject who judges by reason, not authority: the root of the Enlightenment, liberal individualism and secular government.
- His mechanical view of nature influenced **Hobbes** (who debated him) and the scientific study of society.
- The cogito is the starting point that later thinkers attack: Kierkegaard (existence before thought), Sartre (builds on it), Foucault and Derrida (deconstruct the self-sufficient subject).
- **Muslim parallel**: al-Ghazali used methodic doubt five centuries earlier in *al-Munqidh min al-Dalal* (Deliverance from Error) to reach certainty through divine light rather than reason alone. A good point for an answer.

### Critique
Gilbert Ryle mocked dualism as "the ghost in the machine"; doubting everything may lead to solipsism; "thinking" presupposes language and society; feminists and postmodernists criticise the disembodied, universal "rational man".

### Short note: "Cogito, ergo sum" (2023 format)
Meaning → method of doubt → certainty of the thinking self → God and the world → significance (modern philosophy, individualism, Enlightenment) → criticism (Ryle, Kierkegaard: "I exist, therefore I think"; Ghazali parallel).

## 2. SØREN KIERKEGAARD (1813–1855)

### Life and context
- Born in **Copenhagen**, Denmark; son of a wealthy, melancholic, deeply religious father. Broke off his engagement to Regine Olsen (1841), an event that shaped his work. Wrote under many pseudonyms. Spent his last years attacking the comfortable Danish state church. Died at 42.
- Reacting against **Hegel's** grand "system", bourgeois complacency and the new mass press.

### Key works
*Either/Or* (1843); *Fear and Trembling* (1843); *The Concept of Anxiety* (1844); *Concluding Unscientific Postscript* (1846); *Two Ages* / *The Present Age* (1846); *The Sickness unto Death* (1849).

### Core ideas
- **Father of existentialism**: the starting point of philosophy is the concrete, choosing individual, not abstract systems.
- "**Subjectivity is truth**": the truth that matters is the truth one lives by and commits to passionately.
- **Three stages (spheres) of existence**: **aesthetic** (pleasure, novelty, ends in boredom and despair); **ethical** (duty, commitment, social roles); **religious** (a personal relation to God reached by a "**leap of faith**").
- *Fear and Trembling*: Abraham's willingness to sacrifice Isaac shows the "teleological suspension of the ethical": the "knight of faith" stands as an individual before God above universal ethics.
- **Anxiety** is "the dizziness of freedom"; **despair** is the "sickness unto death", failing to be oneself.

### Political ideas
- *The Present Age*: modern society is marked by **levelling**: the individual dissolves into "**the public**", an abstraction created by the press. "**The crowd is untruth**." Reflection replaces passion and action.
- Critique of mass democracy, public opinion and conformity; responsibility belongs to individuals, not crowds.
- Attack on **state religion**: Christianity reduced to a civil institution is no longer Christianity.

### Critique
Apolitical and individualist; irrationalist; offers no social programme. Yet he influenced Heidegger, Sartre, Camus, theology (Barth) and critiques of mass society.

### Relevance
Social-media mobs and "cancel culture" as the anonymous crowd; populism's appeal to the masses; personal responsibility against conformity.

## 3. JEAN-PAUL SARTRE (1905–1980)

### Life and context
- Born in Paris; studied at the École Normale Supérieure; lifelong partner of **Simone de Beauvoir** (*The Second Sex*, 1949). Prisoner of war (1940–41), then in the Resistance milieu.
- Post-war France was shaken by occupation, collaboration and guilt. Sartre became the most famous public intellectual of the age. **Refused the Nobel Prize in Literature (1964)**. Supported Algerian independence, wrote the preface to Frantz Fanon's *The Wretched of the Earth* (1961), and sat on the Russell Tribunal on the Vietnam War.

### Key works
*Nausea* (1938); **Being and Nothingness** (1943); *No Exit* (1944: "Hell is other people"); "**Existentialism Is a Humanism**" (lecture, 29 October 1945; published 1946); *Critique of Dialectical Reason* (1960).

### Core ideas
- "**Existence precedes essence**": a paper-knife is made to a design (essence first); man has no God-given design. He first exists, then defines himself by his choices. "Man is nothing else but what he makes of himself."
- **Condemned to be free**: we cannot escape choice; not choosing is also a choice.
- **Responsibility**: in choosing for myself I choose an image of man for all; I am responsible for all humanity.
- **Anguish** (awareness of this responsibility), **abandonment** (no God to guide us; "everything is permitted if God does not exist", taken from Dostoevsky), **despair** (we can rely only on what depends on our will).
- **Bad faith** (*mauvaise foi*): denying our freedom by pretending we are things (the café waiter who plays the role too perfectly; "I had no choice").
- **Commitment** (*engagement*): writers and intellectuals must take sides in political struggles.

### The 2023 note: "Existentialism is a humanism"
- **Purpose of the lecture**: to answer critics. Communists said existentialism was bourgeois, quietist and despairing; Catholics said it denied human dignity and morality.
- **Argument**: existentialism is humanism because it places man at the centre as the maker of himself and his values; it is optimistic since it puts destiny in man's hands; it is a doctrine of action, not despair.
- **Not** the humanism that worships "man" as a finished end-value (which leads to fascism, he said), but man constantly going beyond himself ("self-surpassing").
- **Criticism**: Heidegger's *Letter on Humanism* (1946) rejected the label; critics say values become arbitrary; Sartre later moved towards Marxism, admitting that material conditions limit freedom.

### Political relevance
Freedom and responsibility against fatalism; anti-colonial movements; dignity of the individual; the "committed intellectual". In Pakistan's context: personal responsibility versus fatalistic attitudes in political culture.

## 4. KARL POPPER (1902–1994)

### Life and context
- Born in **Vienna** to an assimilated Jewish family. Briefly a Marxist as a teenager; turned against it after seeing young communists killed in a 1919 demonstration.
- Fled Nazism to **New Zealand** (1937–45), where he wrote his political works. Then **London School of Economics** (1946–69). Knighted in 1965.

### Key works
*The Logic of Scientific Discovery* (1934 in German; English 1959); **The Open Society and Its Enemies** (1945; vol. 1 Plato, vol. 2 Hegel and Marx) (CSS 2025 MCQ); *The Poverty of Historicism* (1957, articles 1944–45); *Conjectures and Refutations* (1963).

### Core ideas
- **Falsifiability**: a theory is scientific only if it can be tested and possibly refuted. Marxism and psychoanalysis explain everything, so they cannot be falsified, so they are not science.
- **Critical rationalism**: knowledge grows by conjecture and refutation; we learn from mistakes.
- **Historicism** (the claim that history follows laws that allow us to predict its course) is false and dangerous: Plato, Hegel and Marx are its main sources, and it leads to totalitarianism.
- **Open society**: individuals make decisions, criticise authority and institutions, and can change rulers. **Closed society**: tribal, magical, governed by fixed taboos and an elite claiming special knowledge.
- **Democracy** redefined: the key question is not "who should rule?" (Plato) but "**how can we get rid of bad rulers without bloodshed?**"
- **Piecemeal social engineering**: reform step by step, fight concrete evils, learn from mistakes; reject **utopian** (holistic) engineering, which needs force.
- **Paradox of tolerance**: unlimited tolerance leads to the disappearance of tolerance; a tolerant society may refuse to tolerate the intolerant if they cannot be met by argument.
- Negative utilitarianism: minimise suffering rather than maximise happiness.

### Critique
His readings of Plato and Hegel are called caricatures; Thomas Kuhn and others showed science does not work only by falsification; piecemeal reform may be too slow for deep injustice.

### Relevance
Defence of liberal democracy against populism and authoritarianism; George Soros (his student) named the **Open Society Foundations** after him; evidence-based policy and pilot projects; the paradox of tolerance in debates on hate speech and extremist parties.

## 5. JOHN RAWLS (1921–2002)

### Life and context
- Born in **Baltimore**, USA. Studied at Princeton; served as an infantryman in the Pacific in World War II (saw the aftermath of Hiroshima). Taught at **Harvard** for four decades.
- Wrote during the US civil-rights movement and the Vietnam War. Revived political philosophy after decades dominated by utilitarianism and linguistic analysis.

### Key works
**A Theory of Justice** (1971); *Political Liberalism* (1993); *The Law of Peoples* (1999); *Justice as Fairness: A Restatement* (2001).

### Core ideas
- **Justice as fairness**: justice is "the first virtue of social institutions". Rawls rejects utilitarianism because it can sacrifice individuals for the sum of welfare; it "does not take seriously the distinction between persons".
- **The original position**: imagine free, equal, rational people choosing principles of justice behind a **veil of ignorance**: they do not know their class, race, sex, talents, religion or idea of the good life. Since no one can tailor principles to himself, the choice is fair.
- They would choose by the **maximin** rule: make the worst possible position as good as possible.
- **Two principles of justice**:
  1. **Equal liberty**: each person has an equal right to the most extensive scheme of basic liberties (speech, conscience, vote, person, property) compatible with the same for all.
  2. Social and economic inequalities are allowed only if (a) attached to offices open to all under **fair equality of opportunity**, and (b) they are to the greatest benefit of the **least advantaged** (the **difference principle**).
- **Lexical priority**: the first principle comes before the second; liberty cannot be traded for economic gain.
- **Primary goods**: rights, liberties, opportunities, income, wealth and the social bases of self-respect.
- **Reflective equilibrium**: adjust principles and considered judgements until they fit.
- *Political Liberalism*: in a society of many religions and philosophies (**reasonable pluralism**), stability needs an **overlapping consensus** on political principles and **public reason**.
- *The Law of Peoples*: liberal peoples, **decent hierarchical peoples** (non-liberal but peaceful and rights-respecting, with a consultation hierarchy—he gave a hypothetical Muslim example, "Kazanistan"), burdened societies (owed assistance), outlaw states.

### Critique
| Critic | Objection |
|---|---|
| **Robert Nozick**, *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* (1974) | Entitlement theory: a distribution is just if holdings were acquired and transferred justly; redistribution violates rights; taxation of earnings is "on a par with forced labour" |
| **Communitarians** (Michael Sandel, Alasdair MacIntyre, Michael Walzer, Charles Taylor) | The "unencumbered self" behind the veil is unreal; people are formed by communities, traditions and religion |
| **Amartya Sen**, *The Idea of Justice* (2009) | Focus on what people can actually do and be (**capabilities**), and on reducing manifest injustice rather than designing perfect institutions |
| **Feminists** (Susan Moller Okin) | Ignores justice within the family |
| Utilitarians, libertarians, Marxists | Too much or too little equality |

### Relevance
Welfare states, progressive taxation, affirmative action; Pakistan's **BISP** and targeted transfers, regional quotas in the civil service, free education under **Article 25-A**; "the alternative to utilitarianism" (CSS 2026 MCQ). Islamic link: Islam's concern for the poor (zakat, *bayt al-mal*) resembles the difference principle, though grounded in divine command, not a hypothetical contract.

## 6. MICHEL FOUCAULT (1926–1984)

### Life and context
- Born in **Poitiers**, France; École Normale Supérieure. Taught in Sweden, Poland, Germany and Tunisia. Elected to the Collège de France (1970) as professor of the "History of Systems of Thought". Activist for prisoners' rights (1970s). Died in 1984 of an AIDS-related illness.

### Key works
*Madness and Civilization* (1961); *The Birth of the Clinic* (1963); *The Order of Things* (1966); *The Archaeology of Knowledge* (1969); **Discipline and Punish** (1975); **The History of Sexuality**, vol. 1 (1976); Collège de France lectures, especially *Society Must Be Defended* (1976), *Security, Territory, Population* (1977–78) and *The Birth of Biopolitics* (1978–79).

### Core ideas
- **Power is not a thing** held by the king or a class; it is a relation, exercised everywhere, "capillary", flowing through institutions, practices and discourses. "Power is everywhere… because it comes from everywhere."
- **Power is productive**, not only repressive: it produces knowledge, identities, norms and "normal" people.
- **Power/knowledge**: power and knowledge imply each other. Medicine, psychiatry, criminology and statistics both arise from and support systems of control.
- **Discourse**: rules about what can be said and who can say it, which define truth in an era.
- **Discipline and Punish**: from public torture (the execution of Damiens, 1757) to the modern **prison**. Modern **disciplinary power** works through **hierarchical observation**, **normalising judgement** and the **examination**, producing "**docile bodies**" in prisons, schools, armies, factories and hospitals. Bentham's **Panopticon** is the model: the prisoner, never knowing if he is watched, polices himself.
- **Biopower / biopolitics**: from the 18th century, states manage the life of **populations**: birth rates, health, hygiene, life expectancy, statistics.
- **Governmentality**: the art of government that works through the conduct of conduct: shaping how people govern themselves (neoliberalism makes individuals entrepreneurs of themselves).
- **Genealogy**: tracing the contingent, power-laden history of ideas we take as natural.
- "Where there is power, there is resistance."

### Critique
Habermas: no normative standpoint from which to criticise power; if everything is power, why resist? Leaves no room for agency or truth; his histories are selective; Chomsky–Foucault debate (1971) on human nature and justice.

### Relevance
Mass **surveillance** (NSA, China's social credit experiments, Pakistan's Safe City cameras and NADRA biometric databases, phone-tapping debates); COVID-19 lockdowns and vaccine passes as biopolitics; prison and police reform; social media algorithms as disciplinary technologies; "data colonialism".

## 7. JACQUES DERRIDA (1930–2004)

### Life and context
- Born to a Jewish family in **El Biar, Algeria** (then French). Expelled from school in 1942 under Vichy anti-Jewish laws. Studied at the École Normale Supérieure. Taught in Paris and at US universities (Yale, Johns Hopkins, Irvine).
- In 1967 published three books that launched **deconstruction**. In 1992 his honorary degree at Cambridge was opposed by a group of philosophers, then approved by vote.

### Key works
*Of Grammatology*, *Writing and Difference*, *Speech and Phenomena* (all 1967); *Margins of Philosophy* (1972); "Force of Law" (1989); **Specters of Marx** (1993, a reply to Fukuyama); *Politics of Friendship* (1994); *Rogues* (2003).

### Core ideas
- **Logocentrism**: Western thought privileges presence, speech, reason and a fixed centre of meaning.
- **Binary oppositions** are hierarchies: speech/writing, reason/emotion, man/woman, West/East, civilised/barbarian. Deconstruction reverses and then displaces them, showing the "inferior" term is inside the "superior" one.
- **Différance**: meaning arises from differences between signs and is always **deferred**; it is never fully present or final.
- "**There is nothing outside the text**" (*il n'y a pas de hors-texte*): there is no access to reality free of interpretation and context.
- **Law and justice** ("Force of Law"): law can be deconstructed; **justice** cannot—it is the demand that drives deconstruction.
- **Democracy to come** (*à venir*): democracy is always unfinished, a promise never fully realised; this is his answer to Fukuyama's claim that liberal democracy has arrived.
- *Specters of Marx*: against triumphalism after 1989, the "spirit" of Marx's critique remains necessary; he listed the "ten plagues" of the "new world order" (unemployment, exclusion of the homeless and exiles, economic war, foreign debt, the arms trade, nuclear proliferation, ethnic wars, mafia and drug cartels, the limits of international law, among others).
- Hospitality, forgiveness, cosmopolitanism (writings on refugees and Europe).

### Critique
Obscure; relativist ("anything goes"); politically evasive; John Searle's debate with him; critics at Cambridge in 1992 called his work a challenge to reason and scholarship. Supporters say he exposes hidden hierarchies and exclusions.

### Relevance
Postcolonial theory (Gayatri Spivak translated *Of Grammatology*; Edward Said's *Orientalism*, 1978, uses related ideas); feminist critique of gendered binaries; deconstructing national myths and "us vs them" narratives; critique of the "end of history".

## 8. PIERRE BOURDIEU (1930–2002)

### Life and context
- Born in a village in **Béarn**, south-west France, son of a postal worker. Rose through scholarships to the École Normale Supérieure: this "class defector" experience shaped his work on how education reproduces class.
- Military service and fieldwork in **Algeria** during the war of independence. Professor at the Collège de France from 1981. Late in life, a public critic of neoliberalism and globalisation.

### Key works
*Reproduction in Education, Society and Culture* (with Passeron, 1970); *Outline of a Theory of Practice* (1972); **Distinction: A Social Critique of the Judgement of Taste** (1979); *Homo Academicus* (1984); *The State Nobility* (1989); *Language and Symbolic Power* (1991 English edition); *On Television* (1996).

### Core ideas
- **Capital** comes in several forms that can be converted into each other: **economic** (money, property), **cultural** (education, tastes, accent, diplomas), **social** (networks, connections) and **symbolic** (prestige, recognition, honour).
- **Habitus**: the durable dispositions (ways of seeing, speaking, acting) that people acquire from their class background; they feel natural but reproduce social position.
- **Field**: a structured social arena (politics, law, academia, art, journalism) with its own rules and stakes, where actors compete for capital.
- **Symbolic violence**: domination that works because the dominated accept it as legitimate or natural (e.g. regarding a dialect as "incorrect" or upper-class taste as "good taste").
- **Doxa**: what is taken for granted and goes unquestioned.
- **Reproduction**: schools reward the cultural capital of the privileged while appearing meritocratic, so inequality looks like differences in talent.
- **The state**: extending Weber, the state holds a monopoly of legitimate **physical and symbolic** violence: it classifies, certifies (diplomas, ID cards), names and defines official reality.
- The **political field** becomes a closed game of professionals cut off from citizens.

### Critique
Deterministic: habitus leaves little room for change and agency; mainly French data; complex vocabulary.

### Relevance
Inequality in education (elite English-medium schools, public schools and madrassas in Pakistan); elite capture of the civil service and politics (dynastic families' social capital); the CSS exam itself (English fluency as cultural capital); media and political communication; why meritocracy can reproduce privilege.

## 9. FRANCIS FUKUYAMA (born 1952)

### Life and context
- Born in **Chicago** to a Japanese-American family. Studied classics and political philosophy at Cornell (under Allan Bloom), then political science at Harvard. Worked at the RAND Corporation and on the **US State Department's Policy Planning Staff** (1989).
- Wrote as the Soviet bloc collapsed: Eastern Europe's revolutions (1989), the fall of the Berlin Wall (9 November 1989), the end of the USSR (December 1991).
- Associated with neoconservatives at first; broke with them over the Iraq war (*America at the Crossroads*, 2006). Now at Stanford.

### Key works
"**The End of History?**" (*The National Interest*, summer 1989); **The End of History and the Last Man** (1992); *Trust* (1995); *State-Building* (2004); *The Origins of Political Order* (2011); *Political Order and Political Decay* (2014); *Identity* (2018); *Liberalism and Its Discontents* (2022).

### The thesis
- "What we may be witnessing is not just the end of the Cold War… but the **end of history** as such: that is, the end point of mankind's ideological evolution and the universalisation of **Western liberal democracy** as the final form of human government" (1989, paraphrase close to the original).
- **History** here means Hegel's History: the evolution of ideas about how to organise society. Events (wars, crises) continue; but no rival **universal ideology** can now beat liberal democracy. Monarchy, fascism and communism have been defeated.
- Two engines drive history towards liberal democracy:
  1. **Modern natural science and economic modernisation**: technology gives military advantage and wealth, pushing societies towards market capitalism (the "mechanism of desire").
  2. **The struggle for recognition** (Hegel via Alexandre Kojève): humans have *thymos*, a desire for dignity. Liberal democracy best satisfies it by recognising all citizens equally (**isothymia**).
- **The last man** (from Nietzsche): a comfortable, bourgeois person with no great causes; danger of boredom and of **megalothymia** (desire to be recognised as superior) seeking outlets, perhaps through nationalism or extremism.

### Later revisions
- *Political Order* volumes: good order needs three things in balance: a capable **state**, the **rule of law**, and **accountable government** ("getting to Denmark"). States can decay through "**repatrimonialisation**" (capture by elites and families) and vetocracy (US gridlock).
- *Identity* (2018): demands for dignity based on nation, religion, race or sex (identity politics, populism) threaten liberal democracy.
- He has admitted setbacks but argues that no alternative model with **universal appeal** has emerged; China's model depends on its own culture and performance.

### Arguments for (supporting the thesis)
- Number of electoral democracies rose sharply after 1989 ("third wave", Huntington).
- Market economies everywhere, even in China and Vietnam.
- No serious rival ideology with global appeal.
- Popular movements (Arab Spring 2011, Hong Kong 2019) still demand liberal rights.

### Arguments against
| Critic / event | Objection |
|---|---|
| **Samuel Huntington**, *The Clash of Civilizations* (1993 article; 1996 book) | Future conflict will be between civilisations (Western, Islamic, Sinic, etc.), not ideologies; liberal democracy is not universal |
| 9/11 and political Islam | Rival visions of order remain powerful |
| **China** | Authoritarian capitalism delivering growth without democracy |
| Russia's wars (Georgia 2008, Ukraine 2014 and 2022) | Return of great-power conflict ("the return of history", Robert Kagan) |
| **Democratic recession** | Freedom House has recorded a decline in global freedom every year since 2006 [verify count]; populism in the West (Trump, Brexit, Orbán) |
| **Derrida**, *Specters of Marx* (1993) | Triumphalism ignores misery and inequality under the "new world order" |
| Marxists and the left | Inequality and financial crises show liberal capitalism is not stable |
| Islamic thinkers | Liberalism is one civilisation's model, not humanity's end; religion is a permanent source of meaning |

### Balanced conclusion
Fukuyama was right that no rival ideology has universal appeal and that people want dignity and prosperity; wrong to expect smooth convergence. Liberal democracy is now contested from within (populism) and without (China, Russia). The "end of history" is better read as a normative hope than a forecast.

### Relevance to Pakistan
Pakistan's hybrid governance and repeated democratic transitions illustrate both the appeal of democracy (elections never abolished for long) and the persistence of non-liberal forces (military, religion, dynasties): Fukuyama's later framework (state capacity, rule of law, accountability) fits Pakistan's problems well.
