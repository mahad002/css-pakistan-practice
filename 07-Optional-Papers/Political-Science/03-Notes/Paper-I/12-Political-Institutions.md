# 12. Political Institutions and the Role of Government: Legislature, Executive, Judiciary, Political Elites, Civil and Military Bureaucracy

## 1. LEGISLATURE

### 1.1 Functions
1. **Law-making** (ordinary laws, constitutional amendments).
2. **Financial control**: approve budget and taxes ("no taxation without representation"); audit (Public Accounts Committee).
3. **Control of the executive**: questions, debates, motions, committees, votes of no confidence (parliamentary), impeachment (presidential).
4. **Representation** of people and regions.
5. **Deliberation**: national debate on policy.
6. **Electoral**: elects PM (parliamentary); elects President (Pakistan's electoral college: both Houses + provincial assemblies, Art. 41).
7. **Judicial**: impeachment trials (US Senate; Pakistan's joint sitting removes the President, Art. 47).
8. **Constituent**: amends the constitution.
9. **Legitimation** of government decisions.

### 1.2 Unicameral and bicameral
- **Bicameral**: USA (Congress), UK (Commons and Lords), India, **Pakistan** (National Assembly 336; Senate 96), Germany, France.
- **Unicameral**: China (NPC), **Iran** (Majlis; CSS 2025 MCQ), Turkey (Grand National Assembly), Israel, New Zealand, Sweden.

**Arguments for a second chamber**: represents federating units equally (federations—CSS 2024 MCQ: bicameralism suits federal states); revision of hasty legislation; brings expertise; checks the lower house; continuity (Pakistan's Senate is never dissolved; half its members retire every three years).

**Against**: Abbé Sieyès: "If a second chamber dissents from the first, it is mischievous; if it agrees, it is superfluous." Delay and deadlock; undemocratic if unelected (House of Lords); costly.

### 1.3 Decline of legislatures
Lord Bryce noted it a century ago. Causes: growth of executive power and cabinet dominance; strict party discipline; delegated legislation and ordinances; technical complexity; media focus on leaders; international agreements and IMF conditions decided by executives; committee weakness.

### 1.4 Pakistan's Parliament
- **Majlis-e-Shoora** (Art. 50): President + National Assembly + Senate.
- **National Assembly** (Art. 51): 336 seats (266 general by FPTP, 60 women, 10 non-Muslims, by party-list PR). Term five years.
- **Senate** (Art. 59): 96 members (after FATA merger: 23 from each province—14 general, 4 women, 4 technocrats/ulema, 1 non-Muslim—plus 4 from Islamabad) [verify the breakdown]; six-year term; elected by provincial assemblies by single transferable vote.
- **Money bills** originate in and are passed by the NA only (Art. 73); Senate can only recommend.
- **Weaknesses**: low attendance; quorum failures; executive dominance; ordinances; walkouts and boycotts; little committee capacity; floor-crossing restrictions (Art. 63-A) make MNAs follow leaders.

### 1.5 Violation of parliamentary values in Pakistan (2018 note)
Frequent ordinances bypassing Parliament (the PTI government issued dozens in 2018–22 [verify count]); poor attendance; abusive language and fights on the floor; boycotts; bills passed in minutes without debate (e.g. a large package bulldozed through a joint sitting in November 2021; the 26th Amendment passed overnight in October 2024); weak opposition role; delayed Senate elections; misuse of the Speaker's office (the April 2022 Deputy Speaker's ruling dismissing a no-confidence motion, struck down by the Supreme Court); ignoring committee reports. Remedies: parliamentary calendar, empowered committees, limits on ordinances, codes of conduct, televised proceedings, internal party democracy.

## 2. EXECUTIVE

### 2.1 Meaning
The branch that enforces laws and runs government. Narrow sense: head of state, head of government and ministers (political executive). Broad sense: also the civil service, police, armed forces (permanent executive).

### 2.2 Types
| Type | Example |
|---|---|
| Nominal vs real | King/President (nominal) vs PM and cabinet (real) in parliamentary systems |
| Single vs plural | US President (single) vs Swiss Federal Council (seven members, collegial) |
| Parliamentary vs presidential | UK, Pakistan, India vs USA |
| Semi-presidential | France (dual executive) |
| Hereditary vs elected | Monarchs vs presidents |
| Political vs permanent | Ministers vs civil servants |

### 2.3 Functions
Enforcement of laws; administration; foreign relations (treaties, ambassadors); defence (commander-in-chief); legislative (summoning/dissolving parliament, veto, ordinances, initiating most bills); financial (preparing budgets); judicial (pardons, appointing judges); emergency powers.

### 2.4 Growth of executive power
Welfare state; wars and security crises; economic planning; technical complexity; delegated legislation; media-focused leader politics; party discipline. In the UK, "elective dictatorship" (Lord Hailsham, 1976). In the US, the "imperial presidency" (Arthur Schlesinger Jr., 1973). In parliamentary systems the executive (PM and cabinet) is usually the **most powerful institution** (CSS 2026 MCQ).

### 2.5 Pakistan's executive
- **President** (Arts. 41–49): head of state, symbol of unity; acts on the PM's advice (Art. 48), with limited discretion. **58(2)(b)** (presidential power to dissolve the NA, from 1985) was used in 1988, 1990, 1993, 1996; repealed in 1997 (13th Amendment), restored by Musharraf (17th, 2003), repealed again by the **18th Amendment (2010)**.
- **Prime Minister and Cabinet** (Arts. 90–99): real executive; collectively responsible to the NA (Art. 91(6)).
- **Ordinances** (Art. 89): when the Senate or NA is not in session; lapse after **120 days** (about four months; CSS 2025 MCQ) unless laid and extended once by a resolution for another 120 days; cannot be issued on money-bill subjects without restriction [verify].
- **Caretaker governments** before elections (Arts. 224, 224A).
- Current (as of Nov 2026): President **Asif Ali Zardari** (since March 2024); PM **Shehbaz Sharif** (since March 2024).

## 3. JUDICIARY (2018, 2019)

### 3.1 Functions
Adjudicate disputes; interpret law and the constitution; **judicial review** (strike down unconstitutional laws and acts); protect fundamental rights (writs: habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, certiorari, quo warranto); guardian of federalism (settle centre–province disputes); advisory jurisdiction (Pakistan Art. 186); judge-made law through precedent; supervise lower courts.

### 3.2 Judicial review
- Power of courts to declare laws or executive acts void if they violate the constitution (CSS 2024 MCQ: it means to **interpret** the law against the constitution).
- **Marbury v. Madison** (1803, US Chief Justice John Marshall): "It is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is."
- UK: no power to strike down Acts of Parliament (parliamentary sovereignty), only declarations of incompatibility under the Human Rights Act 1998.
- Pakistan: Arts. 184(3) (Supreme Court original jurisdiction on fundamental rights of public importance), 199 (High Court writs); since the 27th Amendment (Nov 2025) the **Federal Constitutional Court** hears constitutional cases [verify which jurisdictions moved].

### 3.3 Independence of the judiciary: conditions
1. **Method of appointment**: merit-based, insulated from politics (judicial commissions).
2. **Security of tenure**: removal only for misconduct through a special process (Pakistan's **Supreme Judicial Council**, Art. 209; US impeachment).
3. **Adequate salaries** that cannot be reduced during tenure.
4. **Separation from executive** (Pakistan's Art. 175(3): separation was required within a set period; achieved in the 1990s after *Sharaf Faridi* [verify]).
5. **Qualifications**: legal expertise.
6. **No post-retirement political jobs** or limits on them.
7. **Immunity** for judicial acts; contempt powers.
8. **Public confidence** and a free legal profession.

### 3.4 Independent judiciary and checks and balances (2018)
Without an independent judiciary: no neutral arbiter between legislature and executive; constitution unenforceable; rights unprotected; federal disputes unresolved; elections unverified. Examples: US Supreme Court's *United States v. Nixon* (1974) forcing release of the Watergate tapes; Pakistan's restoration of Chief Justice Iftikhar Chaudhry (2009) after the Lawyers' Movement; South Africa's Constitutional Court ordering President Zuma to repay funds (2016). Counter-examples where courts legitimised coups: Pakistan's doctrine of necessity (*Dosso* 1958, *Nusrat Bhutto* 1977, *Zafar Ali Shah* 2000). Conclusion: checks and balances cannot exist without judicial independence; but judicial independence also needs judicial restraint.

### 3.5 Judicial activism vs restraint
- **Activism**: courts actively shape policy, use broad interpretation, suo motu powers. Positive: protects rights, fills governance gaps (Indian PIL, Pakistan's missing-persons cases). Negative: encroaches on legislature and executive, lacks expertise and accountability, populism (Pakistan: Chief Justice Saqib Nisar's dam fund and hospital visits, 2018).
- **Restraint**: courts defer to elected branches unless clear violation.
- **Judicial activism as a corollary of poor governance** (Paper II 2023): partly yes—failures of executive invite intervention; but it also reflects judges' ambitions and political alignments.

### 3.6 Pakistan's judiciary: current structure (Nov 2026)
- **26th Amendment** (21 October 2024): Chief Justice of Pakistan chosen by a **Special Parliamentary Committee** from the three most senior judges, for a fixed **three-year** term; restructured **Judicial Commission** with parliamentarians; **constitutional benches** in the Supreme Court and High Courts; limits on suo motu.
- **27th Amendment** (13 November 2025): **Federal Constitutional Court** created for constitutional interpretation and federal–provincial disputes; its decisions bind all courts including the Supreme Court; first Chief Justice **Aminuddin Khan** (sworn in 14 November 2025). Two Supreme Court judges (Mansoor Ali Shah, Athar Minallah) resigned in protest.
- Debate: supporters say it ends judicial overreach; critics say it subordinates the judiciary to the executive and violates the separation of powers.
- Also: Federal Shariat Court (Art. 203-C), High Courts (5), district judiciary, special courts.

## 4. POLITICAL ELITES

### 4.1 Elite theory
- **Vilfredo Pareto** (*The Mind and Society*, 1916): every society is ruled by an elite; **circulation of elites**—"history is a graveyard of aristocracies"; elites are "lions" (use force) or "foxes" (use cunning), borrowing Machiavelli's image.
- **Gaetano Mosca** (*The Ruling Class*, 1896): in all societies two classes appear: a minority that rules and a majority that is ruled; the organised minority dominates the unorganised majority.
- **Robert Michels**: the iron law of oligarchy (1911).
- **C. Wright Mills**, *The Power Elite* (1956): in the US, interlocking military, corporate and political elites rule (the "military-industrial complex", which Eisenhower warned against in 1961).
- **Pluralist reply**: Robert **Dahl**, *Who Governs?* (1961): in New Haven, different elites dominated different issues; power is dispersed and competitive ("polyarchy").
- **Democratic elitism** (Schumpeter): democracy is competition among elites for votes.

### 4.2 Pakistan's elites
- **Hamza Alavi**, "The State in Post-Colonial Societies" (1972): an "**overdeveloped state**" inherited from colonialism, in which the military-bureaucratic oligarchy mediates between three propertied classes (landlords, industrial bourgeoisie, metropolitan capital).
- **Mahbub ul Haq** (1968): "**22 families**" controlled about two-thirds of industrial assets and much banking and insurance [verify figures].
- Today: military, senior bureaucracy, landed families, industrialists and real-estate magnates, dynastic political families, religious elites, media owners. UNDP's *Pakistan National Human Development Report* (2021) estimated elite privilege at about **$17.4 billion** a year (about 6% of GDP) [verify].
- Consequences: elite capture of policy and subsidies, narrow tax base, weak social spending, resistance to land and tax reform.

## 5. CIVIL BUREAUCRACY (2023)

### 5.1 Max Weber's ideal-type bureaucracy (*Economy and Society*, 1922; CSS 2024 MCQ)
1. **Hierarchy** of offices.
2. **Rules**: fixed, written procedures.
3. **Specialisation** and division of labour.
4. **Impersonality**: decisions without favour or emotion ("sine ira et studio").
5. **Merit** recruitment by qualifications and exams; career service with promotion by seniority and merit.
6. **Written records** (files).
7. **Separation** of office from the person: officials do not own their posts.
8. **Salaried**, full-time professionals.
Bureaucracy is the most efficient form of legal-rational authority, but risks becoming an "**iron cage**".

### 5.2 Other views
- **Marx**: bureaucracy serves the ruling class.
- **Fred Riggs**: "prismatic" societies (developing countries) where formal rules coexist with traditional, personal practices.
- **New Public Management** (1980s–90s): run government like business: targets, contracts, privatisation.
- **Red tape**, inertia, empire-building (Parkinson's law), "bureaupathology".

### 5.3 Features of a professional and apolitical bureaucracy (2023)
1. **Merit-based recruitment** through an independent commission (FPSC, Art. 242).
2. **Neutrality**: serves any elected government impartially; no party affiliation (Government Servants (Conduct) Rules bar political activity).
3. **Security of tenure** and protection from arbitrary transfers (the Supreme Court's *Anita Turab* judgment, 2013, required fixed tenures and reasoned transfers).
4. **Expertise** and continuous training (NSPP, NIPA, specialised academies).
5. **Accountability** to law and Parliament, not to individuals.
6. **Anonymity**: ministers take public responsibility.
7. **Integrity**: codes of conduct, asset declarations.
8. **Performance management** and promotion by merit.
9. **Adequate pay** to reduce corruption.
10. **Transparency** and right to information.

### 5.4 Pakistan's civil bureaucracy
- **Colonial legacy**: the Indian Civil Service ("steel frame"); the **Civil Service of Pakistan** (CSP) dominated the state in 1947–71; bureaucrats-turned-rulers (Ghulam Muhammad, Iskander Mirza, Chaudhry Muhammad Ali).
- **Ayub era**: civil–military partnership; 303 officers purged (1959).
- **Bhutto's reforms (1973)**: abolished the CSP and service cadre privileges; unified grades (BPS 1–22); lateral entry; removed constitutional guarantees of service; purged 1,300 officers (1972) [verify count].
- **Zia and Musharraf**: military officers inducted into civil posts; Musharraf's devolution (2001) weakened the district management group.
- **Today**: 12 occupational groups recruited through CSS [verify current number]; politicisation, frequent transfers, "postings for loyalty", corruption, NAB fear, low pay; reform reports (Ishrat Husain's Civil Service Reform, 2018–20).

## 6. MILITARY BUREAUCRACY

### 6.1 Theories of civil–military relations
- **Samuel Huntington**, *The Soldier and the State* (1957): **objective civilian control** (professional, autonomous, apolitical military that obeys civilians) vs **subjective control** (civilians control by politicising the military). Professionalism keeps soldiers out of politics.
- **S.E. Finer**, *The Man on Horseback* (1962): the military intervenes because of its organisational advantages (centralised command, hierarchy, discipline, communication, esprit de corps, weapons). **Levels of intervention**: (1) **influence** (constitutional persuasion), (2) **pressure/blackmail** (threats), (3) **displacement** (replacing one civilian government with another), (4) **supplantment** (direct military rule). It depends on the **political culture**: the lower the culture, the deeper the intervention.
- **Morris Janowitz**: civic-military fusion in new states.
- **Praetorianism** (Huntington; Perlmutter).

### 6.2 Causes of military intervention in developing states
Weak political institutions and parties; corruption and misgovernance; economic crisis; ethnic conflict; security threats; military's corporate interests; foreign support (Cold War); legitimisation by courts and elites.

### 6.3 Pakistan
- **Coups**: 1958 (Ayub), 1977 (Zia), 1999 (Musharraf); Yahya (1969) by transfer; about 33 years of direct military rule.
- **Hybrid regime**: indirect influence over foreign, security and even economic policy (National Security Council, Special Investment Facilitation Council 2023).
- **Economic role**: Fauji Foundation, Army Welfare Trust, DHA, FWO, NLC (Ayesha Siddiqa, *Military Inc.*, 2007).
- **27th Amendment (2025)**: **Chief of Defence Forces** post (held by the COAS); Chairman JCSC abolished from 27 November 2025; Field Marshal **Asim Munir** notified as first CDF on 5 December 2025 for five years; constitutional protection for five-star ranks.
- **Debate**: necessary stabiliser in a hostile region vs obstacle to democratic consolidation. Constitutional safeguards: Art. 6 (high treason), Art. 243–245; oath (Third Schedule) forbids political activity.
