# 13. Forms of Government: Monarchy, Democracy, Dictatorship, Totalitarian and Authoritarian, Unitary, Federal, Confederation, Presidential and Parliamentary

Asked: confederation vs federation 2016, 2026; federal form: conditions and success 2017; unitary vs federal 2020; 18th Amendment and Pakistan's federal system 2019; authoritarianism vs totalitarianism 2026; totalitarianism note 2016; parliamentary system in Pakistan 2021. This is the most reliable topic in Section B: prepare it fully.

**Two different classifications**
- By **who rules** and **how**: monarchy, aristocracy, democracy, dictatorship (Aristotle's tradition).
- By **distribution of power across territory**: unitary, federal, confederal.
- By **executive–legislative relation**: parliamentary, presidential, semi-presidential.

---

## 1. MONARCHY

**Meaning**: rule by one person who usually inherits the office.

| Type | Features | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Absolute** | Monarch holds real power, unlimited by constitution | Saudi Arabia, Brunei, Oman, Eswatini, Vatican (elective) |
| **Constitutional (limited)** | Monarch is a ceremonial head of state; power with elected government | UK, Japan, Spain, Netherlands, Sweden, Thailand |
| **Elective** | Monarch chosen from among rulers | **Malaysia** (Yang di-Pertuan Agong chosen every five years by the Conference of Rulers), Vatican, UAE presidency in practice |
| **Hereditary** | Succession by birth | Most monarchies |

**Merits**: continuity, unity, a neutral head above party, tradition, quick decisions (absolute).
**Demerits**: hereditary principle ignores merit; absolute monarchy risks tyranny; costly; undemocratic.

## 2. DEMOCRACY

### 2.1 Meaning
From Greek *demos* (people) + *kratos* (rule). Abraham Lincoln (Gettysburg, 1863): "government of the people, by the people, for the people". Seeley: "a government in which everyone has a share".

### 2.2 Types
- **Direct**: citizens decide (Athens; Swiss cantons' Landsgemeinde; referendums, initiatives, recall).
- **Indirect / representative**: citizens elect representatives.
- **Liberal democracy**: elections + rights + rule of law + limited government.
- **Social democracy**: democracy + welfare state (Scandinavia).
- **Consociational** (Arend Lijphart): power-sharing among segments in divided societies (Belgium, Switzerland, Lebanon): grand coalition, mutual veto, proportionality, segmental autonomy.
- **Majoritarian vs consensus** democracies (Lijphart, *Patterns of Democracy*, 1999).
- **Deliberative**: decisions through reasoned public discussion (Habermas).
- **Illiberal democracy** (Fareed Zakaria, 1997): elected governments that curb rights.

### 2.3 Theories
- **Classical**: rule by an informed, active citizenry (Rousseau, Mill).
- **Procedural / elitist** (Joseph Schumpeter, *Capitalism, Socialism and Democracy*, 1942): "the democratic method is that institutional arrangement for arriving at political decisions in which individuals acquire the power to decide by means of a competitive struggle for the people's vote".
- **Polyarchy** (Robert Dahl, 1971): two dimensions—**contestation** and **inclusion**; institutions: elected officials, free and fair elections, inclusive suffrage, right to run, freedom of expression, alternative information, associational autonomy.
- **Waves** (Huntington, *The Third Wave*, 1991): first wave (1828–1926), second (1943–62), third (1974–: Portugal, Spain, Latin America, Eastern Europe), each followed by reverse waves.

### 2.4 Conditions for successful democracy
Education and literacy; economic development and a middle class (Lipset); tolerance and democratic culture; strong parties and opposition; independent judiciary and free press; rule of law; civilian control of the military; national unity; decentralisation; leadership committed to democratic norms.

### 2.5 Merits and demerits
**Merits**: legitimacy; accountability; protects rights; peaceful change; political education; equality; better long-term decisions (Amartya Sen: no major famine has occurred in a functioning democracy with a free press).
**Demerits**: rule of the ignorant (Plato), majority tyranny (Tocqueville, Mill), slow decisions, costly elections, money and populism, short-termism, instability in coalitions. Churchill (1947): "democracy is the worst form of government except all those other forms that have been tried from time to time".

### 2.6 Pakistan
EIU Democracy Index places Pakistan among "authoritarian" or "hybrid" regimes in recent years [verify current category]. Problems: military interventions, disputed elections, dynastic parties, weak local government, judicial–executive conflict.

## 3. DICTATORSHIP

**Meaning**: rule by one person or a small group with unlimited power, not accountable to the people, usually seized or kept by force.

**Types**: military (Pinochet in Chile, Zia in Pakistan); personal (Saddam Hussein, Gaddafi); one-party (USSR, North Korea); theocratic; royal dictatorship.

**Features**: concentration of power; no free elections or fake ones; suppression of opposition and press; rule by decree; personality cult; use of force and intelligence services; often claims to rescue the nation from chaos.

**Merits claimed**: quick decisions, order, development (Park Chung-hee in South Korea; Lee Kuan Yew's Singapore as a soft authoritarian model).
**Demerits**: no legitimacy or accountability, succession crises, human rights abuses, corruption, eventual collapse.

## 4. TOTALITARIANISM AND AUTHORITARIANISM (2016, 2026)

### 4.1 Totalitarianism
- A regime that seeks **total control** over every aspect of public and private life: politics, economy, culture, religion, family, thought.
- Term first used positively by Italian fascists (Giovanni Gentile; Mussolini: "everything within the state, nothing outside the state, nothing against the state").
- **Hannah Arendt**, *The Origins of Totalitarianism* (1951): totalitarianism is new; it uses **ideology** and **terror** to dissolve society into isolated, atomised masses; the concentration camp is its laboratory.
- **Carl Friedrich and Zbigniew Brzezinski**, *Totalitarian Dictatorship and Autocracy* (1956): the "**six-point syndrome**":
  1. An elaborate official **ideology** covering all of life and promising a perfect final society.
  2. A **single mass party**, usually led by one man.
  3. A system of **terror** by party and secret police.
  4. A near-complete **monopoly of mass communications**.
  5. A **monopoly of weapons**.
  6. **Central direction of the economy**.
- **Examples**: Nazi Germany (1933–45), Stalin's USSR, Mao's China, North Korea; Khmer Rouge Cambodia.

### 4.2 Authoritarianism
- **Juan Linz** (1964): "political systems with **limited, not responsible, political pluralism**; without elaborate and guiding **ideology** but with distinctive **mentalities**; without intensive or extensive political **mobilisation** except at some points; and in which a leader or occasionally a small group exercises power within formally ill-defined but actually quite predictable limits."
- Demands **obedience** in politics, not total commitment; leaves society, religion, economy partly autonomous.
- **Types**: military regimes (Pakistan under Ayub and Zia, Egypt), personal dictatorships, one-party states, absolute monarchies, theocracies, **competitive authoritarian** (hybrid) regimes (Steven Levitsky and Lucan Way, 2010: elections are real but unfair—Russia, Turkey under some assessments, Venezuela).
- **Examples**: Franco's Spain, Pinochet's Chile, Gulf monarchies, Egypt under Sisi.

### 4.3 Thematic differences (2026 question)

| Theme | Totalitarianism | Authoritarianism |
|---|---|---|
| Scope of control | Total: public and private life, thought | Limited mainly to politics; private life largely left alone |
| Ideology | Elaborate, utopian, mandatory | Vague "mentalities" (order, nation, religion, development) |
| Mobilisation | Constant, mass mobilisation (rallies, youth organisations) | Demobilisation; wants passive, apolitical citizens |
| Pluralism | None; all associations absorbed by party-state | Limited pluralism (Church, army, business, some parties) |
| Party | Single mass party fused with the state | Often no party, or a weak official party; army or bureaucracy may rule |
| Terror | Systematic, mass, arbitrary; aims to remake society | Selective repression of opponents |
| Economy | Centrally directed | Market or mixed, often crony capitalism |
| Leader | Charismatic, cult-like; embodies ideology | Leader or junta within predictable limits |
| Goal | Transform humanity and society | Preserve order and the regime |
| Duration / change | Rare; mostly 20th century | Common; can transition to democracy (Spain, Chile, South Korea) |
| Jeane Kirkpatrick's thesis (1979) | Cannot reform from within | Can evolve towards democracy |

**Similarities**: no accountable government, no free elections, suppression of opposition, limits on freedom, rule by force when needed.

**Today**: digital surveillance gives authoritarian states quasi-totalitarian tools (China's monitoring in Xinjiang), blurring the line.

## 5. UNITARY GOVERNMENT (2020)

**Meaning**: all powers are vested in a single central government; regional or local bodies exist by its will and can be changed or abolished by it.

**Features**: single, supreme central authority; constitution may be flexible or rigid; single legislature (often); local units are administrative agents; single citizenship; uniform laws.

**Examples**: UK (with devolution since 1998 to Scotland, Wales, Northern Ireland, which Parliament could in theory reverse), France, Japan, China, Italy, Sri Lanka, Bangladesh, Iran, Turkey.

**Merits**: uniformity of law and policy; strong, quick decisions; less costly; suits small, homogeneous states; clear responsibility; national unity.
**Demerits**: over-centralisation and red tape; neglect of local needs; risk of despotism; overburdened centre; unsuitable for large, diverse states; weak local participation.

## 6. FEDERAL GOVERNMENT (2017, 2019, 2026)

### 6.1 Definitions
- **K.C. Wheare** (*Federal Government*, 1946): "the method of dividing powers so that the general and regional governments are each, within a sphere, coordinate and independent".
- **A.V. Dicey**: federalism is "a political contrivance intended to reconcile national unity and power with the maintenance of state rights". The units desire **union but not unity**.

### 6.2 Features
1. **Division of powers** between centre and units by the constitution (CSS 2026 MCQ: the most essential feature).
2. **Written constitution**.
3. **Rigid** constitution: amendment needs consent of both levels or special majorities.
4. **Supremacy of the constitution**.
5. **Independent judiciary** to settle disputes (federal court as umpire).
6. **Bicameral legislature** with an upper house representing units (often equally: US Senate, Pakistan's Senate).
7. **Dual government** (two levels each acting directly on citizens); sometimes dual citizenship (USA).
8. Fiscal arrangements (revenue sharing).

### 6.3 Prerequisites / conditions for a successful federation (2017, 2026)
1. **Geographical contiguity** (East and West Pakistan's 1,600 km separation weakened the first federation).
2. **Desire for union, not unity** (Dicey).
3. **Common interests**: defence, economy, foreign policy.
4. **Similarity of social and political institutions**.
5. **Absence of a dominating unit**: no single unit so large that it can overawe the rest (Wheare). (Punjab's population share, about 53% in 2023, is a constant issue in Pakistan.)
6. **Political maturity** and tolerance; spirit of compromise.
7. **Capable leadership**.
8. **Adequate financial resources** for each level, with fair revenue sharing (Pakistan's NFC Award).
9. **Independent judiciary**.
10. **Intergovernmental institutions** (Pakistan's Council of Common Interests, Art. 153–154; National Economic Council, Art. 156).
11. **Democracy**: federalism cannot work under centralised military rule.

### 6.4 Types
- **Coming-together** federations (independent units unite: USA 1787, Switzerland, Australia) vs **holding-together** (a unitary state decentralises to keep unity: India, Belgium, Spain) (Alfred Stepan).
- **Dual federalism** ("layer cake") vs **cooperative federalism** ("marble cake").
- **Symmetric** vs **asymmetric** (special status for some units: Quebec; formerly Jammu and Kashmir in India).
- Centralised (India, Pakistan) vs decentralised (Switzerland, USA).

### 6.5 Merits and demerits
**Merits**: unity with diversity; suits large, plural states; local autonomy; protects minorities; checks central despotism; experimentation ("laboratories of democracy", Justice Brandeis); efficiency in local matters.
**Demerits**: weak centre in crises; costly duplication; conflicts and litigation; inequality between units; secession risk; slow decisions; rigid constitution.

### 6.6 Federation versus unitary (2020)

| Point | Unitary | Federal |
|---|---|---|
| Source of local powers | Granted by the centre; can be withdrawn | Guaranteed by the constitution |
| Constitution | May be flexible, may be unwritten (UK) | Written and rigid |
| Judiciary | Not necessarily a federal umpire | Supreme/federal court as umpire |
| Legislature | Often unicameral | Usually bicameral |
| Citizenship | Single | Single or dual |
| Suitable for | Small, homogeneous states | Large, diverse states |
| Examples | UK, France, China | USA, India, Pakistan, Germany |

## 7. CONFEDERATION (2016, 2026)

### 7.1 Meaning
A union of **sovereign states** that create a central body by **treaty** for limited common purposes (usually defence and foreign affairs), while keeping their sovereignty. The centre acts on states, not directly on citizens.

### 7.2 Examples
- **USA under the Articles of Confederation** (1781–89): Congress could not tax, regulate trade or raise an army directly; replaced by the federal Constitution of 1787.
- **Swiss Confederation** (before 1848; Switzerland kept the name "Confoederatio Helvetica" but became a federation).
- **German Confederation** (1815–66).
- **Confederate States of America** (1861–65).
- **Senegambia** (1982–89).
- **Serbia and Montenegro** (2003–06).
- **Commonwealth of Independent States** (1991) and the **EU** have confederal features (the EU is often called *sui generis*).

### 7.3 Features
Sovereign members; treaty basis; right of **secession**; central body (a diet or congress of delegates) with limited powers; decisions often by **unanimity**; no direct authority over citizens; no independent revenue; weak or no common executive and courts.

### 7.4 Differences between confederation and federation

| Point | Confederation | Federation |
|---|---|---|
| Basis | Treaty among sovereign states | Constitution |
| Sovereignty | Retained by member states | Divided/shared; the federation is sovereign internationally |
| International personality | Members remain separate international persons | Single international person |
| Central authority acts on | Member governments | Citizens directly |
| Secession | Allowed | Not allowed (USA 1861–65; Pakistan has no secession clause) |
| Citizenship | Of member states only | Federal (sometimes also state) citizenship |
| Revenue | Contributions from members | Independent taxing power |
| Decisions | Unanimity | Majority within constitutional rules |
| Durability | Weak, often temporary; tends to break up or become a federation | Stable, permanent |
| Examples | Articles of Confederation, German Confederation | USA, Germany, India, Pakistan |

### 7.5 Why confederations fail
Weak centre, no direct revenue, unanimity paralysis, secession threats, lack of common identity. Many evolve into federations (USA, Switzerland, Germany) or dissolve (Senegambia).

## 8. PRESIDENTIAL AND PARLIAMENTARY SYSTEMS (2021)

### 8.1 Parliamentary system (Westminster model; CSS 2026 MCQ: a type of government)
**Features**:
1. **Dual executive**: nominal head of state (monarch or president) and real head of government (PM).
2. PM and cabinet drawn from and part of the legislature: **fusion of powers** (CSS 2026 MCQ).
3. **Collective responsibility** of the cabinet to the lower house (CSS 2026 MCQ).
4. Government survives only while it has the confidence of the house: **vote of no confidence**.
5. **PM's leadership** ("first among equals" in theory; dominant in practice).
6. Flexible term: house can be dissolved early.
7. Strong party discipline.
8. **Shadow cabinet** and recognised opposition.

**Examples**: UK, India, Pakistan, Japan, Canada, Australia, Germany, Malaysia.

**Merits**: harmony between executive and legislature; responsible government; flexible (bad government can be removed without impeachment); representation of diverse views in cabinet; debate and accountability (question hour).
**Demerits**: instability in coalitions (Italy; Pakistan in the 1950s had seven PMs in 1951–58); cabinet dictatorship with large majorities; weak separation of powers; ministers chosen for loyalty, not expertise.

### 8.2 Presidential system
**Features**:
1. **Single executive**: President is both head of state and government.
2. **Separation of powers**: executive and legislature separately elected; ministers not members of the legislature.
3. **Fixed term**: President cannot be removed by a vote of no confidence (only by impeachment); legislature cannot be dissolved by him.
4. **Checks and balances**: veto, Senate confirmation, judicial review.
5. Cabinet responsible to the President.

**Examples**: USA, Brazil, Mexico, Indonesia, Philippines, **Turkey** (since 2018), **Iran** (president under the Supreme Leader).

**Merits**: stable executive; clear separation; experts in cabinet; quick decisions in crises; democratic mandate for the executive.
**Demerits**: deadlock between President and Congress (government shutdowns); rigidity (no easy removal); risk of authoritarianism; "winner takes all" (Juan Linz, "The Perils of Presidentialism", 1990: presidentialism less stable in divided societies, especially in Latin America).

### 8.3 Semi-presidential system
Maurice Duverger (1980): a directly elected president with considerable powers + a PM and cabinet responsible to the legislature. **France** (Fifth Republic, 1958), Russia, Sri Lanka (partly). **Cohabitation**: president and PM from rival parties (France 1986–88, 1993–95, 1997–2002). See `../Paper-II/03-France.md`.

### 8.4 Comparison table

| Point | Parliamentary | Presidential |
|---|---|---|
| Executive | Dual | Single |
| Relationship | Fusion | Separation |
| Responsibility | Cabinet to legislature | President to people (and constitution) |
| Term | Flexible | Fixed |
| Removal | No-confidence vote | Impeachment |
| Dissolution | Possible | Not possible |
| Ministers | Members of legislature | Not members |
| Stability | Depends on majority | Stable executive; risk of deadlock |
| Examples | UK, India, Pakistan | USA, Brazil, Turkey |

### 8.5 Pakistan's experience (2021: "the adoption of the parliamentary system is the result of past experience")
- **1935 Act** and British tradition → parliamentary form adopted in **1956** and **1973**.
- **1962 Constitution** (Ayub): **presidential**; concentration of power, indirect election via Basic Democrats, alienation of East Pakistan; it collapsed in 1969.
- Military rulers repeatedly tilted towards **presidential power** (Zia's 8th Amendment with 58(2)(b); Musharraf's 17th). Their failure (and the break-up of 1971 under centralised rule) convinced politicians that a parliamentary, federal form suits a plural society: hence the **13th** (1997) and **18th** (2010) Amendments restoring parliamentary supremacy.
- Arguments still made for a presidential system: stability, technocrats in cabinet, direct mandate. Counter-arguments: risk of Punjab-dominated winner-takes-all politics; smaller provinces' fears; history of presidential rule linked with dictatorship.
