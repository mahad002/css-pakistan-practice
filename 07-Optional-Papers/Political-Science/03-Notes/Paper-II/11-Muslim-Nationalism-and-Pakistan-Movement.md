# 11. Political Movements in India (Colonial Period): Rise of Muslim Nationalism and the Pakistan Movement (Sir Syed, Iqbal, Jinnah)

Asked: rise of Muslim nationalism and its implications for Congress 2016; Iqbal and Jinnah's services 2017; Jinnah and constitutional democracy 2018; was Iqbal the author of the idea of Pakistan? 2019; Jinnah's political legalism 2024. MCQs 2025–26: Sir Syed's British Indian Association (1866); Jinnah's constitutional methods; Iqbal vs Sir Syed (Muslim political identity); influences on Iqbal (Islamic philosophy); the 1935 Act (federal structure with princely states).

This topic overlaps heavily with Pakistan Affairs. For the detailed chronology read `../../../06-Compulsory-Papers/5-Pakistan-Affairs/03-Notes-History/` files 01 and 05–08. Here the focus is **political-science analysis**: nationalism, leadership, method and ideology.

---

## 1. What "Muslim nationalism" means

- **Nationalism** (see `../Paper-I/14-Political-Ideologies.md`): the claim that a people forms a nation entitled to self-government.
- **Muslim nationalism in South Asia**: the claim that the Muslims of India were a separate **nation**, not merely a religious minority, because of distinct religion, culture, history, law, language (Urdu), customs and political interests. Its political form was the **Two-Nation Theory**.
- Scholars' interpretations:
  - **Primordial / ideological** (official Pakistani view; I.H. Qureshi, K.K. Aziz): Muslims were always a separate community; the movement expressed this.
  - **Instrumental / elite** (Paul Brass): Muslim elites (landlords, professionals of the UP) mobilised religion to protect their interests.
  - **Constructivist / contingent** (Ayesha Jalal, *The Sole Spokesman*, 1985): Jinnah used the Pakistan demand as a bargaining counter for parity and safeguards within a loose Indian federation; partition was not his first preference.
  - **Faisal Devji** (*Muslim Zion*, 2013): Pakistan as a political idea not tied to soil or blood.
  - Francis Robinson: interaction between Islamic ideas and elite interests.

## 2. Causes of the rise of Muslim nationalism (2016)

1. **Distinct identity**: Islam's separate beliefs, law, social customs; Muslims as former rulers (Mughals) with historical memory.
2. **Decline of Muslim power** after Aurangzeb (1707), the **War of Independence of 1857** and British reprisals that fell heavily on Muslims; loss of jobs, estates and status.
3. **Educational and economic backwardness** compared with Hindus who embraced English education earlier.
4. **Hindu revivalism**: Arya Samaj (1875), cow-protection movements (1880s–90s), Shuddhi and Sangathan (1920s), Bal Gangadhar Tilak's Ganapati and Shivaji festivals; Hindu Mahasabha (1915).
5. **Hindi–Urdu controversy** (from **1867** in Benares): Hindu demands to replace Urdu (Persian script) with Hindi (Devanagari) convinced Sir Syed that the two communities could not remain one.
6. **Representative institutions**: majority rule would mean permanent Hindu dominance (about three-to-one); hence the demand for **separate electorates** and weightage.
7. **Congress's claim to speak for all Indians** and its refusal to accept Muslim safeguards (Nehru Report, 1928).
8. **Partition of Bengal (1905)** and its **annulment (1911)** under Hindu agitation.
9. **Congress ministries (1937–39)**: Bande Mataram, the Wardha education scheme, the Vidya Mandir scheme, refusal of coalitions with the League (UP).
10. **Leadership and organisation**: Sir Syed, the Aga Khan, Iqbal, Jinnah; the **All-India Muslim League** (founded **30 December 1906**, Dhaka).
11. **External influences**: pan-Islamism and the **Khilafat Movement** (1919–24) mobilised Muslim masses.

## 3. Implications of Muslim nationalism for Congress politics (2016)

1. **Challenged Congress's claim** to represent all Indians: the League insisted it alone spoke for Muslims (Jinnah: the League is the "sole representative" of Muslims). Congress relied on "nationalist Muslims" (Maulana Azad, Jamiat Ulema-e-Hind, Khudai Khidmatgars).
2. **Forced compromises**: the **Lucknow Pact** (1916) accepted separate electorates; then Congress reversed in the **Nehru Report** (1928), widening the gulf.
3. **Muslim Mass Contact Campaign** (1937): Congress tried to win Muslims directly, which alarmed the League and strengthened it.
4. **Communal polarisation** of politics: Congress's mass campaigns used Hindu symbols (Gandhi's Ram Rajya language; Bande Mataram), confirming League fears.
5. **Weakened Congress's bargaining position with Britain**: the British used Muslim demands (Cripps 1942 "local option"; Wavell's Simla Conference 1945 collapsed over League's demand to nominate all Muslim members).
6. **Ultimately partition**: Congress leaders (Nehru, Patel) accepted partition in 1947 rather than a weak centre under the Cabinet Mission Plan; Gandhi opposed.
7. **Long-term**: India's secular nationalism defined itself against the Two-Nation Theory; communal politics continued in India (Hindu nationalism's rise).

## 4. SIR SYED AHMAD KHAN (1817–1898)

### 4.1 Life and context
Born in Delhi to a family close to the Mughal court; joined the East India Company's judicial service (1838); saved British lives at Bijnor in **1857**; witnessed the destruction of Delhi's Muslim elite.

### 4.2 Contribution to Muslim nationalism
| Area | Contribution |
|---|---|
| **Reconciliation with the British** | *Asbab-e-Baghawat-e-Hind* (*The Causes of the Indian Revolt*, 1858–59): blamed British policies and the absence of Indians in the legislative council; *Loyal Mohammedans of India*; argued Muslims had not been the main rebels |
| **Education** | Schools at Moradabad (1859) and Ghazipur (1863); **Scientific Society** (1864) translating Western works; ***Tahzib-ul-Akhlaq*** (1870); **Muhammadan Anglo-Oriental College**, Aligarh (classes from 1875; foundation stone 8 January 1877; became Aligarh Muslim University in 1920); **Muhammadan Educational Conference** (1886) |
| **Religious modernism** | Rational interpretation of Islam compatible with science (*naichari* school, criticised by ulama and by Afghani); reply to William Muir's *Life of Mahomet* (*Khutbat-e-Ahmadiya*) |
| **Political awakening** | **British Indian Association** of the North-Western Provinces (**1866**, CSS 2025 MCQ); advised Muslims to stay out of Congress (speeches at Lucknow, December 1887, and Meerut, 1888); **United Patriotic Association** (1888); **Muhammadan Anglo-Oriental Defence Association** (1893) |
| **Two-Nation idea** | After the Hindi–Urdu controversy (1867) he told Shakespeare, a British official, that the two communities would not join wholeheartedly; argued that in a representative system the larger community would override the smaller: an early statement of the Two-Nation idea; he used "nation" (qaum) for Muslims |
| **Legacy** | The **Aligarh Movement** produced the leadership of the Muslim League (Simla Deputation members, Mohsin-ul-Mulk, Viqar-ul-Mulk) and the students who campaigned in 1945–46 |

**Critique**: loyalism to the British; elitism (focus on the ashraf); opposition from ulama (Deoband) and Afghani; at times he spoke of Hindus and Muslims as "two eyes of a beautiful bride", so his "two-nation" view developed over time.

**Iqbal vs Sir Syed** (CSS 2026 MCQ): Sir Syed focused on education and loyalty; Iqbal emphasised **Muslim political identity** and a separate homeland.

## 5. ALLAMA MUHAMMAD IQBAL (1877–1938)

(His political philosophy is in `../Paper-I/07-Muslim-Thought-Modern.md`.)

### 5.1 Political role
- President of the Punjab Muslim League; member of the Punjab Legislative Council (elected 1926).
- **Allahabad Address** (29 December 1930) at the League's annual session: called for a "consolidated North-West Indian Muslim State" (Punjab, NWFP, Sindh, Balochistan) "within the British Empire, or without"; argued Indian Muslims were a distinct cultural unit and that European democracy could not work in India without recognising communities.
- **Second and Third Round Table Conferences** (1931, 1932).
- **Letters to Jinnah** (1936–37, published 1943): urged a separate federation of Muslim provinces; argued that the shari'a and Muslim economic problems needed a free Muslim state; supported Jinnah's leadership; persuaded him that the League must become a mass party.
- Encouraged Jinnah to return from London (he returned in 1934).
- Influenced most by **Islamic philosophy** (CSS 2026 MCQ), as well as Western philosophers (Nietzsche, Bergson) whom he critiqued.

### 5.2 "Iqbal is credited with creating the idea of Pakistan" (2019)

**Arguments for**
1. First to propose, from the **Muslim League's official platform**, a separate Muslim state in the north-west (1930).
2. Gave the **philosophical basis**: Muslims as a nation based on Islam (millat), rejection of territorial nationalism, khudi and revival.
3. His **letters to Jinnah** (1937) prefigured the Lahore Resolution: Jinnah wrote that Iqbal's views "were substantially in consonance with my own" and led him to the conclusions reached in the Lahore Resolution (Jinnah's foreword to the letters, 1943).
4. His poetry created the emotional consciousness of Muslim nationhood.
5. Jinnah called him "friend, guide and philosopher".

**Arguments against / qualifications**
1. **Earlier proposals** existed: Abdul Halim Sharar (1890), Lala Lajpat Rai (1924, partition along communal lines), Sardar Muhammad Gul Khan (1923) [verify], and others.
2. Iqbal's scheme was a state "**within or without**" the British Empire, perhaps a unit within an Indian federation; it excluded **Bengal**.
3. He did not use the name "Pakistan" (coined by **Rahmat Ali**, 1933) and in 1934 denied, in a letter to Edward Thompson, that he proposed the "Pakistan" scheme of Rahmat Ali [verify date].
4. He died in **April 1938**, two years before the Lahore Resolution; Jinnah and the League turned the idea into a mass demand.

**Conclusion**: Iqbal is the **spiritual and intellectual father** of Pakistan ("the poet who dreamed"), who supplied the idea's philosophical justification and its first official political formulation; **Jinnah** gave it organisation, legal shape and realisation.

## 6. QUAID-I-AZAM MUHAMMAD ALI JINNAH (1876–1948)

### 6.1 Phases
| Phase | Years | Position |
|---|---|---|
| **Liberal constitutionalist in Congress** | 1906–1920 | Joined Congress (1906); private secretary to Dadabhai Naoroji; member of the Imperial Legislative Council (1910); joined the League (1913) while staying in Congress; architect of the **Lucknow Pact** (1916); "**ambassador of Hindu–Muslim unity**" (Sarojini Naidu) |
| **Break with Gandhi's methods** | 1920 | Left Congress at the Nagpur session over **non-cooperation** and mass agitation, which he thought unconstitutional and dangerous |
| **Search for safeguards** | 1920s | Delhi Muslim Proposals (1927); opposed the Nehru Report; **Fourteen Points** (1929): federal constitution, provincial autonomy, one-third Muslim representation at the centre, separate electorates, safeguards for religion and culture |
| **Withdrawal** | 1930–34 | Round Table Conferences; disillusioned, practised law in London |
| **Rebuilding the League** | 1934–40 | Returned 1934; League reorganised; 1937 elections; reacted to Congress ministries; mass membership drive; Lucknow session (1937) |
| **Pakistan demand** | 1940–47 | **Lahore Resolution**; title "Quaid-i-Azam"; 1945–46 elections as a plebiscite; Cabinet Mission; 3 June Plan |
| **Governor-General** | 1947–48 | First Governor-General; **11 August 1947** address to the Constituent Assembly (religious freedom, equal citizenship); died 11 September 1948 |

### 6.2 Jinnah's political legalism (2024) and "constitutional democratic process for constitutional democracy" (2018)
**Meaning**: achieving political goals through **law, constitutional negotiation, legislatures and elections**, not mass civil disobedience or violence. (CSS 2026 MCQ: Jinnah believed Muslim interests were best safeguarded through constitutional and legal methods.)

**Evidence**
1. **Opposed non-cooperation and civil disobedience** (1920; 1930; Quit India 1942) as unconstitutional, risking chaos and serving only the majority.
2. **Legislative politics**: decades in the Imperial and Central Legislative Assemblies (e.g. the Child Marriage Restraint Act, Waqf Validating Act 1913 [verify]); used debates to articulate Muslim demands.
3. **Constitutional proposals**: Lucknow Pact, Delhi Proposals, Fourteen Points, response to the Nehru Report, RTC memoranda.
4. **Elections as legitimacy**: the **1945–46 elections** were explicitly fought as a referendum on Pakistan; the League's sweep gave the demand democratic legitimacy.
5. **Negotiation**: Gandhi–Jinnah talks (1944), Simla Conference (1945), Cabinet Mission (1946), 3 June Plan (1947); he accepted the Cabinet Mission Plan (a loose federation with groupings) before withdrawing over Congress's interpretation.
6. **Legal framing of the claim**: self-determination for a nation; minority safeguards; the Lahore Resolution phrased as a constitutional plan ("geographically contiguous units… demarcated into regions").
7. **Never imprisoned**, unlike Gandhi and Nehru; he dressed, argued and acted as a barrister.
8. **Pakistan was created by law**: the **Indian Independence Act 1947**, passed by the British Parliament.
9. **For constitutional democracy**: his vision of Pakistan was a democratic, federal state with equal citizenship (11 August 1947 speech; addresses on democracy and rule of law).

**Exceptions and critiques**
- **Direct Action Day** (16 August 1946) led to the Great Calcutta Killings; Jinnah later said it was meant as peaceful protest, but critics say it broke with legalism.
- Critics say he relied on British goodwill; that the League's mass mobilisation in 1946–47 used religious appeals; Ayesha Jalal argues partition resulted from failed bargaining rather than a plan.
- As Governor-General he used wide executive powers (dismissal of the NWFP ministry, August 1947), seen by some as centralising.

**Conclusion**: Jinnah's method was overwhelmingly constitutional and legal, which gave Pakistan's creation legitimacy in law and international recognition; this legacy contrasts with later military interventions in Pakistan.

### 6.3 Services of Iqbal and Jinnah together (2017)
- Iqbal: ideology, vision, Allahabad, letters, mass consciousness through poetry, persuaded Jinnah.
- Jinnah: organisation of the League into a mass party, negotiation, legal strategy, unity of Muslims across provinces, leadership in 1940–47, founding the state.
- **Partnership**: the thinker and the doer; Iqbal's letters and Jinnah's foreword (1943) show the link.
- Community services: education (both supported Aligarh and Islamia College), economic interests (Iqbal on Muslim poverty; Jinnah's support for Muslim banks and businesses—Habib Bank's move to Karachi, Muslim Commercial Bank 1947, Orient Airways 1946), protection of Urdu and personal law, political representation.

## 7. Two-Nation Theory: summary for answers

- **Statement**: Hindus and Muslims are two separate nations by religion, culture, history, social customs, law and outlook; therefore Muslims are entitled to self-determination in Muslim-majority areas.
- **Exponents**: Sir Syed (early form), Iqbal (philosophical), Jinnah (political): "Hindus and Muslims belong to two different religious philosophies, social customs and literature… They neither intermarry nor interdine" (Lahore, 22 March 1940, paraphrase).
- **Critiques**: 1971 separation of East Pakistan (ethnicity over religion); millions of Muslims remained in India; Maulana Azad's and the Deobandi (Madani) composite nationalism.
- **Defence**: the theory concerned the right of Muslims of the subcontinent to political self-determination, not the claim that religion alone holds a state together; Bangladesh did not rejoin India.
