## 1. Concepts

- **Foreign policy**: the strategies a state uses to pursue its **national interests** in relations with other states and actors (goals + means + process).
- **National interest**: the core aims a state pursues: survival, security, territorial integrity, economic welfare, ideology and prestige. Hans **Morgenthau**: "interest defined in terms of power"; national interest as the "**compass**" of foreign policy. **Core** (vital) interests vs secondary interests; permanent vs variable.
- **Pakistan's stated objectives** (Article 40 of the Constitution and official statements): preserve and strengthen fraternal relations among Muslim countries based on Islamic unity; support the common interests of the peoples of Asia, Africa and Latin America; promote peace and goodwill among nations; peaceful settlement of disputes; plus Kashmir's right to self-determination; economic development; nuclear deterrence.
- **Quaid-i-Azam's principle** (February 1948 broadcast to the US): "friendship towards all and malice towards none".

---

## 2. Pakistan's national interests

1. **Security and territorial integrity** against India's conventional superiority; on the western border against militancy from Afghanistan.
2. **Kashmir**: self-determination for Kashmiris under UN resolutions.
3. **Nuclear deterrence** ("credible minimum", now "full-spectrum").
4. **Economic security**: trade, investment, remittances, debt relief, IMF support, energy.
5. **Ideological and Muslim solidarity**: OIC, Palestine (no recognition of Israel), Muslim causes.
6. **Strategic partnerships**: China (all-weather), the US (security and economy), Saudi Arabia and the Gulf (finance, remittances, defence pact 2025).
7. **Regional connectivity**: CPEC, Central Asia, Afghanistan transit.
8. **Countering terrorism** and isolating hostile proxies.
9. **Image and prestige**: peacekeeping, mediation (2026 Iran war), UN Security Council membership (2025–26), SCO chairmanship (2026–27).

## 4. Key relationships (current, Nov 2026)

### 4.1 India
- **Core issues**: Kashmir (UNSC Res. 47, 1948; Simla Agreement 1972; India's revocation of Article 370 on 5 August 2019); terrorism allegations; water (Indus Waters Treaty, 1960); Siachen, Sir Creek; trade.
- **2025 crisis**: Pahalgam attack (22 April) → India held the IWT "in abeyance" (23 April) and closed the Attari crossing → Pakistan closed airspace and suspended trade → **Operation Sindoor** (7 May) → Pakistan's **Bunyan-um-Marsoos** (10 May) → ceasefire (10 May, announced by Trump; India denies US mediation).
- **2026**: Court of Arbitration award (31 August 2026) held that "abeyance" is impermissible; India rejects it; back-channel and Track-2 contacts (Colombo, Bangkok, Doha); friction at the SCO summit (September 2026).
- **Goals**: deterrence and crisis stability; Kashmir on the international agenda; restore the IWT; resume dialogue on all issues; avoid war; trade only with progress on core issues.

### 4.2 China
- **History**: recognition (1950; Pakistan among the first), boundary agreement (**2 March 1963**), Karakoram Highway (1978), Pakistan's role in Nixon's opening to China (1971), China's support in 1965 and 1971 wars, nuclear and missile cooperation, "**all-weather strategic cooperative partnership**" (2015).
- **CPEC** (launched 2015; about $25–30bn invested [verify]; Phase II from September 2025: industry, agriculture, IT, mining); Gwadar port; ML-1 railway plan ($6.7bn) still awaiting finance.
- **Defence**: about 80% of Pakistan's major arms imports from China (SIPRI) [verify]; J-10C and PL-15 used in May 2025; JF-17; submarines (Hangor class).
- **Diplomacy**: China blocks India's moves at the UN (e.g. listing militants until 2019), supports Pakistan on Kashmir; Pakistan supports China on Taiwan, Xinjiang, Tibet, Hong Kong, South China Sea.
- **75th anniversary of diplomatic relations**: May 2026.
- **Challenges**: attacks on Chinese nationals (Dasu 2021 and March 2024; Karachi airport October 2024); circular debt to Chinese power producers; slow CPEC delivery; US pressure.

**Driving factors (2024)**: (1) **common rival** India (balance of power; China–India border war 1962, Galwan 2020); (2) **strategic geography** (China's access to the Arabian Sea and Middle East; Pakistan's need for a great-power backer); (3) **defence cooperation**; (4) **economic needs** (CPEC, loans, rollovers about $4bn a year [verify]); (5) **diplomatic support** at the UN; (6) **mutual non-interference** and shared positions on sovereignty; (7) **US unreliability** in Pakistan's view (1965 and 1971 arms embargoes; Pressler); (8) **elite consensus** across civil and military leadership; (9) China's Belt and Road ambitions.

**"Reciprocal development"? (2023)**: Pakistan gains infrastructure, energy (over 8,000 MW added under CPEC [verify]), Gwadar, defence; China gains a western route, a strategic partner, market, influence. But asymmetry: Pakistan's debt and dependence; local grievances (Gwadar's fishermen, Balochistan); critics call it an unequal partnership. Balanced: mutually beneficial but asymmetric.

### 4.3 United States
- **Phases**: alignment (1954–65: Mutual Defence Agreement 1954, SEATO, CENTO, US aid; Badaber base); estrangement (1965 embargo; 1971); renewal (1979–89 Afghan jihad; $3.2bn and $4.02bn aid packages); sanctions (Pressler 1990; nuclear tests 1998; coup 1999); **post-9/11 alliance** (major non-NATO ally 2004; Kerry–Lugar–Berman 2009; Coalition Support Funds) amid mistrust (Abbottabad raid, 2 May 2011; Salala attack, November 2011); drift (2017–21: Trump's tweet of January 2018; Afghan withdrawal 2021).
- **2025–26 warming**: Trump thanked Pakistan for arresting an ISKP suspect (March 2025); credited himself with the May 2025 ceasefire (Pakistan nominated him for the Nobel Peace Prize); Field Marshal Munir's White House lunch (June 2025); **tariff deal at 19%** (July 2025) while India faced up to 50%; PM Sharif and Munir with Trump (September 2025); Pakistan voted for the US-drafted Gaza resolution 2803 and joined the "Board of Peace"; minerals and oil exploration talks.
- **Strains**: the 2026 Iran war (anti-US protests in Karachi, 1 March 2026); reported 10% tariff over forced-labour concerns (July 2026) [verify]; Democrats' control of Congress from January 2027 may bring scrutiny of human rights and democracy.
- **Goals**: market access (the US is Pakistan's largest export market), investment, security cooperation (counter-terrorism), balance between the US and China (avoid "bloc" choices), IMF support (US influence there).

### 4.4 Afghanistan
- Historical: Afghanistan opposed Pakistan's UN entry (1947); "Pashtunistan"; Pakistan hosted refugees and the anti-Soviet jihad; recognised the first Taliban government (1996).
- **Since August 2021**: Taliban rule; TTP sanctuaries; Pakistan's hopes for "strategic depth" turned into a "strategic liability".
- **2025–26 war**: heavy fighting in October 2025; **Doha ceasefire (19 October 2025)** mediated by Qatar and Türkiye; Istanbul talks failed; **February 2026 "open war"** (Operation Ghazab lil-Haq): Pakistani strikes on Kabul and Kandahar; at least 358 Afghan civilians killed (UNAMA); Eid ceasefires; border crossings repeatedly closed (Torkham closed again briefly on 1 November 2026).
- **Refugees**: about 2.66 million Afghans returned or were deported since September 2023 (UNHCR–IOM, to 1 August 2026).
- **Goals**: end TTP sanctuaries, secure the border (fencing), trade and transit (UAP railway to Uzbekistan), regional diplomacy (China–Pakistan–Afghanistan trilateral).

### 4.5 Others
- **Saudi Arabia and the Gulf**: **Strategic Mutual Defence Agreement** with Saudi Arabia (17 September 2025); **Makkah Joint Defence Agreement** with Saudi Arabia and Türkiye (signed 7 August 2026, in force 14 November 2026); deposits and oil on deferred payment; about half of remittances.
- **Iran**: 909 km border; gas pipeline stalled by sanctions; January 2024 cross-border strikes; Pakistan as **mediator** in the 2026 US–Iran war.
- **Russia**: improved ties (Imran Khan's visit, 24 February 2022; oil imports 2023); SCO.
- **Türkiye**: defence industry, KAAN, naval corvettes (MILGEM); Makkah pact.
- **Bangladesh**: rapprochement after August 2024; BNP government (2026); direct trade and flights.

## 5. National capacity (2022, 2026)

**Meaning**: a state's total ability to pursue its goals: hard power (military, economy, technology) and soft power (diplomacy, culture, cohesion, governance).

| Indicator | Pakistan (latest) | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| **Economic** | GDP growth **3.7%** (FY26); per capita **$1,901**; public debt **68.3% of GDP**; interest payments over 42% of federal spending (FY27 budget); reserves about $21.5bn (October 2026); exports about $32bn (goods); tax-to-GDP about 10–11%; IMF EFF $7bn | Weakest link: dependence on external financing limits policy independence |
| **Military** | About 650,000 active personnel [verify]; nuclear arsenal about **170 warheads** (SIPRI 2026); defence budget **Rs3.01 trillion** (FY27, +17.7%); combat experience; performance in May 2025 raised its standing | Strongest asset; credible deterrence; but costly for a weak economy |
| **Human development** | HDI **0.544**, rank **168/193** (UNDP 2025); literacy **63%**; 20–26 million children out of school; poverty **28.9%** (FY26); life expectancy 67.8; Global Gender Gap 143/145 | Severe constraint on productivity, innovation and soft power |
| **Demographic** | 241.5 million (2023), growing 2.55%; two-thirds under 30 | Potential "dividend" or burden depending on jobs and skills |
| **Governance and cohesion** | Political instability, civil–military imbalance, terrorism (GTI rank 1), provincial tensions | Undercuts diplomacy and investment |
| **Diplomatic** | UNSC member (2025–26); SCO chair (2026–27); mediator (2026); strong ties with China, Gulf, improving with the US | Above its economic weight |
| **Soft power** | Diaspora, cricket, culture, peacekeeping, climate-vulnerability advocacy | Underused |

**Why capacity constrains an independent foreign policy (2022)**: borrowing makes Pakistan sensitive to lenders' preferences (IMF, US influence, China, Saudi Arabia); energy imports expose it to shocks (Hormuz closure 2026); weak exports limit leverage; internal instability distracts. **Remedies**: economic reform (tax base, exports, energy), human capital (education, health), political stability and consensus, technology and IT exports, regional trade.

## 6. Ideology in foreign policy (2024)

**Role of ideology**
1. **Muslim world**: Pakistan saw itself as a leader of Muslim causes: early pan-Islamic conferences (1949–52), the **second Islamic Summit at Lahore (February 1974)**, OIC activism, support to Arab states in 1967 and 1973, recognition of Bangladesh at the 1974 summit.
2. **Palestine and Israel**: non-recognition of Israel (passport bars travel); support for a Palestinian state on pre-1967 borders with Al-Quds as capital.
3. **Kashmir**: framed as the unfinished business of partition and Muslim self-determination.
4. **Afghanistan**: the 1980s jihad framed in Islamic terms; Taliban links.
5. **Nuclear programme**: Bhutto's "Islamic bomb" rhetoric (though officially national).
6. **Domestic legitimacy**: rulers use Islamic solidarity to gain support (Zia, Bhutto).

**Limits: interest over ideology**
- Alliances with the US and China (atheist communist state) for security.
- Silence on Xinjiang; neutrality in the Iran–Iraq war; refusal to join the Saudi-led war in Yemen (2015).
- Relations with Iran and Saudi Arabia balanced for sectarian and economic reasons.
- Muslim states' indifference on Kashmir (UAE's honours to Modi, 2019).

**Judgement**: ideology defines Pakistan's identity and some red lines (Israel, Palestine, Kashmir); interests (security and economy) decide most choices: "pragmatic idealism".

## 7. Public opinion and the press in foreign policy-making (2025)

**How public opinion shapes policy**
- **Constraints**: anti-US sentiment limited cooperation (drone strikes; parliament's resolution after Salala, 2011, closed NATO supply routes for seven months); public pressure made it impossible to recognise Israel or abandon Kashmir; parliament's April 2015 resolution on neutrality in Yemen reflected opinion; protests after Khamenei's killing (March 2026).
- **Mobilisation**: governments use opinion to strengthen bargaining positions (Kashmir campaigns; 2019 Balakot response; May 2025 "victory" narratives).
- **Electoral politics**: Imran Khan's "absolutely not" (2021) on US bases and the "cipher" controversy (2022) turned foreign policy into domestic politics.
- **Limits**: foreign policy is elite-driven: the military, the Foreign Office and the PM decide; public opinion is often reactive and shaped by state narratives.

**Role of the press**
- **Agenda-setting and framing**: TV talk shows and social media frame India, the US and Afghanistan; nationalist framing during crises.
- **Watchdog**: investigative reports (Dawn Leaks, October 2016; Abbottabad Commission report leak 2013) triggered crises.
- **Channel for state narratives**: ISPR's influence; PEMRA regulation; restrictions under PECA (2016; amended 2025); X blocked (2024–25).
- **Diplomatic signalling**: op-eds by leaders in foreign papers; interviews.
- **Social media**: diaspora and youth campaigns; disinformation (India–Pakistan "information war" in May 2025).

**Judgement**: public opinion and the press set the **boundaries** of acceptable policy and can trigger shifts in crises, but core strategic decisions remain with a narrow civil–military elite; a freer, more informed press and active parliamentary debate would make foreign policy more democratic and durable.

## 8. Foreign policy-making process in Pakistan (2017 note)

| Actor | Role |
|---|---|
| **Prime Minister and Cabinet** | Constitutionally responsible (Art. 90–91); the PM leads diplomacy |
| **Ministry of Foreign Affairs** | Professional diplomats; policy formulation, negotiation, embassies |
| **Military (GHQ, ISI, now the Chief of Defence Forces)** | Dominant on India, Afghanistan, the US, China and nuclear policy; corps commanders' meetings; the CDF since 2025 |
| **National Security Committee** (2013; earlier National Security Council) | PM, ministers and service chiefs coordinate security and foreign policy; National Security Policy 2022–26 (first public version, January 2022) |
| **Parliament** | Foreign affairs committees; resolutions (Yemen 2015; Salala 2011); debates; ratification of some agreements; weak oversight |
| **President** | Ceremonial; receives credentials |
| **Economic ministries and SIFC** | Economic diplomacy (IMF, Gulf investment, China) |
| **Think tanks, media, public opinion, diaspora** | Inputs and pressure |
| **External actors** | Allies and lenders (China, US, Saudi Arabia, IMF) |

**Problems**: civil–military imbalance; personalised decisions; lack of continuity; weak parliamentary role; reactive rather than strategic; economic weakness.

**Reforms**: institutionalise the National Security Committee and a secretariat; parliamentary oversight; economic diplomacy at the centre; public foreign policy documents; long-term strategy across governments.

## 9. How national interest shapes foreign policy: Pakistan as a case study (2026)

**Framework**: national interest (survival, security, economy, ideology, prestige) → objectives → policy instruments (alliances, diplomacy, deterrence, trade) → outcomes.

**Pakistani examples**
1. **Survival and security vs India** → **SEATO/CENTO** (1954–55), China alliance (1963 onward), nuclear deterrence (1998), deterrence in May 2025.
2. **Western border security** → support to Afghan mujahideen (1980s), Taliban (1990s), border fencing and strikes against the TTP (2025–26).
3. **Economic survival** → IMF programmes; Gulf deposits; CPEC; the 2025 US trade deal and minerals cooperation.
4. **Great-power balance** → opening China to the US (1971); balancing between China and the US now; ties with Russia.
5. **Ideology and prestige** → OIC, Palestine, Kashmir campaigns; mediation (Iran war 2026) for prestige and influence.
6. **Energy security** → Iran gas pipeline (stalled), TAPI, Gulf oil on deferred payment, Hormuz contingency plans.

**When interest and ideology clash**: interest usually wins (China over Xinjiang; US alliances); but ideology sets red lines (Israel).

**Conclusion**: Pakistan's foreign policy is best explained by a **realist** reading of national interest (security against India, economic survival), modified by ideology, domestic politics and the international power structure.

## 11. Geostrategic position, peace and economic opportunities (2018)

- **Assets**: corridor between China and the Arabian Sea (CPEC), Central Asia's shortest route to warm waters (Gwadar, Karachi), energy pipelines (TAPI; Iran–Pakistan), proximity to the Gulf and Hormuz, large market.
- **National interest in peace**: economic opportunities require peace with neighbours (Afghanistan, India), internal security, and stable relations with great powers ("geo-economics over geopolitics", National Security Policy 2022).
- **Obstacles**: insecurity in Balochistan and KP, Afghan instability, India hostility, great-power rivalry, governance.
- **Way forward**: connectivity diplomacy (UAP railway; CASA-1000), regional trade, security for corridors, economic reform.
