# 01. Political System of the United States of America

Asked: Senate as the most powerful upper house (vs India and Pakistan) 2016; US President vs UK Prime Minister 2017; Indian vs US President 2018; checks and balances 2019, 2021; party systems of the USA and Germany 2026. **The most-asked country in Paper II.** Facts on current politics are as of **24 Nov 2026**.

---

## 1. Background

- Thirteen British colonies declared independence on **4 July 1776** (Declaration drafted by Thomas Jefferson, inspired by Locke).
- **Articles of Confederation** (1781–89): a weak confederation (no power to tax, no executive, unanimity for amendment). Its failure (Shays' Rebellion, 1786–87) led to the **Philadelphia Convention** (May–September 1787).
- **Federalist Papers** (1787–88) by Hamilton, Madison and Jay defended the new constitution (Madison's Federalist 10 on factions; 51: "ambition must be made to counteract ambition").

---

## 2. The Constitution

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Adopted | Signed **17 September 1787**; ratified 1788; in force **1789** |
| Length | The oldest written national constitution in use; short: a preamble ("We the People…") and **7 articles** |
| Articles | I Congress; II President; III Judiciary; IV States; V Amendment; VI Supremacy; VII Ratification |
| Amendments | **27**. The first ten = **Bill of Rights (1791)**. Key later ones: 13th (abolition of slavery, 1865), 14th (equal protection and due process, 1868), 15th (race no bar to voting, 1870), 16th (income tax, 1913), 17th (direct election of senators, 1913), 19th (women's suffrage, 1920), 22nd (two-term limit for President, 1951), 25th (presidential succession and disability, 1967), 26th (voting age 18, 1971), 27th (congressional pay, 1992) |
| Rigidity | Amendment proposed by **two-thirds of both Houses** (or a convention called by two-thirds of states) and ratified by **three-quarters of states** (38 of 50) |
| Principles | Popular sovereignty; republicanism; **federalism**; **separation of powers**; **checks and balances**; judicial review; limited government; rule of law; individual rights |
| Bill of Rights | Aims to **protect individual liberties** (CSS 2025 MCQ): speech, religion, press, assembly (1st); arms (2nd); searches (4th); due process and self-incrimination (5th); fair trial (6th); cruel punishment (8th); reserved powers to the states or people (10th) |

**Growth beyond the text**: judicial interpretation (*Marbury*, *McCulloch v. Maryland* 1819 on implied powers, the commerce clause), conventions (party system, cabinet), statutes, presidential practice.

## 3. Federalism

- **50 states** with their own constitutions, governors, legislatures and courts. Powers of the federal government are **enumerated** (Art. I, s. 8: tax, borrow, regulate interstate commerce, coin money, declare war, raise armed forces) plus the "**necessary and proper**" clause (implied powers). Others are **reserved** to the states (10th Amendment).
- **Supremacy clause** (Art. VI): federal law prevails over state law.
- Evolution: **dual federalism** (layer cake, to 1930s) → **cooperative federalism** (New Deal, Great Society; marble cake) → "new federalism" (Reagan) → contested federal–state struggles (immigration, abortion after *Dobbs*, marijuana, National Guard deployments).
- Compared with Pakistan (2016 question): US states have residual powers and their own constitutions; Pakistan's provinces had a Concurrent List until 2010; US Senate gives equal state representation, as Pakistan's Senate does for provinces; US fiscal federalism relies on grants-in-aid, Pakistan's on the NFC Award; US federation is "coming-together", Pakistan's is "holding-together" with a stronger centre.

## 4. The Executive: the President

### 4.1 Election and qualifications
- Natural-born citizen, at least **35**, resident for 14 years (Art. II).
- Four-year term; **two-term limit** (22nd Amendment, 1951, after Franklin Roosevelt's four wins).
- Elected indirectly by the **Electoral College**: **538 electors** (each state's House seats + 2 Senators; DC has 3); **270** to win; winner-takes-all in 48 states (Maine and Nebraska split). A candidate can win the presidency while losing the popular vote (2000, 2016). If no majority, the House chooses (each state delegation one vote).
- Primaries and caucuses choose party nominees; national conventions confirm them.

### 4.2 Powers
| Area | Powers | Checks |
|---|---|---|
| Executive | Chief executive; executive orders; enforces laws; runs 15 executive departments and agencies | Courts can strike down orders; Congress controls funding |
| Appointments | Nominates cabinet secretaries, ambassadors, federal judges (about 1,200 posts need Senate confirmation) | **Senate "advice and consent"** (simple majority) |
| Military | **Commander-in-chief** | Only Congress declares war (Art. I); War Powers Resolution (1973): notify Congress within 48 hours, withdraw within 60–90 days without authorisation |
| Foreign policy | Negotiates treaties; recognises states; receives ambassadors; executive agreements | Treaties need **two-thirds of the Senate** (Treaty of Versailles was rejected in 1919–20) |
| Legislative | State of the Union; recommends bills; **veto** | Congress can override by two-thirds of both houses; **pocket veto** if Congress adjourns within 10 days |
| Judicial | Pardons and reprieves (except impeachment) | — |
| Emergency | Wide statutory powers (IEEPA 1977, National Emergencies Act 1976) | Courts and Congress (in February 2026 the Supreme Court struck down tariffs imposed under IEEPA) |

### 4.3 Removal
**Impeachment**: the House impeaches by simple majority for "treason, bribery, or other high crimes and misdemeanors"; the Senate tries, with the Chief Justice presiding for a President; conviction needs **two-thirds**. Impeached presidents: **Andrew Johnson** (1868), **Bill Clinton** (1998), **Donald Trump** (2019 and 2021). **None was removed** (CSS 2025 MCQ). **Richard Nixon** resigned (1974) before impeachment over Watergate.

### 4.4 Vice President and Cabinet
- The **Vice President** succeeds on death, resignation or removal (nine VPs have become President mid-term); presides over the Senate with a casting vote.
- **Cabinet**: secretaries of the 15 departments (State, Treasury, Defense, Justice, etc.), not members of Congress; advisory; responsible to the President alone.
- **Executive Office of the President**: White House staff, National Security Council, Office of Management and Budget.

### 4.5 Growth of presidential power
Wars, depressions, the Cold War, mass media and the "imperial presidency" (Schlesinger, 1973); expanded use of executive orders and emergency powers; "unitary executive" theory; Supreme Court's *Trump v. United States* (July 2024) gave presidents broad immunity for official acts.

## 5. The Legislature: Congress

### 5.1 House of Representatives
- **435 members**, apportioned among states by population after each census (minimum one per state); two-year terms; elected by FPTP in single-member districts (gerrymandering disputes).
- Qualifications: 25 years, citizen for 7 years.
- Presided by the **Speaker** (second in line of succession after the VP).
- Special powers: **revenue bills originate** here; **impeachment**; chooses President if the Electoral College is deadlocked.

### 5.2 Senate
- **100 senators** (two per state, regardless of population); **six-year** terms, one-third elected every two years; directly elected since the **17th Amendment (1913)**.
- Qualifications: 30 years, citizen for 9 years.
- Special powers: **confirm** appointments; **ratify treaties** (two-thirds); **try impeachments**; chooses the VP if the Electoral College is deadlocked.
- **Filibuster**: unlimited debate, ended by **cloture** with 60 votes (abolished for nominations in 2013 and 2017).

### 5.3 Why the US Senate is "the most powerful upper house" (2016)
1. **Co-equal legislative power**: every bill, including budgets, must pass both houses in identical form (only revenue bills must start in the House).
2. **Executive powers**: confirms cabinet, judges and ambassadors; ratifies treaties.
3. **Judicial power**: impeachment trials.
4. **Long, staggered terms** give independence and continuity.
5. **Equal state representation** gives small states disproportionate weight.
6. **Filibuster** gives a minority of 41 a veto on most legislation.
7. **Prestige**: senators are national figures; many presidents came from the Senate.

**Compared with India's Rajya Sabha**: up to 250 members (238 elected indirectly by state assemblies by STV + 12 nominated); permanent body, one-third retire every two years; can only delay **money bills** for **14 days**; deadlock on ordinary bills resolved in a **joint sitting**, where the larger Lok Sabha dominates; cannot remove the government; special powers: Article 249 (allow Parliament to legislate on state subjects) and Article 312 (create all-India services).

**Compared with Pakistan's Senate**: 96 members (23 per province + 4 for Islamabad); indirect election by provincial assemblies by STV; six-year terms, half retire every three years; never dissolved; **money bills**: only recommendations within 14 days (Art. 73); ordinary bills: mediation committee and joint sitting (Art. 70); no role in confirming appointments (except through parliamentary committees on judges under the 26th Amendment); the Chairman acts as President in his absence.

**Conclusion**: the US Senate is the only upper house that is both co-equal in legislation and holds executive and judicial powers; the Rajya Sabha and Pakistan's Senate are revising chambers representing the units.

### 5.4 Committees and legislative process
Standing committees (Appropriations, Ways and Means, Armed Services, Foreign Relations, Judiciary) hold hearings, amend and often kill bills; conference committees reconcile versions. Woodrow Wilson: "Congress in its committee-rooms is Congress at work."

## 6. The Judiciary

- **Supreme Court**: nine justices (number set by statute since 1869); appointed by the President with Senate confirmation; **life tenure** "during good behaviour"; removable only by impeachment.
- **Judicial review** established in **Marbury v. Madison** (1803) by Chief Justice John Marshall.
- Lower courts: 13 Courts of Appeals (circuits), 94 District Courts; parallel state court systems.
- **Landmark cases**: *Dred Scott* (1857); *Plessy v. Ferguson* (1896, "separate but equal"); *Brown v. Board of Education* (1954, school desegregation); *Miranda v. Arizona* (1966); *Roe v. Wade* (1973) overturned by *Dobbs v. Jackson* (2022); *United States v. Nixon* (1974); *Bush v. Gore* (2000); *Citizens United v. FEC* (2010); *Obergefell* (2015, same-sex marriage); *Trump v. United States* (2024, immunity); the IEEPA tariffs ruling (20 February 2026).
- **Current**: Chief Justice **John Roberts** (since 2005); a **6–3 conservative majority** (three justices appointed by Trump in his first term).
- Debates: politicisation of nominations; proposals for term limits; judicial activism vs originalism.

## 7. Checks and Balances (2019, 2021)

*Diagram in words*: a triangle with **Congress**, **President** and **Supreme Court** at the corners; arrows between each pair labelled with the checks below.

| Branch | Checks on the Executive | Checks on the Legislature | Checks on the Judiciary |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Executive** | — | Veto; can call special sessions; VP casts tie-breaking Senate vote | Nominates judges; pardons |
| **Legislature** | Overrides veto (two-thirds); impeachment and removal; Senate confirms appointments and ratifies treaties; power of the purse; investigations and oversight hearings; declares war | Bicameralism (each house checks the other) | Confirms or rejects judges; impeaches judges; sets court size and jurisdiction; proposes amendments to reverse rulings |
| **Judiciary** | Declares executive acts unconstitutional (*Youngstown Steel*, 1952; tariff ruling 2026) | Declares laws unconstitutional | — |

**Examples**:
- Senate rejected the **Treaty of Versailles** (1919–20).
- Congress overrode Nixon's veto of the **War Powers Resolution** (1973).
- *United States v. Nixon* (1974): tapes released; Nixon resigned.
- Senate rejected **Robert Bork**'s Supreme Court nomination (1987).
- Congress used the power of the purse: **government shutdowns** (2013; 2018–19, 35 days; October–November 2025, 43 days, the longest) [verify the 2025 figure].
- Supreme Court struck down tariffs under IEEPA (February 2026).
- Impeachments of Clinton and Trump (acquitted).

**Evaluation**: protects liberty and prevents tyranny (Madison), but causes **gridlock** and shutdowns; "vetocracy" (Fukuyama); depends on party polarisation: under **unified government** checks weaken; under **divided government** they bite (from January 2027 Democrats control both houses against a Republican President).

## 8. Parties and the Party System

### 8.1 Two-party system
- **Democrats** (from Jefferson's Democratic-Republicans; modern form under Andrew Jackson, 1828): centre-left; New Deal coalition; today urban, minority, educated and young voters.
- **Republicans** (founded **1854** against slavery; Lincoln): centre-right; business, rural, evangelical and white working-class voters; transformed by Trump's "Make America Great Again" populism.
- Third parties (Libertarian, Green) rarely win seats.

**Why two parties?** FPTP in single-member districts (**Duverger's law**); winner-takes-all Electoral College; ballot access laws; primaries absorb dissent inside the big parties; historical dualism.

### 8.2 Features
- **Decentralised**: 50 state parties; national committees are weak; candidates build their own campaigns.
- **Primaries**: voters, not party bosses, choose candidates.
- **Weak discipline** compared with UK or Germany (though polarisation has increased party-line voting).
- **Money**: super PACs after *Citizens United*; 2024 federal elections cost over $15 billion [verify].
- **Polarisation**: ideological sorting, partisan media, gerrymandering.
- **Interest groups and lobbies**: AIPAC, NRA, AARP, US Chamber of Commerce, AFL-CIO; about 12,000 registered lobbyists.

## 10. US President vs UK Prime Minister (2017)

| Point | US President | UK Prime Minister |
|---|---|---|
| Position | Head of state **and** government | Head of government only (King is head of state) |
| Mandate | Directly (via Electoral College) for a fixed four-year term | Leader of the majority party in the Commons; no fixed term |
| Removal | Only impeachment (two-thirds Senate) | Vote of no confidence; or by his own party (Thatcher 1990, Johnson 2022, **Starmer 2026**) |
| Cabinet | Chosen freely, outside Congress; advisers only | Drawn from Parliament; collective responsibility |
| Legislature | Separate; cannot dissolve Congress; Congress may be controlled by opponents | Controls the Commons through party majority; can request dissolution |
| Legislation | Can only recommend; veto | Government drives legislation; with a majority, nearly certain to pass ("elective dictatorship") |
| Appointments | Need Senate confirmation | No confirmation needed |
| Foreign/war | Commander-in-chief, but Congress declares war and the Senate ratifies treaties | Royal prerogative exercised by PM; convention of Commons vote on war since 2003 |
| Judiciary | Supreme Court can strike down his acts and laws | No judicial review of Acts of Parliament |

**Verdict**: in formal terms the President has more independent powers; in practice a UK PM **with a solid majority** may achieve more of his programme. Richard Neustadt: "presidential power is the power to persuade." The PM's power is fragile when his party turns on him.

## 11. US vs Indian President (2018)

| Point | USA | India |
|---|---|---|
| Role | Real executive | **Nominal** head of state; real power with PM and Council of Ministers |
| Election | Electoral College based on popular vote | Indirect: electoral college of elected MPs and MLAs, by STV |
| Term | 4 years, two-term limit | 5 years, re-eligible (no limit) |
| Advice | Not bound by cabinet | Bound by ministers' advice (Art. 74, made binding by the 42nd Amendment 1976; can return advice once under the 44th Amendment 1978) |
| Veto | Qualified veto (overridable) | Suspensive veto; **pocket veto** (no time limit for ordinary bills) |
| Ordinances | No | Yes (Art. 123) |
| Emergency | Statutory | National (Art. 352), President's Rule in states (Art. 356), financial (Art. 360) |
| Removal | Impeachment by Congress | Impeachment by Parliament (two-thirds of each house) for violating the Constitution |
