## 1. Outline of Pakistan's federal system (2024)

| Element | Provision |
|---|---|
| Units | Four provinces (Punjab, Sindh, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Balochistan) and the Islamabad Capital Territory (Art. 1); FATA merged into KP in 2018 (25th Amendment). Gilgit-Baltistan and Azad Jammu and Kashmir are administered separately, outside Art. 1's provinces |
| Population shares (Census 2023) | Punjab about 53%; Sindh about 23%; KP about 17%; Balochistan about 6% [rounded] |
| Division of powers | **Federal Legislative List** Part I (exclusive federal: defence, foreign affairs, currency, nuclear, citizenship, etc.) and Part II (subjects run with the CCI: railways, major ports, electricity, national planning, census, inter-provincial matters); everything else (residuary) belongs to provinces (Art. 142). The **Concurrent List was abolished in 2010** |
| Bicameral Parliament | National Assembly by population; **Senate** with equal provincial representation (23 seats each + 4 for Islamabad) |
| Intergovernmental bodies | **Council of Common Interests** (Arts. 153–154): PM (chair), four chief ministers, three federal ministers; decides policy on Part II subjects; must meet at least every 90 days; complaints by provinces against Parliament go to a joint sitting. **National Economic Council** (Art. 156): national plans. **National Finance Commission** (Art. 160): revenue sharing every five years. **Indus River System Authority** (IRSA, 1992 Act) for the 1991 Water Apportionment Accord. Inter-provincial complaints on water (Art. 155) |
| Fiscal federalism | Federal Board of Revenue collects most taxes (income tax, sales tax on goods, customs); provinces collect sales tax on services, agricultural income tax, property-related taxes; the **divisible pool** is shared under the NFC Award |
| Natural resources | Net hydel profits to the producing province (Art. 161); gas royalties and excise to provinces; **oil and gas jointly and equally owned** by the federation and the province (Art. 172(3)) |
| Judiciary | Supreme Court; **Federal Constitutional Court** (2025) decides federation–province disputes; high courts in each province |
| Emergency powers | Arts. 232–234: emergency; **governor's rule** where the constitutional machinery fails (Art. 234 requires parliamentary approval) |
| Provincial governments | Governor (appointed by the President on PM's advice), Chief Minister and cabinet responsible to the provincial assembly |

### Why federalism was chosen (2024)
1. **Diversity**: ethnic, linguistic and regional plurality (Punjabi, Sindhi, Pashtun, Baloch, Saraiki, Mohajir communities).
2. **Historical legacy**: the **1935 Act's** federal scheme and the Pakistan Movement's demand for **provincial autonomy** (Jinnah's Fourteen Points; the Lahore Resolution's "autonomous and sovereign" units).
3. **Size and geography**: large territory, varied regions.
4. **Lesson of 1971**: centralisation and denial of autonomy contributed to the break-up of Pakistan.
5. **Democratic consensus** of 1973 and 2010.

## 2. Historical problems of Pakistani federalism (2016)

1. **Over-centralisation**: strong centre in every constitution; long periods of military rule (unitary in practice).
2. **East–West imbalance** (1947–71): parity, One Unit, Bengali grievances (economic disparity, under-representation in army and civil service), Six Points (1966), separation (1971).
3. **Punjab's dominance**: majority of population, army and bureaucracy, fuelling smaller provinces' resentment.
4. **One Unit (1955–70)**: abolished provincial identities in the West.
5. **Concurrent List** gave the centre overriding power.
6. **Resource disputes**: water (Kalabagh dam, canals), gas royalties (Balochistan's Sui gas), NFC formula based almost only on population until 2009.
7. **Dismissal of provincial governments** (Balochistan 1973; governor's rule).
8. **Ethno-nationalist movements**: Baloch insurgencies (1948, 1958, 1962, 1973–77, 2004–present); Sindhudesh; Pashtunistan (earlier).
9. **Weak intergovernmental institutions**: CCI rarely met before 2010.
10. **Fiscal dependence** of provinces on federal transfers.

**Comparison with the US (2016)**: the US is a "coming-together" federation of states with original powers, residuary powers, their own constitutions, a powerful Senate and an independent Supreme Court as umpire; Pakistan is a "holding-together" federation where the centre created provinces' powers, long dominated by the centre and the military. Since 2010 Pakistan resembles the US more (residuary powers with provinces), but fiscal dependence and administrative capacity remain very different.

## 3. The 18th Amendment (April 2010)

### 3.1 Background
- Passed **unanimously**; signed **19 April 2010** by President Zardari.
- Drafted by a **Parliamentary Committee on Constitutional Reform** of 26 members from all parliamentary parties, chaired by **Senator Raza Rabbani** (nine months, 77 meetings [verify]).
- Inspired by the **Charter of Democracy** (Benazir Bhutto and Nawaz Sharif, London, 14 May 2006).
- Amended about **100 articles** [some sources say 102].

### 3.2 Key changes

**(a) Parliamentary democracy restored**
- **Art. 58(2)(b)** removed; President acts on PM's advice; appoints service chiefs, governors, CEC on advice/consultation.
- Removed the bar on a third term as PM.
- Repealed the 17th Amendment and LFO 2002; removed General Zia's name from the constitution.
- **Art. 6** strengthened: courts cannot validate high treason; suspension "in abeyance" added as treason.

**(b) Provincial autonomy**
- **Concurrent Legislative List abolished** (47 subjects to provinces: education, health, labour, environment, culture, social welfare, tourism, population welfare, etc.).
- About **17 federal ministries devolved** (in phases to June 2011).
- **CCI** strengthened (PM chairs; 90-day meetings; permanent secretariat; more subjects in Federal List Part II).
- **NFC** (Art. 160(3A)): provinces' share cannot be lower than in the previous award.
- **Joint ownership of oil and gas** (Art. 172(3)).
- Provinces may raise **domestic and international loans** (Art. 167(4)), within limits set by the NEC [verify condition].
- Provinces' consent before building **hydroelectric stations** in their territory (Art. 157(2)).
- Provinces can impose **sales tax on services** (Federal List entry 49 amended).
- NWFP renamed **Khyber Pakhtunkhwa**.

**(c) Rights**: **Art. 10A** (fair trial), **19A** (right to information), **25A** (free and compulsory education for ages 5–16).

**(d) Judiciary**: **Art. 175A** Judicial Commission and Parliamentary Committee for judges' appointments (revised by the 19th Amendment, then by the **26th** in 2024); Islamabad High Court.

**(e) Elections**: CEC by consensus of PM and Leader of the Opposition; caretaker governments by consultation (developed in the 20th Amendment).

**(f) Local government**: **Art. 140A** made it mandatory.

**(g) Parties**: Art. 63A (party head decides defection); Art. 17 no longer requires intra-party elections (the Elections Act does).

|---|---|

| Extra | 1% of the divisible pool to KP for the war on terror; Balochistan's share guaranteed even if revenue fell short; gas development surcharge arrears |
| Status | No new award since; the 7th has been extended annually by presidential orders since 2015; the **10th NFC** was constituted and met in 2025 [verify status]; the IMF and federal government want the provinces to bear more expenditure (debt servicing, defence, BISP, higher education) or to raise their own taxes (agricultural income tax laws passed by provinces in 2024–25 under the IMF programme) |

## 5. Political and administrative implications of the 18th Amendment (2016)

**Political**
- Restored parliamentary supremacy; ended presidential dissolution; stronger PM.
- Strengthened the federation's legitimacy among smaller provinces ("a **new social contract**", 2018).
- Built a precedent of **consensus** constitution-making.
- Strengthened provinces as political centres; chief ministers became powerful (e.g. Punjab).
- Weakened the centre's ability to impose uniform policies; some say it weakened national cohesion in education and health.
- Made military rule harder legally (Art. 6), though not impossible in practice.

**Administrative**
- Massive transfer of functions to provinces; provinces had to build capacity overnight.
- Duplication and confusion: the centre re-created ministries under new names (e.g. the Ministry of National Health Services, Regulations and Coordination in 2013; Federal Education and Professional Training; Climate Change; the HEC dispute).
- Provinces wrote their own curricula and education laws; disputes over the **Single National Curriculum** (2020–21).
- Uneven provincial capacity; Balochistan struggled most.
- Data and coordination gaps (e.g. COVID-19 response, 2020, needed the NCOC as a coordination body).
- Fiscal strain at the centre: after the NFC, the federal government keeps about 42.5% of the divisible pool but carries debt service and defence, so it borrows heavily; fiscal deficit pressures.

## 6. "A new social contract" for provincial autonomy (2018)

**Arguments for**
- Unanimous consensus of all parties: a political agreement among the federating units.
- Answered decades-old demands of the smaller provinces (Sindh, KP, Balochistan): the Concurrent List, resource ownership, NFC floor.
- Linked autonomy with democracy (the Charter of Democracy).
- Changed the federal bargain: the centre for national functions, provinces for service delivery.

**Arguments against / limits**
- A contract among elites, not citizens; provinces did not pass powers to **local governments** (the third tier).
- Balochistan's grievances continue (insurgency, missing persons).
- Fiscal side incomplete: no new NFC award since 2009 (only extensions).
- Military and federal bureaucracy still dominant in security and key economic decisions (SIFC, 2023).

## 7. Has the 18th Amendment transformed federalism / resolved devolution? (2019, 2023)

**Transformations achieved**: residuary powers to provinces; fiscal transfers more than doubled in absolute terms; provincial institutions (public service commissions, education departments) expanded; CCI meets more regularly (though still irregularly at times); provinces now negotiate with the centre on water, gas, and federal projects; 25th Amendment (2018) extended provincial governance to ex-FATA.

**Unresolved devolution**
1. **Provinces have not devolved power to districts** ("provincial centralisation"): Article 140A ignored or weakly applied; Punjab had no elected local governments from 2019 until the planned polls in 2027.
2. **Federal re-centralisation**: ministries re-created at the centre; overlap in health, education, environment, food security.
3. **Fiscal imbalance**: provinces depend on federal transfers for about 80–90% of their revenue [verify]; they collect little of their own (agricultural income tax, property tax); the centre runs large deficits.
4. **Capacity gaps** in provinces.
5. **Natural resources**: joint ownership is not fully operational (Balochistan's claims on Reko Diq, gas).
6. **Water disputes**: Sindh–Punjab disputes; the **Cholistan canals** project was halted after protests and a CCI decision in **April 2025** [verify].
7. **Security**: KP and Balochistan face insurgencies; federal security operations; **governor's rule and emergency in KP (11 October 2026)** after the PTI chief minister announced a march on Islamabad.
8. **Census and representation**: disputes over the 2017 and 2023 censuses (Sindh, Karachi undercount claims).
9. **Proposals to roll back**: calls from some federal circles and the IMF to revisit the NFC floor and devolved subjects; reported drafts of a 28th Amendment.

## 8. Will the 18th Amendment end federal tension "once for all"? (2025)

**Suggested position**: **No**. The 18th Amendment was a necessary, historic step that addressed the **legal** structure of federalism, but federal tension in Pakistan comes from **political, fiscal, ethnic and civil–military** factors that no single amendment can settle. Tension is also normal in all federations (US, India, Canada); the goal is to manage it through institutions, not end it.

**Arguments**
1. *It resolved core legal grievances*: Concurrent List, resources, NFC floor, CCI: smaller provinces' main demands for 40 years.
2. *But fiscal federalism is unsettled*: no new NFC award since 2009; federal deficits create pressure to reverse the floor; provinces' weak own revenue.
3. *Third tier missing*: without strong local government, the provinces have become new centres; urban demands (Karachi) remain.
4. *Political culture*: centre and provinces ruled by rival parties clash (PTI's KP vs the federal government; PPP's Sindh); governor's rule in KP (2026) shows the tools of central intervention remain.
5. *Balochistan*: grievances over resources, development (CPEC), security and missing persons persist.
6. *Civil–military imbalance*: key decisions (security, CPEC, SIFC) are centralised outside the federal bargain.
7. *Demands for new provinces* (South Punjab, Hazara, Karachi) challenge the four-province structure.
8. *Implementation*: CCI meetings irregular; capacity weak.

**Way forward**: a fresh NFC award with incentives for provincial tax effort and fiscal responsibility; strengthen CCI with regular meetings and a secretariat; constitutional protection for local government (amend 140A); dialogue with Balochistan; transparent sharing of resource revenues; clear division in health and education standards (national standards, provincial delivery); respect for provincial mandates (no use of governor's rule for political reasons); independent census.
