## 1. Political culture of Pakistan

See `../Paper-I/10-Comparative-Politics.md` §2.5 for the full list. In one paragraph: a **mixed parochial–subject** culture with a growing **participant** element (urban youth, social media); **fragmented** by ethnicity, sect and region; **personality-centred** and **patronage-based** (biradari, electables, dynasties); **low institutional trust**; a **praetorian** tradition that accepts military "rescue"; religion as a source of legitimacy and mobilisation; **confrontational** politics (disputed elections, boycotts, long marches); weak tolerance of dissent.

## 3. Role of the civil bureaucracy

- **Colonial inheritance**: the ICS "steel frame"; the **CSP** cadre dominated policy in 1947–71.
- **Bureaucrats as rulers**: Ghulam Muhammad (Governor-General), Iskander Mirza (President), Chaudhry Muhammad Ali (PM): a "bureaucratic state" before 1958.
- **Partnership with the military**: the "military–bureaucratic oligarchy" (Hamza Alavi; Khalid bin Sayeed) under Ayub.
- **Bhutto's 1973 reforms** broke the CSP's power and politicised appointments (lateral entry).
- **Zia and Musharraf** inducted military officers into civil posts (the 10% quota for ex-officers in civil groups under Zia [verify]).
- **Today**: politicised postings and transfers; "loyal officers" rotated with each government; NAB fears causing decision paralysis; low pay; reforms proposed but slowly implemented.
- See `../Paper-I/12-Political-Institutions.md` §5 for Weber, features of a professional bureaucracy, and reform.

## 4. Role of the military; civil–military relations (2023)

### 4.1 Why the military became dominant
1. **Security environment**: India rivalry from birth (Kashmir 1947–48); Afghanistan's hostility (Durand Line); the army as guarantor of survival.
2. **Institutional strength vs weak civilian institutions**: disciplined, organised, Punjab-based recruitment, compared with a weak Muslim League and fragmented parties.
3. **Cold War alliances** (SEATO 1954, CENTO 1955, US aid) built the army's resources and links with Washington.
4. **Political crises** that invited intervention (1958, 1977, 1999).
5. **Judicial validation** (doctrine of necessity).
6. **Economic interests** (Fauji Foundation, Army Welfare Trust, DHA, FWO, NLC).
7. **Public perception**: the army as the most efficient and trusted institution.

### 4.2 Modes of influence (Finer's levels)
- **Supplantment** (direct rule): 1958–71, 1977–88, 1999–2008.
- **Displacement**: removal of civilian governments through presidential dismissal (1990, 1993, 1996) with military backing.
- **Pressure/blackmail**: Kargil (1999), Memogate (2011), the Dawn Leaks (2016).
- **Influence**: foreign and security policy (India, Afghanistan, US, nuclear), now economic policy through the **Special Investment Facilitation Council** (SIFC, June 2023), and governance of KP and Balochistan.
- **"Hybrid regime"** (2018–22 and after 2024): elected governments with the establishment as a senior partner.

### 4.3 Effects ("the military's political role has undermined civilian institutions and democratic political culture": 2023 statement)
- Interrupted democratic continuity (no PM has finished a five-year term).
- Weakened parties: King's parties (Convention Muslim League, PML-Q, BAP, IPP), engineered alliances (IJI, 1988).
- Depoliticisation and non-party elections (1985).
- Distorted the constitution (8th, 17th Amendments).
- Encouraged reliance on unelected institutions and conspiratorial culture.
- Economic costs (defence spending Rs3.01 trillion in FY27, about 16% of the budget, per budget documents).
- **Counter-view**: the army held the state together in crises (1965, terrorism 2009–17, floods), drove counter-terrorism (Zarb-e-Azb), and its role reflects civilian failures.

### 4.4 2025–26 developments
- **May 2025** India–Pakistan conflict raised the army's prestige; General **Asim Munir** promoted **Field Marshal** (May 2025).
- **27th Amendment** (November 2025): **Chief of Defence Forces** post (COAS also CDF), five-year term notified on 5 December 2025; Chairman JCSC abolished; life immunity for five-star ranks.
- Debate: unified command and stability vs further imbalance.

### 4.5 Way forward
Civilian supremacy through performance and legitimacy (Turkey's lesson); parliamentary oversight of defence (Defence Committees, budget scrutiny); a functioning National Security Committee chaired by the PM; consensus among parties not to invite the military; rule of law and accountability for all; professionalism in line with Huntington's "objective control".

## 5. When does a democracy turn to military dictatorship or totalitarian rule? (2022)

**Conditions** (Linz, Huntington, Levitsky and Ziblatt's *How Democracies Die*, 2018):
1. Political deadlock and polarisation; parties treat rivals as enemies.
2. Economic crisis, inflation, unemployment.
3. Weak institutions (parties, courts, parliament) and corruption.
4. Security threats or civil unrest.
5. An ambitious military or a populist leader who ignores norms (attacks courts, press, elections).
6. Elite and middle-class support for "order".
7. Judicial and constitutional legitimation (necessity, emergency decrees).
8. External backing.

**Case studies**
- **Pakistan 1958**: constitutional instability, seven PMs in seven years, economic problems, provincial tensions, Mirza's and Ayub's ambitions, court validation in *Dosso* (1958).
- **Pakistan 1977**: disputed election, PNA agitation, deadlock, the army's "Operation Fair Play".
- **Pakistan 1999**: PM–army clash after Kargil; dismissal of General Musharraf; the coup.
- **Weimar Germany 1933**: depression, Versailles humiliation, Article 48, Hitler appointed legally then the Enabling Act: democracy to totalitarianism.
- **Chile 1973**: polarisation, economic crisis, Pinochet's coup.
- **Egypt 2013**: polarisation under Morsi, mass protests, Sisi's coup.
- **Myanmar 2021**: army rejected election results.

## 6. Judiciary in Pakistan's politics; judicial activism and governance (2023)

### 6.1 Phases
| Phase | Character |
|---|---|
| 1954–2000 | **Doctrine of necessity**: *Tamizuddin Khan* (1955), *Dosso* (1958) validated coups; *Asma Jilani* (1972) reversed *Dosso* after Yahya; *Nusrat Bhutto* (1977) validated Zia; *Zafar Ali Shah* (2000) validated Musharraf |
| 1990s | Mixed: *Nawaz Sharif* case (1993) restored the dismissed government; *Al-Jehad Trust* (1996) on judges' appointments; 1997 attack on the Supreme Court |
| 2007–09 | **Lawyers' Movement**: Chief Justice **Iftikhar Chaudhry**'s removal (March 2007) and restoration (March 2009); 3 November 2007 emergency declared unconstitutional (2009) |
| 2009–22 | **Judicial activism**: suo motu (Art. 184(3)); PM **Gilani** convicted of contempt (2012); PM **Nawaz Sharif** disqualified (2017, Panama Papers, Art. 62(1)(f)); price controls, dam fund (2018); rulings on missing persons and Karachi; the 2022 Deputy Speaker ruling struck down |
| 2023–24 | Clash with the executive over provincial elections, PTI cases, reserved seats |
| 2024–25 | **26th Amendment** (CJP chosen by a parliamentary committee; constitutional benches) and **27th Amendment** (Federal Constitutional Court); resignations of two senior judges (November 2025) |

### 6.2 Is judicial activism a natural corollary of poor governance? (2023)
**Yes, partly**: when executives and legislatures fail (corruption, missing persons, service delivery, environmental damage), citizens turn to courts; courts filled vacuums (Indian PIL is the same story); public support for the Lawyers' Movement shows demand for judicial remedies.
**But not only**: activism also reflected judges' personal ambitions and populism; alignment with political or establishment interests (critics' view of the 2017 disqualification); courts lacking expertise made costly decisions (Reko Diq 2013, leading to a $6bn ICSID award; Pakistan Steel Mills privatisation blocked 2006); it encroached on the separation of powers and weakened elected institutions.
**Conclusion**: poor governance invites activism, but unrestrained activism further weakens governance; the solution is better governance plus judicial restraint, not the subordination of courts (the critics' concern about the 26th and 27th Amendments).

## 7. Feudalism

- **Meaning in Pakistan**: large landholdings (*jagirs*, *waderas* in Sindh, *sardars* and tribal chiefs in Balochistan, *chaudhries* and *makhdooms* in Punjab) giving owners economic, social and political control over tenants and villages.
- **Origins**: Mughal and British land grants (loyalty rewards after 1857); unionist landlords' alliance with the British; the Muslim League's need for landlords' votes in 1946.
- **Land reforms**: 1959 (Ayub: ceiling 500 acres irrigated / 1,000 unirrigated), 1972 (Bhutto: 150/300 acres), 1977 (100/200); weakly implemented through transfers to relatives; the Federal Shariat Court's **Qazalbash Waqf** judgment (1989) held land reforms un-Islamic [verify]; a 2011 petition revived the issue.
- **Political effects**: landlords dominate rural constituencies ("electables"); patron–client voting; resistance to agricultural income tax (provinces exempted it for decades until 2024–25 IMF-driven laws); weak education and rural development; *jirgas* and private justice.
- **Debate**: some scholars argue "feudalism" is a misnomer: landholding is now more fragmented, and urban, business and military elites are equally powerful (Akbar Zaidi). Yet landed families still form a large share of legislators.

## 8. Dynastic politics

- **Examples**: Bhutto–Zardari (PPP: Zulfikar → Benazir → Asif Zardari / **Bilawal Bhutto Zardari**); Sharif (PML-N: Nawaz → Shehbaz → **Maryam Nawaz**, Punjab CM since 2024; Hamza Shahbaz); Chaudhrys of Gujrat (PML-Q); Wali Khan family (ANP); Maulana Mufti Mahmood → **Fazlur Rehman** (JUI-F); many families in provincial politics.
- **Causes**: weak party organisation and internal democracy; charisma and martyrdom (Bhutto family); patronage networks; voters' identification with families; money; social capital (Bourdieu); military rule's suppression of party institutions; South Asian pattern (Gandhis, Rajapaksas, Sheikh Hasina and Khaleda Zia).
- **Effects**: blocks merit and new leadership; corruption cases intertwined with families; party splits on succession; but also provides continuity and recognition in a weak party system.
- **Ibn Khaldun link**: dynasties decline in the third generation (see Paper I file 06).

## 9. Political parties and interest groups (2017)

Party profiles and weaknesses: `../Paper-I/11-Political-Participation.md` §6.8. Interest groups: §7.5 there.

**"Parties and interest groups have established and demolished the political system": analysis**
- *Established*: the Muslim League created Pakistan; parties wrote the 1973 Constitution and the 18th Amendment; the Charter of Democracy (2006); the Lawyers' Movement (an interest group) restored the judiciary (2009); the business community supported economic reforms; religious groups shaped constitutional identity (Objectives Resolution, Second Amendment).
- *Demolished / destabilised*: party conflicts invited military interventions (1958 chaos; 1977 PNA movement; 1990s rivalry); parties allied with the military against rivals (IJI 1988); PNA agitation (1977); dharnas (2014 PTI–PAT; 2017 TLP Faizabad); traders and landlords resisted taxation; sugar and wheat lobbies influenced policy (2020 sugar inquiry); religious pressure groups blocked reforms (e.g. blasphemy-law reform after the Salmaan Taseer assassination, 2011).
- *Balanced judgement*: parties and groups are both builders and breakers; the deeper problem is weak institutionalisation (Huntington) and the military's role, which keeps parties dependent and confrontational.

## 10. Elections and voting behaviour

### 10.1 Election history (brief)
| Year | Result and note |
|---|---|
| 1970 | First general election on adult franchise; Awami League swept East Pakistan (160 of 162), PPP won the West; power not transferred → 1971 |
| 1977 | PPP won; PNA alleged rigging → agitation → coup |
| 1985 | Non-party elections under Zia |
| 1988, 1990, 1993, 1997 | Alternation of PPP and PML-N/IJI; 1990 election funding scandal (*Asghar Khan* case, 2012 judgment) |
| 2002 | Under Musharraf; PML-Q formed government; MMA won KP |
| 2008 | PPP won after Benazir Bhutto's assassination (27 December 2007) |
| 2013 | PML-N won; first civilian-to-civilian transfer; PTI's rigging claims and 2014 dharna |
| 2018 | PTI won; RTS breakdown; opposition called it "selected" |
| **2024** (8 February) | PTI-backed independents (after losing the "bat" symbol) won the most seats; PML-N–PPP coalition formed; rigging allegations (Form 45 vs Form 47); mobile internet suspended on polling day; reserved seats denied to PTI after legal battles |

### 10.2 Determinants of voting behaviour in Pakistan
1. **Biradari / caste / tribe** (especially rural Punjab): voting as a group.
2. **Patronage and "thana–kachehri" politics**: candidates who can deliver jobs, police and court help, development funds.
3. **Feudal and tribal influence** (Sindh, south Punjab, Balochistan).
4. **Ethnicity and language** (MQM in Karachi; ANP among Pashtuns; Baloch parties).
5. **Religion and sect** (JUI-F, JI, TLP; Barelvi and Deobandi networks; pirs' endorsements).
6. **Party loyalty and leader's charisma** (Bhutto in Sindh; Imran Khan among youth and urban voters; Sharif in central Punjab).
7. **Economic performance** (inflation, load-shedding).
8. **Urban middle class and youth**: issue voting, social media, anti-corruption narratives.
9. **Gender**: women's turnout gap (about 10 percentage points lower in 2018 and 2024) [verify].
10. **Establishment's preferences** (perceived "engineering" affects outcomes and turnout).
11. **Media and social media** (TikTok, YouTube; X blocked February 2024 to May 2025 [verify end date]).

## 11. Religion and politics

- **Pakistan Movement**: Islam as the basis of Muslim nationhood; Jinnah's mix of Islamic ideals and a modern democratic state (debate over the 11 August 1947 speech).
- **Constitutional Islamisation**: Objectives Resolution (1949); Islamic Republic (1956); Second Amendment (1974); **Zia's Islamisation** (Hudood Ordinances 1979, Federal Shariat Court 1980, Zakat and Ushr 1980, blasphemy laws 295-B 1982 and **295-C 1986**, Shariat Ordinance and Act 1988/1991, Islamic studies in curricula); Federal Shariat Court's riba judgment (2022).
- **Religious parties**: JI, JUI-F, JUP, MMA (won KP in 2002), **TLP** (Barelvi; Faizabad sit-in 2017; 2021 protests; banned in 2021 and lifted; banned again in **October 2025** after a march [verify]).
- **Sectarianism**: Sunni–Shia violence (1980s–2010s; Kurram clashes 2024); Deobandi–Barelvi rivalry.
- **Militancy in the name of religion**: TTP; the state's counter-narrative (Paigham-e-Pakistan, 2018).
- **Religion as legitimacy**: rulers from Zia to today invoke Islam; the military's "Islam and Pakistan" ideology.
- **Minorities**: Ahmadis (declared non-Muslim 1974; Ordinance XX of 1984), Christians (Jaranwala attacks 2023), Hindus (forced conversion concerns in Sindh); blasphemy-law misuse; reserved seats (10 in NA).
- **Analysis**: religion unifies (shared identity) and divides (sectarianism, extremism); the challenge is to keep Islamic principles (justice, accountability) while protecting pluralism (Jinnah's vision).

## 12. Ethnicity and national integration (2019, 2021)

### 12.1 Meaning
**National integration**: the process of uniting diverse ethnic, linguistic, religious and regional groups into a cohesive political community with shared loyalty, while respecting diversity.

### 12.2 Ethnic map
Punjabis (about 38–45% depending on how Saraikis are counted), Pashtuns (about 18%), Sindhis (about 14%), Saraikis, Urdu-speaking Mohajirs (about 7%), Baloch (about 3–4%), Hindko, Brahui, Kashmiris, Gilgit-Baltistan's groups [rough shares, verify by Census 2023 language data].

### 12.3 Ethnic movements
- **Bengali nationalism** → 1971.
- **Baloch nationalism**: insurgencies 1948, 1958–59, 1962–69, **1973–77**, and since **2004–06** (killing of Nawab Akbar Bugti, 2006); groups BLA, BLF; grievances over resources (Sui gas), CPEC/Gwadar, missing persons; Baloch Yakjehti Committee protests (Mahrang Baloch arrested 2025 [verify]); major attacks (Jaffar Express hijacking, March 2025 [verify]).
- **Sindhi nationalism**: G.M. Syed's Sindhudesh (1970s); grievances over water, jobs, Karachi.
- **Mohajir politics**: MQM (1984); Karachi's violence (1990s).
- **Pashtun politics**: Pashtunistan (1940s–70s); ANP; Pashtun Tahaffuz Movement (PTM, 2018; banned October 2024).
- **Saraiki and Hazara province demands**.
- **Gilgit-Baltistan** (constitutional status) and **AJK** (Joint Awami Action Committee protests; banned June 2026; elections July–August 2026).

### 12.4 Obstacles to national integration
1. **Centralisation** and long military rule (denial of autonomy).
2. **Punjab's perceived dominance** in army, bureaucracy and resources.
3. **Economic disparities** (Balochistan's poverty; uneven development).
4. **Resource disputes** (water, gas, NFC).
5. **Language disputes** (Urdu vs Bengali; Sindhi language riots 1972).
6. **Weak national parties**; growth of regional and ethnic parties.
7. **Sectarian and religious divisions**.
8. **Undemocratic practices**: rigged elections, dismissals, governor's rule.
9. **Security policies**: military operations, enforced disappearances.
10. **Foreign interference** (alleged Indian and Afghan support to insurgents).
11. **Education and media**: curricula that ignore regional histories; misinformation.
12. **Census disputes** and representation.

### 12.5 Remedies
Genuine provincial autonomy (implement the 18th Amendment); a fair NFC and resource sharing (royalties, jobs for locals in projects like Reko Diq and CPEC); strong local government; democratic continuity and free elections; political dialogue in Balochistan, ending enforced disappearances; equitable development (Aghaz-e-Huqooq-e-Balochistan, 2009; quotas in services); recognition of regional languages and cultures; inclusive curricula; national parties with roots in all provinces; rule of law; accountable security policy; inclusive economic growth and jobs for youth.
