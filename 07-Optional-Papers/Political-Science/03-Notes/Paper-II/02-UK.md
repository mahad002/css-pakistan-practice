# 02. Political System of the United Kingdom

Asked: US President vs UK PM 2017 (see `01-USA.md` §10); local government in a unitary state 2020, 2021 (see `../Paper-I/15-Local-Self-Government.md` §2.1); main characteristics of the UK system and public participation 2024; British vs French judicial systems 2016 (see `03-France.md`). Current facts are as of **24 Nov 2026**.

## 1. The Constitution

### 1.1 Uncodified
The UK has **no single written constitution**. Its constitution is found in several sources:

| Source | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Statutes** | Magna Carta (1215); **Bill of Rights (1689)**; Act of Settlement (1701); Acts of Union (1707 Scotland; 1800 Ireland); **Parliament Acts 1911 and 1949**; European Communities Act 1972 (repealed 2020); **Human Rights Act 1998**; Scotland Act, Government of Wales Act and Northern Ireland Act (1998); House of Lords Act 1999; **Constitutional Reform Act 2005**; EU (Withdrawal) Act 2018; Dissolution and Calling of Parliament Act 2022; **House of Lords (Hereditary Peers) Act 2026** |
| **Conventions** | Unwritten rules followed as binding: the monarch gives assent to bills; the PM is from the Commons and commands its confidence; collective cabinet responsibility; Salisbury convention (the Lords do not block manifesto bills); Sewel convention (Westminster normally does not legislate on devolved matters without consent) |
| **Common law** | Judicial decisions (e.g. *Entick v Carrington*, 1765; *Miller* cases 2017, 2019) |
| **Authoritative works** | A.V. Dicey, *Introduction to the Study of the Law of the Constitution* (1885); Walter Bagehot, *The English Constitution* (1867); Erskine May's *Parliamentary Practice* |
| **Royal prerogative** | Powers exercised by ministers in the Crown's name (treaties, deployment of forces, appointments) |

### 1.2 Features
1. **Uncodified and evolutionary**: grew over centuries ("the British constitution is not made but has grown").
2. **Flexible**: amended by an ordinary Act of Parliament (CSS 2026 MCQ: the UK constitution is known for flexibility).
3. **Parliamentary sovereignty**: "the King-in-Parliament" is the highest legal authority (CSS 2025 MCQ). Dicey: Parliament can make or unmake any law; no person or body can override or set aside its legislation; no Parliament can bind its successors.
4. **Rule of law** (Dicey): supremacy of regular law over arbitrary power; equality before the law; rights protected by ordinary courts.
5. **Constitutional monarchy**.
6. **Unitary state with devolution**.
7. **Parliamentary (cabinet) government**: fusion of executive and legislature.
8. **Bicameralism**: Commons and Lords.
9. **Conventions** play an outsized role.
10. **Independent judiciary** without power to strike down Acts of Parliament.

### 1.3 Limits on parliamentary sovereignty today
EU membership (1973–2020: *Factortame*, 1990, let courts disapply UK law that conflicted with EU law); Human Rights Act 1998 (courts issue "declarations of incompatibility"); devolution (politically hard to reverse); international treaties (ECHR); referendums as political constraints; the Supreme Court's rulings on prerogative (*Miller I*, 2017: Parliament's approval needed to trigger Article 50; *Miller II*, 2019: Boris Johnson's five-week prorogation was unlawful).

## 2. The Monarchy

- **King Charles III** since **8 September 2022** (coronation 6 May 2023). Head of state, head of the Commonwealth, Supreme Governor of the Church of England, commander-in-chief in name.
- **Legal powers vs reality**: royal assent, appointing the PM, dissolution and prorogation, opening Parliament (King's Speech) are exercised on ministerial advice or by convention. Last refusal of royal assent: Queen Anne, 1708.
- **Bagehot**: the monarch has "the right to be consulted, the right to encourage, and the right to warn"; the "dignified" part of the constitution (as opposed to the "efficient" part: the cabinet).
- Role: continuity, national unity, ceremonial, soft power. Monarch is the **head of state**, not of government (CSS 2024 MCQ).
- The UK consists of **England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland** (CSS 2024 MCQ: "Ireland", i.e. the Republic, is not part of it).

## 3. The Executive: Prime Minister and Cabinet

### 3.1 Prime Minister
- Appointed by the monarch as the leader who can command a **majority in the House of Commons** (by convention an MP since 1902; Lord Home renounced his peerage in 1963).
- Powers: forms and reshuffles the cabinet; chairs cabinet; controls the agenda; leads the majority party; advises dissolution; uses prerogative powers (treaties, war, appointments, honours); heads the civil service; national and international spokesperson.
- **"Primus inter pares"** (first among equals) in theory; in practice debates over **prime ministerial government** (Richard Crossman, 1963) and "elective dictatorship" (Lord Hailsham, 1976).
- **Limits**: party revolt (Thatcher 1990; May 2019; Johnson 2022; Truss 2022 after 49 days; **Starmer 2026**); Commons majority; cabinet; media; courts; devolved governments; markets (the 2022 mini-budget crisis).

### 3.2 Cabinet
- About 20–23 senior ministers. **Collective responsibility**: ministers publicly support cabinet decisions or resign (Robin Cook resigned over Iraq, 2003). **Individual ministerial responsibility**: ministers answer to Parliament for their departments.
- Cabinet committees; the Cabinet Office; No. 10 staff.

### 3.3 Civil service
Permanent, **politically neutral**, recruited on merit since the **Northcote–Trevelyan Report (1854)**; anonymous; headed by the Cabinet Secretary. Special advisers (political appointees) are a growing debate.

## 4. The Legislature: Parliament

### 4.1 House of Commons
- **650 MPs**, elected by **first-past-the-post** in single-member constituencies; maximum term five years (Dissolution and Calling of Parliament Act 2022 restored the PM's power to request dissolution).
- **Speaker** (neutral after election).
- Functions: makes law; scrutinises government (Prime Minister's Questions every Wednesday; select committees; debates; votes of no confidence); controls finance (supply and taxation); represents constituents; recruits ministers.
- **Opposition**: "His Majesty's Most Loyal Opposition", with a shadow cabinet; Leader of the Opposition is paid by the state.
- **Select committees** (since 1979) shadow departments; chairs elected by the House (since 2010).

### 4.2 House of Lords
- Unelected; about **800+ members** before the 2026 reform [verify current size]: **life peers** (appointed under the Life Peerages Act 1958, the majority), **26 Lords Spiritual** (bishops of the Church of England), and until 2026, **hereditary peers**.
- The **House of Lords Act 1999** removed most hereditary peers but kept **92**; the **House of Lords (Hereditary Peers) Act 2026** (royal assent March 2026) **removed the remaining hereditary peers**; some were reappointed as life peers in a compromise.
- Powers limited by the **Parliament Acts 1911 and 1949**: can delay ordinary bills for about **one year** and money bills for **one month**; cannot block manifesto commitments (Salisbury convention).
- Role: revising chamber; expertise (crossbenchers); scrutiny of secondary legislation; debate.
- Reform debate: elected or appointed? smaller size? retirement age? Labour's 2024 manifesto promised further reform after hereditary peers.

### 4.3 How a bill becomes law
First reading → second reading (principle) → committee stage → report stage → third reading → the other House → royal assent. Private members' bills (e.g. the Terminally Ill Adults (End of Life) Bill debated in 2024–26) [verify status].

## 5. The Judiciary

- Three legal systems: **England and Wales**, **Scotland**, **Northern Ireland**.
- **Supreme Court of the United Kingdom**: created by the **Constitutional Reform Act 2005**, began work in **October 2009**, replacing the Appellate Committee of the House of Lords; 12 justices; appointed through an independent selection commission; final court of appeal (except Scottish criminal cases).
- The 2005 Act also ended the Lord Chancellor's role as head of the judiciary (transferred to the Lord Chief Justice) and created the Judicial Appointments Commission: a move towards **separation of powers**.
- **No judicial review of Acts of Parliament**, but review of executive action (legality, rationality, procedural fairness) and declarations of incompatibility under the Human Rights Act.
- **Rule of law** and judicial independence protected by convention and the 2005 Act (s. 3: ministers must uphold judicial independence).

## 6. Parties and Elections

### 6.1 Party system
Historically a **two-party system** (Conservatives and Labour since the 1920s; earlier Conservatives and Liberals), now fragmenting into a **multi-party** system in votes while FPTP keeps two big parties in seats.

| Party | Position | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Labour** | Centre-left | Founded 1900; governments 1945–51 (Attlee: NHS 1948), Blair 1997–2007, Starmer 2024–26, **Burnham** 2026– |
| **Conservative** | Centre-right | Oldest; Thatcher 1979–90; in power 2010–24; leader **Kemi Badenoch** (from November 2024) [verify she is still leader] |
| **Liberal Democrats** | Centrist-liberal | 72 seats in 2024 |
| **Reform UK** | Right-wing populist (Nigel Farage) | 14% of votes but 5 seats in 2024; large gains in the May 2025 and May 2026 local elections |
| **SNP** | Scottish nationalist | Governs Scotland since 2007 |
| Greens, Plaid Cymru, DUP, Sinn Féin | — | — |

### 6.2 The 2024 general election (4 July 2024)
Labour won **411 of 650** seats on about **34%** of the vote (a landslide); Conservatives 121 (their worst result); Liberal Democrats 72; SNP 9; Reform UK 5 (14.3% of the vote); turnout about **60%** (lowest since 2001). The most disproportional result in modern British history: an argument for electoral reform.

### 6.3 2026: change of Prime Minister
- **May 2026 local elections**: Labour's worst losses for a governing party in over three decades; Reform UK surged.
- Ministers resigned; **Andy Burnham** (Mayor of Greater Manchester) returned to Parliament by winning the **Makerfield by-election (19 June 2026)**.
- **Keir Starmer** announced his resignation (22 June 2026).
- Burnham was the only candidate; declared **Labour leader on 17 July 2026**; appointed **Prime Minister on 20 July 2026** (the fourth PM of Charles III's reign; seventh in about a decade). Ed Miliband became Foreign Secretary.
- Next general election due by **August 2029**.

This shows a feature of parliamentary government: the PM can change without a general election.

## 7. Devolution

| Body | Created | Powers |
|---|---|---|
| **Scottish Parliament** (Holyrood) | 1999, after the 1997 referendum | Primary legislation on health, education, justice, some taxes; Scottish independence referendum (2014): 55% No |
| **Senedd Cymru** (Welsh Parliament) | 1999 (Assembly); full law-making powers from 2011; renamed 2020 | Health, education, local government |
| **Northern Ireland Assembly** | 1998 **Good Friday (Belfast) Agreement** | Power-sharing between unionists and nationalists; suspended several times (2017–20, 2022–24) |
| English regions | Combined authorities with metro mayors | Transport, housing, skills |

Devolution makes the UK a "**union state**" rather than a classic unitary state; Westminster remains sovereign in law.

## 8. Brexit
- Referendum **23 June 2016**: Leave 51.9%, Remain 48.1%.
- Article 50 triggered March 2017; withdrawal on **31 January 2020**; Trade and Cooperation Agreement from 1 January 2021; Northern Ireland Protocol, replaced by the Windsor Framework (2023).
- Constitutional effects: return of sovereignty to Parliament; strain on the Union (Scotland and Northern Ireland voted Remain); courts' role (*Miller*). See `05-Global-and-Regional-Integration.md` for effects on the EU (2026 question).

## 9. Main characteristics and public participation (2024)

### 9.1 Characteristics (summary for an answer)
Uncodified, flexible constitution; parliamentary sovereignty; rule of law; constitutional monarchy; cabinet government and fusion of powers; PM's leadership; bicameral Parliament with an unelected upper house; two-party dominance under FPTP; neutral civil service; independent judiciary without strike-down power; unitary state with devolution; conventions.

### 9.2 Mechanisms of public participation
| Mechanism | Example |
|---|---|
| Elections | General, devolved, local, mayoral elections; by-elections |
| Referendums | EEC membership (1975), Alternative Vote (2011, rejected), Scottish independence (2014), Brexit (2016) |
| E-petitions | Petitions with **100,000 signatures** are considered for debate in Parliament; the government responds to 10,000 |
| Select committees | Public evidence and consultations |
| Devolution and local government | Closer decisions; metro mayors |
| Consultations and citizens' assemblies | Climate Assembly UK (2020) |
| Civil society and protest | Pressure groups, unions, mass protests (Iraq war 2003; Gaza protests 2023–26) |
| Party membership | Leadership elections (Conservative members chose Truss in 2022) |

### 9.3 Has it been successful?
**Yes**: stable democracy; peaceful transfers; responsive (PM removed when unpopular); devolution; e-petitions; strong civil society; free media.
**But**: FPTP distorts representation (2024: 34% votes → 63% seats); **unelected Lords**; low turnout (60% in 2024); power concentrated in the executive; falling trust; referendums can divide (Brexit); limited local finance; Reform UK voters feel unrepresented in seats.
**Conclusion**: participation is real but filtered through a majoritarian system; electoral reform, an elected second chamber and deeper devolution are the main proposals.
