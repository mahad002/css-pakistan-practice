## 1. Constitution-making, 1947–1956

### 1.1 Interim framework
- **Indian Independence Act 1947** and the **Government of India Act 1935** (adapted) as the interim constitution.
- **Governor-General** with wide powers (Jinnah, then Khawaja Nazimuddin 1948–51, **Ghulam Muhammad** 1951–55, **Iskander Mirza** 1955–56); Section 92-A (1948) let the centre take over provincial governments.
- **First Constituent Assembly**: elected indirectly (1946 provincial elections); about 69 members at first, later 79; acted as both **constitution-maker and federal legislature** (dual role).

### 1.2 Main steps

| Date | Step |
|---|---|
| 12 March 1949 | **Objectives Resolution** passed (moved by Liaquat Ali Khan on 7 March); **Basic Principles Committee** (BPC) set up |
| September 1950 | **BPC interim report**: Urdu as the only national language, equal powers for both houses; strongly opposed in East Bengal; withdrawn |
| 16 October 1951 | **Liaquat Ali Khan assassinated** |
| 22 December 1952 | **BPC final report** (Nazimuddin): parity between East and West in a bicameral legislature; a board of ulama to check laws for Islamic conformity; criticised in Punjab |
| 1952 | **Language Movement**: police firing on students in Dhaka (21 February 1952) |
| April 1953 | Governor-General **Ghulam Muhammad dismissed PM Nazimuddin**, though he had a majority in the Assembly |
| 7 October 1953 | **Bogra Formula** (PM Muhammad Ali Bogra): upper house of 50 (10 for each of five units); lower house of 300 by population (East 165, West 135); parity (175–175) in joint sittings; widely welcomed |
| March 1954 | **United Front** routed the Muslim League in East Bengal elections (League won about 10 of 309 seats) |
| September 1954 | Assembly curtailed the Governor-General's power to dismiss ministers (amendments to the 1935 Act) and repealed PRODA |
| **24 October 1954** | **Ghulam Muhammad dissolved the Constituent Assembly** just as the draft was nearly ready |
| 1955 | ***Maulvi Tamizuddin Khan* case**: the Sindh Chief Court restored the Assembly; the **Federal Court** (Chief Justice Muhammad Munir) reversed on a technicality (Assembly's laws lacked the Governor-General's assent); the Governor-General's Reference (1955) introduced the **doctrine of necessity** |
| July 1955 | **Murree Pact**: parity between the two wings; One Unit; joint or separate electorates to be decided later |
| June–July 1955 | **Second Constituent Assembly** (80 members: 40 each wing) elected by provincial assemblies |
| 14 October 1955 | **One Unit**: West Pakistan's provinces and states merged into a single province |
| 29 February 1956 | Constitution passed; in force **23 March 1956** |

### 1.3 Why constitution-making was delayed (2019) / major issues (2017, 2021)
1. **Loss of leadership**: Jinnah died on 11 September 1948; Liaquat assassinated in 1951; no leader of national stature remained.
2. **East–West representation**: East Bengal had about **55%** of the population; West Pakistan feared domination; solutions oscillated between population-based representation and **parity** (Bogra Formula, Murree Pact).
3. **Language**: Urdu alone vs Urdu and Bengali; the Language Movement (1948–52); settled in 1956 with both as national languages.
4. **Role of Islam**: modernists vs ulama over the Islamic character of the state, the ulama board, the definition of an Islamic state (the Munir Report of 1954 on the anti-Ahmadi riots found the ulama disagreed on the definition of a Muslim).
5. **Federalism and provincial autonomy**: division of powers; One Unit; smaller provinces' fears of Punjab.
6. **Electorates**: joint or separate (decided only in 1957 in favour of joint electorates).
7. **Form of government**: parliamentary vs presidential; powers of the head of state.
8. **Weak Muslim League**: a movement, not a party; few roots in West Pakistan's provinces; collapsed in East Bengal (1954); factionalism.
9. **Bureaucratic–military oligarchy**: Ghulam Muhammad, Iskander Mirza, Chaudhry Muhammad Ali, General Ayub Khan (in the cabinet from 1954); palace intrigues; dismissal of governments and the Assembly.
10. **Dual role of the Assembly**: members enjoyed power as legislators and had no incentive to finish a constitution that would mean fresh elections.
11. **Judicial endorsement** of executive actions (*Tamizuddin Khan*, doctrine of necessity).
12. **Immediate crises**: refugees (about 7–8 million), Kashmir war (1947–48), division of assets, canal water dispute, economic weakness.
13. **Feudal social structure** and lack of democratic culture.

**Contrast with India**: India's Constituent Assembly finished in under three years (1949) with a dominant party (Congress), a settled leadership (Nehru, Patel, Ambedkar), and a less divided geography.

## 2. The three constitutions compared (2017, 2020, 2021)

### 2.1 Summary table

| Point | **1956** | **1962** | **1973** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Framer | Second Constituent Assembly | **Ayub Khan** (Shahabuddin Commission's recommendations largely ignored) | National Assembly (elected 1970) through an all-party committee; constitutional accord of 20 October 1972 |
| Adopted / in force | 29 Feb / **23 March 1956** | 1 March / **8 June 1962** | 10 April / **14 August 1973** |
| Ended | Abrogated **7 October 1958** (Mirza; Ayub took over 27 October) | Abrogated **25 March 1969** (Yahya) | In force; suspended 1977–85 and 1999–2002 |
| Form | **Federal, parliamentary** | **Presidential**; federal in name, highly centralised | **Federal, parliamentary** |
| Head of state | President (Muslim, 40+), elected by national and provincial assemblies; wide discretionary powers | President (Muslim, 35+) elected indirectly by 80,000 **Basic Democrats**; real executive | President (Muslim, 45+) elected by an electoral college of both houses and provincial assemblies; acts on PM's advice (Art. 48) |
| Head of government | Prime Minister responsible to the National Assembly | No Prime Minister | Prime Minister responsible to the National Assembly |
| Legislature | **Unicameral**: National Assembly of 300 (150 each wing: **parity**) + 10 women | **Unicameral**: 156 (75 each wing + 6 women), elected by Basic Democrats | **Bicameral**: National Assembly (by population) + **Senate** (equal provincial representation) |
| Federalism | Three lists (federal 30, provincial 94, concurrent 19 items); two provinces (One Unit) | Two lists (central 49 items; residual to provinces) but centre could legislate on any matter in the national interest; governors appointed by the President | Federal and Concurrent lists until **2010**; now Federal Legislative List only (Parts I and II); CCI, NEC, NFC |
| Name | **Islamic Republic of Pakistan** | Republic of Pakistan; "Islamic" restored by the First Amendment (1963) | Islamic Republic of Pakistan |
| Islam | Directive principles; no law against Quran and Sunnah; Islamic research organisation | Advisory Council of Islamic Ideology; Islamic Research Institute; "repugnancy" left to legislators | State religion (Art. 2); Objectives Resolution operative (Art. 2A, 1985); Council of Islamic Ideology; Art. 227; Federal Shariat Court (1980); definition of Muslim (Second Amendment, 1974) |
| Fundamental rights | Justiciable | **Not justiciable** until the First Amendment (1963) | Justiciable (Arts. 8–28), expanded by the 18th (10A, 19A, 25A) and 26th (9A) |
| Judiciary | Supreme Court; high courts | Supreme Court; high courts; Supreme Judicial Council | Supreme Court; high courts; Federal Shariat Court (1980); Federal Constitutional Court (2025) |
| Languages | **Urdu and Bengali** | Urdu and Bengali | Urdu (English for official purposes until arrangements made) |
| Amendment | Simple procedure with two-thirds of NA and President's assent | Two-thirds of NA and President's assent | Two-thirds of the total membership of **each house** (Art. 239) |
| Elections held under it | **None** | Indirect elections 1962, 1964–65 | Many (1977, 1985 non-party, 1988–2024) |
| Character | Compromise of elites; untested | Imposed by a military ruler | Consensus document of elected representatives |

### 2.2 Critical analysis

**1956 Constitution**
- *Merits*: first indigenous constitution; Islamic and democratic features; federal parliamentary system; both national languages; fundamental rights.
- *Weaknesses*: **parity** denied East Pakistan its majority; **One Unit** angered the smaller West Pakistani provinces; strong **President** (Iskander Mirza dismissed four PMs in two years); delayed elections (none were ever held); weak parties; it survived only 31 months.
- *Unique as* (CSS 2025 MCQ): it first declared Pakistan an **Islamic Republic** with a **federal** parliamentary structure.

**1962 Constitution**
- *Merits*: stability and economic growth for some years; strong executive; Basic Democracies gave a rural base.
- *Weaknesses*: concentration of power in one man; indirect elections excluded the masses and urban middle class; fundamental rights initially non-justiciable; East Pakistan's alienation (Six Points, 1966); collapsed with Ayub's fall (1969). Called "**a constitution of one man, by one man, for one man**" (a common criticism).

**1973 Constitution**
- *Merits*: **consensus** of all parties (including the opposition); parliamentary federalism with a Senate; strong Islamic and rights provisions; bodies for federal coordination (CCI, NEC, NFC); Article 6 against abrogation; survived two military suspensions and returned each time.
- *Weaknesses and distortions*: early amendments under Bhutto curbed courts and rights (3rd–6th); suspension and **8th Amendment** (Zia: 58(2)(b)); **17th** (Musharraf); recurring President–PM and judiciary–executive conflicts; provincial grievances until 2010; the 26th and 27th Amendments (2024–25) seen by critics as weakening judicial independence.
- *Restorations*: **13th (1997)** and **18th (2010)** Amendments restored parliamentary and federal character.

### 2.3 The 1956 Constitution vs the amended 1973 Constitution (2020)
Similarities: Islamic Republic, parliamentary system, fundamental rights, federal structure.
Differences: unicameral vs bicameral; parity between two wings vs representation by population with an equal Senate; two-province federation (One Unit) vs four provinces with extensive autonomy (after the 18th); President's discretion vs ceremonial President (after the 18th); limited Islamic provisions vs extensive (Art. 2A, 227, FSC); 1956 never tested by an election vs 1973 tested repeatedly; the amended 1973 text has new rights (10A, 19A, 25A, 9A), local government (140A), CCI and NFC safeguards, and since 2025 a Federal Constitutional Court and a Chief of Defence Forces.

## 3. Constitutional amendments to date (analysis)

The full list is in the Pakistan Affairs file. For a Political Science answer, group them by theme:

| Theme | Amendments | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Islamic provisions** | 2nd (1974, definition of Muslim), Zia's insertions validated by the 8th (Art. 2A, FSC) | Islamic identity entrenched |
| **Presidential vs parliamentary balance** | 8th (1985) and 17th (2003) **strengthened the President** (58(2)(b)); 13th (1997) and **18th (2010)** **restored parliamentary supremacy** | A pendulum between military-backed presidents and elected PMs |
| **Federalism** | **18th** (Concurrent List abolished; CCI, NFC floor, joint ownership of resources, 140A); **25th** (FATA merger, 2018) | Strongest provincial autonomy in Pakistan's history |
| **Party discipline** | 14th (1997, anti-defection), 18th (party head decides) | Stability vs MPs' independence |
| **Judiciary** | 5th–6th (1976), 18th–19th (2010–11, Judicial Commission), **26th** (2024: Parliamentary Committee chooses CJP; constitutional benches), **27th** (2025: Federal Constitutional Court) | Shifting control of judicial appointments from judges towards Parliament and the executive |
| **Security** | 21st (2015) and 23rd (2017): military courts; **27th**: Chief of Defence Forces, life ranks and immunity | Expanded military role |
| **Electoral** | 20th (2012, caretakers), 22nd (2016, ECP), 24th (2017, delimitation) | Procedural fairness |
| **Rights** | 18th (10A, 19A, 25A), 26th (9A environment) | Expansion of rights |

**Proposed 28th Amendment** (status Nov 2026: **not tabled**): reported drafts on new provinces or administrative units, local government (stronger Art. 140A), NFC changes and education; PPP and smaller provinces opposed changes to provincial shares [all draft details unconfirmed].

## 4. Constitutional development since 1947 and the 18th Amendment (2022)

**Phases**: 1947–56 interim (1935 Act); 1956 parliamentary; 1958–62 martial law; 1962 presidential; 1969–71 martial law and LFO; 1972 interim constitution; 1973 parliamentary; 1977–85 martial law; 1985–99 "8th Amendment" semi-presidentialism; 1999–2002 military rule; 2003–08 17th Amendment; **2010 18th Amendment**; 2024–25 judicial–executive restructuring (26th, 27th).

**Why the 18th Amendment is called revolutionary for provincial autonomy**: abolished the Concurrent List (47 subjects devolved); 17 ministries devolved; provinces' share in the NFC cannot fall; joint ownership of oil and gas; provinces can borrow domestically and abroad (within limits); CCI strengthened; local government made mandatory; consensus (passed unanimously after an all-party committee under Raza Rabbani). See `13-Federalism-and-18th-Amendment.md`.

## 5. Crises of 1947–56 vs the post-2008 era (2025)

| Point | 1947–56 | Post-2008 |
|---|---|---|
| Constitutional basis | No constitution; interim 1935 Act; Assembly dissolved (1954) | Consensual 1973 Constitution restored by the 18th Amendment (2010) |
| Executive instability | Governor-General dismissed PM Nazimuddin (1953); four PMs in 1951–56 (Nazimuddin, Bogra, Chaudhry Muhammad Ali, Suhrawardy from September 1956) | No PM has completed a full term; **Gilani** disqualified (2012, contempt), **Nawaz Sharif** disqualified (2017, Panama case, Art. 62(1)(f)), **Imran Khan** removed by the first successful **no-confidence vote** (April 2022) and later jailed (from 2023) |
| Unelected power | Bureaucrats (Ghulam Muhammad, Mirza) and Army chief (Ayub in cabinet) | "**Hybrid regime**"; establishment's role in government formation (alleged in 2018 and 2024); 27th Amendment's Chief of Defence Forces |
| Judiciary | **Doctrine of necessity** (*Tamizuddin Khan*, 1955) legitimised executive overreach | **Judicial activism** (2007–22): suo motu, PM disqualifications; then **26th and 27th Amendments** curbed the courts; reserved-seats case (2024 ruling for PTI; reversed on review in 2025) |
| Federal tensions | East–West parity; language; One Unit | Centre–province disputes after the 18th (NFC, resources), Balochistan's insurgency, **KP governor's rule (October 2026)**, AJK unrest (2026) |
| Elections | None held nationally; East Bengal 1954 | Regular elections (2008, 2013, 2018, 2024) but all disputed; delayed provincial elections in Punjab and KP (2023) beyond 90 days despite a Supreme Court order |
| Parties | Weak Muslim League; factionalism | Dynastic and personalised parties; PTI's symbol withdrawn (2024) |
| Economy | Refugees, division of assets | Debt crisis; repeated IMF programmes |
| Religion | Objectives Resolution debate; anti-Ahmadi riots and Lahore martial law (1953) | TLP agitations; blasphemy-related violence |

**Structural weaknesses both reveal**
1. **Civil–military imbalance**: unelected institutions shape outcomes.
2. **Weak, personalised political parties** and absence of agreed rules of the game.
3. **Politicised judiciary**: from legitimising coups to removing PMs, and now restructured by the executive.
4. **Centralising tendencies** against federal diversity.
5. **Executive manipulation of constitutional processes** (dissolutions, delayed elections, rushed amendments).
6. **Narrow elite base** and weak accountability.
7. **Economic dependence** that magnifies political crises.

**Difference**: in 1947–56 the problem was the **absence** of a constitution; after 2008 a consensual constitution exists, and the problem is its **erosion and bending**. Progress: the 18th Amendment, three successive elected transfers (2013, 2018, 2024), media and civil society. **Way forward**: a new Charter of Democracy among parties, independent and transparent judicial appointments, electoral reform, respect for timelines (90-day elections), civilian oversight of security policy.
