## 1. GLOBALISATION AND POLITICS

### 1.1 Meaning
- **Anthony Giddens**: "the intensification of worldwide social relations which link distant localities in such a way that local happenings are shaped by events occurring many miles away and vice versa".
- **Dimensions**: economic (trade, finance, production chains), political (international institutions, norms), cultural (media, consumer culture), technological (internet), social (migration), environmental.
- **Drivers**: technology (containers, internet), liberalisation (WTO, Washington Consensus), end of the Cold War, multinational corporations, China's opening (1978) and WTO entry (2001).

### 1.2 Three views (Held and McGrew, *Global Transformations*, 1999)
| School | View |
|---|---|
| **Hyperglobalists** (Kenichi Ohmae, *The End of the Nation State*, 1995) | Markets have made states obsolete |
| **Sceptics** (Hirst and Thompson) | Globalisation is exaggerated; the world was as integrated before 1914; states and regional blocs still dominate |
| **Transformationalists** (Giddens, Held) | Globalisation transforms state power without ending it |

### 1.3 Effects on politics
- **Sovereignty**: states lose control over capital flows, trade policy (WTO rules), monetary policy (markets, IMF conditionality); "**the retreat of the state**" (Susan Strange, 1996).
- **Democracy**: decisions move to unelected bodies and markets ("democratic deficit"); Dani Rodrik's **trilemma**: a country cannot have full economic integration, national sovereignty and democracy at once.
- **Inequality**: growth in Asia lifted hundreds of millions from poverty; but inequality within countries rose; the "elephant curve" (Branko Milanović): Western middle classes lost relatively.
- **Backlash**: populism and nationalism (Brexit, Trump's tariffs 2018 and 2025), anti-globalisation protests (Seattle 1999).
- **New actors**: MNCs, NGOs, global cities, terrorist networks.
- **Deglobalisation / "slowbalisation"** since 2008: trade-to-GDP ratios plateaued; COVID-19 supply-chain shocks; US–China tech war; "friend-shoring"; the 2026 Hormuz closure shock.

### 1.4 Globalisation, domestic politics and state autonomy (Paper I 2019)
Globalisation restricts state autonomy (IMF programmes dictate budgets in Pakistan; capital flight punishes policies), generates domestic conflict (losers vs winners; deindustrialisation; protests against IMF-driven price rises) and inequality (between urban skilled and rural unskilled). But states still choose how to engage: China and South Korea used industrial policy; the 2008 crisis and COVID saw states return as economic actors.

### 1.5 How globalisation promotes regionalisation (2020)
- **Regionalism as adaptation**: states pool sovereignty regionally to compete globally and manage globalisation's risks ("open regionalism").
- Global competition pushes neighbours to create larger markets and supply chains: EU single market (1993), **NAFTA** (1994; replaced by **USMCA**, 2020), **ASEAN** Free Trade Area (1992) and ASEAN Economic Community (2015), Mercosur (1991), **RCEP** (signed 2020, in force 2022: the largest trade bloc, 15 Asia-Pacific states), **AfCFTA** (2018, trading from 2021).
- Stalled global talks (WTO Doha Round) pushed states into regional and bilateral deals.
- Regional value chains (East Asian "factory Asia").
- **Building blocks or stumbling blocks?** (Jagdish Bhagwati: preferential deals create a "spaghetti bowl" of rules and divert trade.)
- South Asia is an exception: low regional integration (SAARC), so globalisation has not produced regionalisation there.

## 2. GLOBAL CIVIL SOCIETY (2017 note)

### 2.1 Meaning
The sphere of non-state, non-market actors operating across borders: international NGOs, social movements, advocacy networks, foundations, faith groups, diaspora networks, media, think tanks (Mary Kaldor, John Keane, Helmut Anheier).

### 2.2 Actors and examples
Amnesty International (1961), Greenpeace (1971), Médecins Sans Frontières (1971), Human Rights Watch, Oxfam, Transparency International, the International Red Cross; World Social Forum (first in Porto Alegre, 2001); Fridays for Future (2018).

### 2.3 Achievements
- **Ottawa Treaty** banning anti-personnel landmines (1997): the International Campaign to Ban Landmines won the 1997 Nobel Peace Prize.
- **Rome Statute** of the International Criminal Court (1998): pushed by an NGO coalition.
- **Jubilee 2000** campaign for debt relief (HIPC initiative).
- **Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons** (2017): ICAN won the 2017 Nobel Peace Prize.
- Climate negotiations (Paris Agreement 2015).
- Issues it pushes: **minority rights** (CSS 2024 MCQ), human rights, environment, gender, labour standards, trade justice.

### 2.4 How it works
Keck and Sikkink, *Activists Beyond Borders* (1998): transnational advocacy networks use information, symbolic, leverage and accountability politics; the "**boomerang pattern**": domestic groups blocked at home go to international allies, who pressure their state from outside.

### 2.5 Critique
Unelected and unaccountable; dominated by Northern NGOs and donors; uneven access; can be co-opted by states; seen in some countries as a tool of foreign influence (Pakistan's 2015 INGO policy, Russia's "foreign agent" law).

## 3. THE EUROPEAN UNION

### 3.1 History of integration

| Year | Step |
|---|---|
| 1950 | **Schuman Declaration** (9 May): pool French and German coal and steel so war becomes "not merely unthinkable, but materially impossible" (plan of Jean Monnet) |
| 1951 | **Treaty of Paris**: **European Coal and Steel Community** (ECSC), six members: France, West Germany, Italy, Belgium, Netherlands, Luxembourg |
| 1957 | **Treaties of Rome**: **European Economic Community** (EEC, a common market) and Euratom (in force 1958) |
| 1968 | Customs union completed |
| 1973 | UK, Ireland, Denmark join |
| 1979 | First direct elections to the European Parliament |
| 1981, 1986 | Greece; Spain and Portugal |
| 1986 | **Single European Act**: single market by 1992; qualified majority voting |
| 1992 | **Maastricht Treaty** (signed 7 February 1992; in force 1 November 1993): created the **European Union** (CSS 2025, 2026 MCQs); three pillars; EU citizenship; plan for the euro |
| 1995 | Austria, Finland, Sweden; **Schengen** area begins |
| 1999 / 2002 | **Euro** introduced (notes and coins 2002) |
| 2004 | Ten new members (mostly ex-communist) |
| 2005 | Constitutional Treaty rejected by French and Dutch referendums |
| 2007 | Bulgaria, Romania |
| 2009 | **Treaty of Lisbon** in force (1 December): legal personality, President of the European Council, High Representative, stronger Parliament, Charter of Fundamental Rights binding, Article 50 exit clause |
| 2013 | Croatia (28th member) |
| 2020 | **UK leaves** (31 January): 27 members |
| 2022 | Ukraine and Moldova become candidates |
| 2026 | Bulgaria adopted the euro on 1 January 2026 (21 euro members) [verify] |

**Successor organisation**: the EU grew out of the EEC/European Communities, which began with the ECSC (CSS 2024 MCQ options list all three; the EU is the successor to the **European Economic Community** / Communities). Its legal base today: the Treaty on European Union (TEU) and Treaty on the Functioning of the EU (TFEU).

### 3.2 Organisational structure (2017)

| Institution | Seat | Composition | Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| **European Council** | Brussels | Heads of state or government + its President + Commission President | Sets overall direction and priorities; decides on treaty change and appointments; President **António Costa** (from December 2024) |
| **Council of the EU** (Council of Ministers) | Brussels/Luxembourg | One minister per state, by subject | Co-legislator; adopts budget with Parliament; rotating six-month presidency (Cyprus Jan–Jun 2026; Ireland Jul–Dec 2026); decisions by **qualified majority** (55% of states with 65% of population) or unanimity (foreign policy, tax, enlargement) |
| **European Commission** | Brussels | 27 Commissioners (one per state); President **Ursula von der Leyen** (second term from December 2024) | **Executive** (CSS 2024, 2026 MCQs): proposes legislation (sole right of initiative), implements policies and budget, enforces law ("guardian of the treaties"), negotiates trade deals |
| **European Parliament** | Strasbourg/Brussels | **720 MEPs** directly elected every five years (last June 2024); President Roberta Metsola | Co-legislator (ordinary legislative procedure); budget; approves and can censure the Commission |
| **Court of Justice of the EU** | Luxembourg | One judge per state + General Court | Interprets EU law; supremacy (*Costa v ENEL*, 1964) and direct effect (*Van Gend en Loos*, 1963) |
| **European Central Bank** | Frankfurt | President Christine Lagarde | Monetary policy for the euro area |
| Court of Auditors; European Investment Bank; agencies | — | — | — |

**High Representative for Foreign Affairs and Security Policy**: Kaja Kallas (from December 2024).

### 3.3 Basic norms of regional politico-economic integration (2017, 2022)
1. **Peace through interdependence** (functional integration).
2. **Pooled sovereignty** and supranational institutions.
3. **Four freedoms**: movement of goods, services, capital and people.
4. **Rule of law** and supremacy of EU law.
5. **Democracy and human rights** (Copenhagen criteria, 1993, for membership).
6. **Subsidiarity** and proportionality (act at the lowest effective level).
7. **Solidarity**: cohesion funds for poorer regions.
8. **Gradualism**: step-by-step spillover.
9. **Open market economy** with social protection.

### 3.4 Theories of integration
| Theory | Thinkers | Idea |
|---|---|---|
| Functionalism | David Mitrany (*A Working Peace System*, 1943) | Cooperation in technical areas creates peace |
| **Neo-functionalism** | Ernst Haas (*The Uniting of Europe*, 1958) | **Spillover**: integration in one sector creates pressure in others; loyalties shift to the supranational level |
| Intergovernmentalism | Stanley Hoffmann; Andrew Moravcsik (liberal intergovernmentalism, 1998) | States control integration according to national interests |
| Federalism | Altiero Spinelli (Ventotene Manifesto, 1941) | A United States of Europe |
| **Democratic peace** | Kant; Michael Doyle (1983) | Democracies do not fight each other |

### 3.5 Democratic peace and EU integration (2022)
- **Definition**: the empirical observation that liberal democracies rarely, if ever, go to war with each other.
- **Basic norms**: (1) **normative** explanation: democracies share norms of peaceful conflict resolution, compromise and rule of law, which they extend to other democracies; (2) **structural/institutional**: elected leaders face voters, parliaments and a free press, which makes war costly; (3) **economic interdependence** (trade raises the cost of war; "commercial peace"); (4) **international institutions** (Kant's federation of free states).
- **Role in the EU**: membership requires democracy (Copenhagen criteria); integration locked in democracy in post-dictatorship Greece, Spain, Portugal (1980s) and post-communist states (2004); Franco-German reconciliation built on shared democracy and economic interdependence; disputes go to courts and councils, not armies; the EU won the **Nobel Peace Prize (2012)**.
- **Limits**: peace also owed to NATO and US security, Cold War pressure; democratic backsliding in Hungary and Poland (Article 7 proceedings); the theory says little about wars with non-democracies (Russia).

### 3.6 Brexit and the EU (2026)
**Background**: referendum 23 June 2016 (51.9% Leave); exit 31 January 2020; EU–UK Trade and Cooperation Agreement (from 2021); Windsor Framework for Northern Ireland (2023); UK–EU "reset" summit (May 2025) with a security and defence partnership [verify details].

**Implications for integration**
- *Negative*: loss of the second-largest economy (about 15% of EU GDP), a nuclear power and UN Security Council permanent member, a major net budget contributor; precedent of exit; trade frictions; reduced liberal, free-trade voice.
- *Positive / unexpected*: **no domino effect**: support for EU membership rose in other states; unity in negotiations (Michel Barnier led for the EU); removal of the UK's opt-outs and vetoes made **deeper integration** easier: defence cooperation (PESCO, 2017; European Defence Fund), joint borrowing (**NextGenerationEU** recovery fund, 2020, €750bn), common responses to Ukraine (sanctions, aid, candidate status).

**Implications for global influence**
- Smaller weight in the UN Security Council, G7 and trade talks; less military capability.
- But the EU's **regulatory power** (the "**Brussels effect**": GDPR, AI Act 2024, Digital Markets Act) and its single market remain globally attractive.
- Cooperation with the UK continues in NATO, on Ukraine and on sanctions.

**Judgement**: Brexit weakened the EU's size and hard power but strengthened its internal cohesion; the larger tests are Russia, US unreliability (tariffs, NATO doubts), populism and economic stagnation.

### 3.7 Current challenges
Russia's war on Ukraine (since February 2022); enlargement (Ukraine, Moldova, Western Balkans); defence ("ReArm Europe", 2025); US tariffs and NATO uncertainty under Trump; migration; democratic backsliding; energy security (Hormuz closure 2026); competitiveness (Draghi report, 2024); the far right's rise.

## 4. SAARC (2016 note, 2023, 2025)

### 4.1 Basics
| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Founded | **8 December 1985**, Dhaka (idea proposed by Bangladesh's President Ziaur Rahman, 1980) |
| Members | **8**: Afghanistan (joined 2007), Bangladesh, Bhutan, India, Maldives, Nepal, Pakistan, Sri Lanka |
| Secretariat | **Kathmandu** (1987) |
| Charter principles | Sovereign equality, non-interference; decisions by **unanimity**; **bilateral and contentious issues excluded** |
| Aim | Foster regional economic cooperation and welfare (CSS 2025 MCQ) |
| Instruments | SAPTA (preferential trade, 1993/1995); **SAFTA** (signed 2004, in force 2006); SAARC Development Fund; South Asian University (Delhi); SAARC Food Bank; Regional Convention on Suppression of Terrorism (1987) |
| Summits | 18 held; the **last was the 18th (Kathmandu, 2014)**; the **19th (Islamabad, November 2016) was postponed** after India withdrew following the Uri attack; Bangladesh, Bhutan and Afghanistan followed |
| Status | **Dormant** at summit level; India prefers **BIMSTEC** (1997, excludes Pakistan); a COVID-19 video conference in March 2020; after Bangladesh's political change (2024) and the BNP's election win (February 2026), Dhaka and Islamabad talked of reviving SAARC |

### 4.2 Performance: missed opportunities (2023)
- **Intra-regional trade** about **5%** of members' total trade (ASEAN about 25%; EU about 60%) [figures approximate].
- Connectivity: few roads, railways, power links across borders; trade often goes via Dubai or Singapore.
- World Bank estimate: South Asia's intra-regional trade could be about $67bn rather than about $23bn [verify; World Bank 2018 "A Glass Half Full"].
- Achievements are modest: SAFTA tariff cuts, cultural and people-to-people programmes, disaster cooperation, SAARC visa exemption scheme (limited), development fund.

### 4.3 Why SAARC could not replicate the EU (2025)
| Factor | EU | SAARC |
|---|---|---|
| Security conflict | Franco-German reconciliation after 1945; common threat (USSR); **NATO** and the US security umbrella | **India–Pakistan rivalry** (Kashmir, wars, terrorism allegations, May 2025 conflict); no common enemy |
| Power balance | Several large states (France, Germany, Italy, UK) balance each other | **Asymmetry**: India has about three-quarters of the region's population and GDP; neighbours fear Indian dominance (Nepal blockade 2015) |
| Institutions | Supranational (Commission, Court, Parliament); majority voting | Weak secretariat; **unanimity**; **bilateral issues barred**, so core disputes cannot be discussed |
| Political will | Leaders committed (Monnet, Schuman, Adenauer, de Gasperi) | Summit diplomacy hostage to bilateral politics |
| Economic complementarity | High intra-industry trade | Similar export baskets (textiles, agriculture); protectionism; long "sensitive lists" under SAFTA; non-tariff barriers |
| External support | Marshall Plan (US) encouraged integration | Extra-regional rivalries (US, China, Russia) |
| Political systems | All democracies after 1990s | Mixed regimes; instability (Afghanistan under the Taliban since 2021 is not recognised by most) |
| Connectivity | Dense infrastructure | Poor connectivity, closed borders (Wagah trade suspended) |
| Identity | Shared European identity and values | Divided by religion, nationalism and history (Partition, 1971) |

**Way forward**: separate trade from disputes; sub-regional cooperation (BBIN; CPEC extension); SAFTA implementation; energy grids; trust-building; a SAARC dispute mechanism; civil society and business pressure; a revived summit.

## 6. INTERNATIONAL FINANCIAL REGIMES: IMF AND WTO

### 6.1 Bretton Woods system
- **Bretton Woods Conference** (New Hampshire, July 1944): created the **IMF** and the **International Bank for Reconstruction and Development** (World Bank). Fixed exchange rates pegged to the dollar, dollar convertible to gold at $35 an ounce; ended when Nixon closed the gold window (1971).
- Trade: the planned International Trade Organization failed; **GATT** (1947) filled the gap; the **WTO** replaced GATT institutionally in 1995 (CSS 2024 MCQ).

### 6.2 International Monetary Fund
| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Founded | 1944 (agreement); came into existence December 1945; Washington DC |
| Members | **191** (Liechtenstein joined in 2024) |
| Purpose | Global monetary cooperation, exchange-rate stability, balance-of-payments support, surveillance (Article IV consultations) |
| Governance | **Quotas** decide votes and access; the **US holds about 16.5%** of votes, a veto over decisions needing **85%**; Managing Director traditionally European: **Kristalina Georgieva** (second term from October 2024) |
| Unit | **Special Drawing Right** (SDR), 1969; basket of dollar, euro, yuan (since 2016), yen, pound |
| Lending | Stand-By Arrangement (short-term); **Extended Fund Facility** (EFF, 3–4 years, structural); Poverty Reduction and Growth Trust (concessional); Rapid Financing Instrument; **Resilience and Sustainability Facility** (climate, 2022) |
| Conditionality | **Structural adjustment** (1980s–90s; CSS 2024 MCQ): fiscal austerity, devaluation, privatisation, liberalisation, subsidy cuts, tax reform; the "**Washington Consensus**" (John Williamson, 1989) |
| Largest borrower | **Argentina** (a $44bn programme in 2018 and a $20bn EFF in 2025) [verify]; others include Ukraine, Egypt, Pakistan |

**Pakistan and the IMF**: about 24–25 programmes since 1958 [verify count]; current **EFF of about $7bn** (37 months, approved September 2024) plus an **RSF of about $1.4bn** (2025); third review completed 8 May 2026; staff-level agreement on the fourth review (about $1.21bn) in October 2026, Board approval pending when checked. Conditions: tax-to-GDP increase, energy tariff reform, State Bank independence, primary surplus, privatisation (PIA sold December 2025).

### 6.3 World Trade Organization
| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Predecessor | **GATT** (signed 1947, 23 countries; rounds of tariff cuts: Kennedy 1964–67, Tokyo 1973–79, **Uruguay 1986–94**) |
| Founded | **1 January 1995** (Marrakesh Agreement, April 1994); Geneva |
| Members | **166** (Comoros and Timor-Leste joined in 2024) |
| Director-General | **Ngozi Okonjo-Iweala** (since 2021; second term from September 2025) |
| Principles | **Most-favoured nation** (non-discrimination between partners); **national treatment** (imports treated like domestic goods); reciprocity; **binding** tariffs; transparency; **special and differential treatment** for developing countries; fair competition |
| Agreements | GATT 1994 (goods); **GATS** (services); **TRIPS** (intellectual property); Agreement on Agriculture; SPS and TBT; Trade Facilitation Agreement (2013/2017); Fisheries Subsidies Agreement (in force 15 September 2025) |
| Dispute settlement | Panels and an **Appellate Body**; the Appellate Body has been **paralysed since December 2019** because the US blocks appointments; an interim appeal arrangement (MPIA, 2020) covers some members |
| Ministerial conferences | Singapore 1996; **Seattle 1999** (collapsed amid protests); **Doha 2001** (Doha Development Agenda, never concluded); Bali 2013; Nairobi 2015 (farm export subsidies abolished); Geneva MC12 2022 (fisheries, COVID vaccine TRIPS waiver); Abu Dhabi MC13 2024; **Yaoundé MC14 (26–30 March 2026)**: three decisions adopted, but no overall declaration, no WTO-reform declaration, and the **e-commerce customs-duty moratorium lapsed** for the first time since 1998 |
| Main goal | Regulate and liberalise international trade (CSS 2025, 2026 MCQs) |

### 6.4 Are global economic institutions indispensable to global governance? (2018)
**Yes**: they provide rules (WTO), crisis lending (IMF in 1997, 2008, COVID: $650bn SDR allocation in 2021), development finance (World Bank), data and surveillance, forums for coordination (G20 since 2008), dispute settlement; no alternative global mechanism exists.
**But**: legitimacy deficits (voting power skewed to the West; European MD and American World Bank president); conditionality controversies; WTO paralysis; rise of alternatives: **AIIB** (Beijing, 2016; Pakistan a founding member; an alternative donor to the World Bank, CSS 2024 MCQ), **New Development Bank** (BRICS, Shanghai, 2015), Chinese bilateral lending (BRI), BRICS (11 members by 2026).
**Judgement**: indispensable but in need of reform: quota realignment, voice for developing countries, restoring WTO dispute settlement.

### 6.5 Role in the development of less developed countries (2020) and limits on reducing inequality (2025)

**Contributions**
- IMF: balance-of-payments support prevents collapse (Pakistan 2019, 2023); policy advice; debt relief (HIPC 1996, MDRI 2005); concessional loans.
- World Bank: infrastructure, health, education loans; poverty data.
- WTO: market access helped export-led growth (China, Vietnam, Bangladesh's garments); rules protect small states against big ones in disputes (e.g. Antigua v. US, 2005).

**Limits and criticisms**
1. **Conditionality and austerity** cut social spending and raise prices, hurting the poor (Joseph Stiglitz, *Globalization and Its Discontents*, 2002; Asian crisis 1997–98).
2. **One-size-fits-all** prescriptions (Washington Consensus) ignored local contexts; Latin America's "lost decade" (1980s).
3. **Unequal governance**: rich countries dominate votes; developing countries are rule-takers.
4. **Agricultural subsidies** of rich countries (US, EU) depress world prices and hurt poor farmers; the Doha Round failed to fix this.
5. **TRIPS** raises medicine prices; the COVID vaccine waiver (2022) came late and narrow.
6. **Policy space**: WTO rules limit industrial policies that today's rich countries once used ("kicking away the ladder", Ha-Joon Chang, 2002).
7. **Debt traps**: repeated IMF programmes without structural change (Pakistan's 24 programmes).
8. **Special and differential treatment** is weak and largely unenforceable.
9. **Capacity gaps**: poor countries lack lawyers and data to use dispute settlement.
10. **Recent breakdown**: US tariffs (2025), Appellate Body paralysis, the failed MC14.

**Is the WTO a tool of developed countries for exploiting developing ones? (2023)**
- *Agree*: rules written in the Uruguay Round reflected Northern interests (TRIPS, services, investment); agricultural subsidies continue; developing countries lack bargaining power; Green Room negotiations exclude them.
- *Disagree*: one country, one vote, consensus decisions (developing countries blocked unfavourable deals at Seattle, Cancún and MC14); dispute settlement has been used successfully by developing countries (Brazil v. US on cotton, 2004); China, India and Vietnam grew through WTO-based trade; WTO membership grew from 123 GATT parties to 166 because countries want to join.
- *Balanced*: an imperfect, unequal but still rules-based system that protects the weak better than pure power politics; reform needed.

**Has the WTO made trade beneficial to most states? (2024)**
- *Successes*: average tariffs fell; world trade grew several-fold; dispute settlement resolved hundreds of cases (over 600 disputes filed since 1995 [verify]); accession of 36+ countries including China (2001); developing countries' share of world exports rose to about half.
- *Failures*: Doha Round dead; benefits concentrated in Asia, not Africa; agriculture and textiles disputes; paralysis since 2019; US unilateral tariffs and China's state subsidies undermine rules.
- *Judgement*: beneficial to most states in aggregate, unevenly distributed; now in crisis.

### 6.6 Russia–Ukraine war: policy options for IMF and WTO (2022)
- **Shocks**: food (Ukraine and Russia supplied about 30% of world wheat exports [verify]), fertiliser and energy price spikes; sanctions; supply-chain disruptions; refugee flows; debt distress in poor importers.
- **IMF options**: emergency financing (Food Shock Window, 2022); support for Ukraine (a $15.6bn EFF in March 2023); rechanneling SDRs to vulnerable countries; debt restructuring through the G20 Common Framework; policy advice on targeted subsidies instead of blanket ones; surveillance of spillovers.
- **WTO options**: keep food and fertiliser trade open; discourage export bans (MC12 declaration on food security, June 2022; WFP purchases exempted from export restrictions); transparency on trade measures; facilitate trade in essential goods; support WTO reform to restore dispute settlement; handle sanctions under security exceptions (GATT Art. XXI) carefully.
- **Limits**: neither body can stop war or sanctions; geopolitical fragmentation into blocs.
