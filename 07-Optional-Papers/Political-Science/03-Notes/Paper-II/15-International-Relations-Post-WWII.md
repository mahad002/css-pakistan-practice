## 1. The world in 1945

- About 70–85 million dead in World War II; Europe and Japan devastated; atomic bombs on **Hiroshima (6 August)** and **Nagasaki (9 August 1945)**.
- **Decline of Europe** (Britain, France exhausted) and rise of two **superpowers**: the **USA** (half of world industrial output, atomic monopoly until 1949) and the **USSR** (largest army, control of Eastern Europe).
- Wartime conferences: **Tehran** (1943), **Yalta** (February 1945: spheres in Europe, UN, Soviet entry against Japan), **Potsdam** (July–August 1945: Germany's division into zones).
- New institutions: **United Nations** (1945), **Bretton Woods** (IMF, World Bank, 1944), GATT (1947).

## 2. The Cold War (1947–1991) (2026)

### 2.1 Meaning
A prolonged global rivalry between the US-led **capitalist-democratic West** and the Soviet-led **communist East**, waged through ideology, arms races, alliances, proxy wars, propaganda, economic competition and espionage, **without direct war** between the superpowers (the term was popularised by Walter Lippmann, 1947).

### 2.2 Causes
1. **Ideological clash**: liberal capitalism vs Marxism-Leninism.
2. **Power vacuum** in Europe and Asia after 1945.
3. **Mutual suspicion**: Soviet security fears (invasions of 1812, 1914, 1941) vs Western fear of communist expansion.
4. **Disputes over Eastern Europe and Germany** (Yalta promises of free elections broken).
5. **Nuclear weapons** and the arms race.
6. **Leaders' perceptions**: Churchill's "**Iron Curtain**" speech (Fulton, 5 March 1946); George **Kennan**'s Long Telegram (February 1946) and "X" article (1947) proposing **containment**.

### 2.3 Phases

| Phase | Years | Key events |
|---|---|---|
| **Origins and tight bipolarity** | 1945–53 | **Truman Doctrine** (12 March 1947: support "free peoples" against subversion; Greece, Turkey); **Marshall Plan** (1948–52, about $13bn for Western Europe); Cominform (1947); **Berlin Blockade** and airlift (June 1948–May 1949); **NATO** (4 April 1949, 12 founders); Soviet atomic test (August 1949); Chinese revolution (October 1949); **Korean War** (1950–53) |
| **Confrontation and coexistence** | 1953–62 | Stalin's death (1953); **Warsaw Pact** (14 May 1955); SEATO (1954) and Baghdad Pact/CENTO (1955) with Pakistan; Hungarian uprising (1956); Suez crisis (1956); Sputnik (1957); **Berlin Wall** (August 1961); **Cuban Missile Crisis** (October 1962), the closest the world came to nuclear war |
| **Détente** | 1963–79 | Hotline and Partial Test Ban Treaty (1963); **NPT** (1968); Sino-Soviet split; **Nixon's visit to China** (February 1972; Pakistan facilitated Kissinger's secret trip, July 1971); **SALT I** and ABM Treaty (1972); **Helsinki Final Act** (1975); US defeat in **Vietnam** (fall of Saigon, 1975) |
| **"Second Cold War"** | 1979–85 | **Soviet invasion of Afghanistan** (December 1979); US boycott of Moscow Olympics (1980); Reagan's arms build-up and "evil empire" rhetoric; Strategic Defense Initiative (1983); support to the Afghan mujahideen through Pakistan |
| **End of the Cold War** | 1985–91 | **Gorbachev**: glasnost, perestroika, "new thinking"; **INF Treaty** (1987); Soviet withdrawal from Afghanistan (Geneva Accords 1988; completed February 1989); revolutions in Eastern Europe; **fall of the Berlin Wall (9 November 1989)**; Malta summit (December 1989); German reunification (3 October 1990); **dissolution of the USSR (26 December 1991)** |

### 2.4 Impact on international relations (2026)
1. **Bipolar structure**: emergence of two superpowers dominating world politics.
2. **Bloc politics**: military alliances (NATO vs Warsaw Pact; SEATO, CENTO, ANZUS, Rio Pact) and economic blocs (Marshall Plan/OEEC vs COMECON, 1949).
3. **Nuclear arms race and deterrence**: mutually assured destruction (MAD); arms control regimes (PTBT, NPT, SALT, ABM, INF, START).
4. **Proxy wars** in the Third World: Korea, Vietnam, Angola, Afghanistan, Nicaragua, Horn of Africa; millions died.
5. **Division of Germany, Europe and Korea**.
6. **Paralysis of the UN Security Council** through vetoes (USSR used about 90 vetoes by 1991 [verify]); the UN's role limited to peacekeeping and decolonisation.
7. **Decolonisation** accelerated and became entangled with superpower competition.
8. **Non-Aligned Movement** (Bandung 1955; Belgrade 1961: Nehru, Nasser, Tito, Sukarno, Nkrumah) as a third way.
9. **Space race** and technological competition (Sputnik 1957; Moon landing 1969).
10. **Ideological propaganda** and covert action (CIA coups in Iran 1953, Guatemala 1954, Chile 1973; Soviet interventions in Hungary 1956, Czechoslovakia 1968).
11. **Economic integration** in the West (European Communities; Japan's rise) under US security.
12. **Pakistan's alignment** with the US (1954–59 pacts; U-2 base at Badaber, 1960 incident) and its role in the Afghan jihad; India's tilt to the USSR (1971 Treaty of Peace, Friendship and Cooperation).

**Legacy**: unresolved conflicts (Korea, Taiwan, Kashmir partly), NATO's expansion and Russia's grievances (leading to the wars in Georgia 2008 and Ukraine 2014/2022), nuclear arsenals, the Afghan war's legacy of militancy in Pakistan.

## 3. Patterns of the balance of power after 1945 (2020)

### 3.1 Concept
**Balance of power**: a distribution of power in which no single state or bloc can dominate the rest; achieved through alliances, armaments, compensations, divide and rule, buffer states and a "holder of the balance" (Hans Morgenthau, *Politics Among Nations*, 1948). Realists see it as the main mechanism of order in an anarchic system.

### 3.2 Patterns

| Pattern | Period | Features |
|---|---|---|
| **Multipolar** (before 1945) | 19th century to 1939 | Several great powers (Britain, France, Germany, Russia, Austria, later US, Japan); Britain as "holder of the balance"; flexible alliances |
| **Tight bipolarity** | 1945–mid-1950s | Two superpowers, rigid blocs; little room for neutrals (Morton Kaplan's "tight bipolar" system) |
| **Loose bipolarity** | mid-1950s–1970 | NAM, decolonisation, France's independent course (de Gaulle left NATO's command, 1966), China's split from the USSR |
| **Nuclear balance of terror** | From 1949; parity by the late 1960s | Deterrence (MAD), second-strike capability; stability through fear |
| **Strategic triangle / tripolar diplomacy** | 1970s | US–USSR–China: Nixon and Kissinger played China against the USSR |
| **Détente and arms control** | 1970s | Managed competition; SALT, Helsinki |
| **Unipolarity** ("unipolar moment", Charles Krauthammer, 1990) | 1991–c. 2008 | US hegemony: Gulf War (1991), NATO expansion, Kosovo (1999), Afghanistan (2001), Iraq (2003) |
| **Multipolarity / "multiplex" world** | 2008– | Rise of China, India, return of Russia; 2008 financial crisis; BRICS (2009); G20 |
| **Emerging US–China bipolarity** | 2017– | Trade and tech war; Indo-Pacific strategy, Quad (revived 2017), AUKUS (2021); China's Belt and Road (2013) |
| **Today's fragmentation** (2022–26) | — | Russia's war on Ukraine; Russia–China "no limits" partnership (2022); the **2026 Iran war** and closure of Hormuz; US tariffs and doubts over alliances; middle powers (Türkiye, Saudi Arabia, India, Brazil) hedging; Pakistan balancing between China and the US |

### 3.3 Debate on stability
- **Kenneth Waltz** (1964, *Theory of International Politics* 1979): **bipolarity is more stable** (clear responsibilities, less miscalculation); the "Long Peace" (John Lewis Gaddis) in Europe 1945–91.
- **Karl Deutsch and David Singer** (1964): **multipolarity** is more stable (more interactions, cross-cutting interests).
- **Hegemonic stability theory** (Kindleberger, Gilpin): a hegemon provides order (US after 1945).
- **Power transition theory** (Organski): war risk rises when a challenger approaches the leader's power (US–China "Thucydides Trap", Graham Allison, 2017).

## 4. The United Nations: peace, security and development (2026)

### 4.1 Basics
- **Charter** signed on **26 June 1945** at San Francisco by 50 states (Poland signed later as the 51st founding member); in force **24 October 1945** (UN Day).
- **193 members** (South Sudan joined in 2011); Pakistan joined on **30 September 1947**.
- **Purposes** (Art. 1): maintain peace and security; develop friendly relations based on equal rights and **self-determination**; international cooperation on economic, social, cultural and humanitarian problems and human rights.
- **Principles** (Art. 2): sovereign equality; peaceful settlement of disputes; **no threat or use of force** against territorial integrity (Art. 2(4)); non-intervention in domestic jurisdiction (Art. 2(7)).

### 4.2 Principal organs
| Organ | Role |
|---|---|
| **General Assembly** | All members, one vote; recommendations; budget; elects non-permanent Security Council members; "Uniting for Peace" (1950) lets it act when the Council is deadlocked |
| **Security Council** | **15 members**: 5 permanent with **veto** (US, UK, France, Russia, China) and 10 elected for two years; decisions need 9 votes and no P5 veto; binding resolutions under **Chapter VII** (sanctions, use of force). **Pakistan is a non-permanent member for 2025–26** (its eighth term) |
| ECOSOC | 54 members; economic and social coordination |
| **International Court of Justice** | The Hague; 15 judges; contentious cases between states and advisory opinions (e.g. 2024 opinion on Israel's occupation; South Africa v. Israel genocide case, 2024–) |
| **Secretariat** | Headed by the **Secretary-General**: **António Guterres** (since 2017; term ends **31 December 2026**). As of mid-November 2026, the Security Council had not yet recommended a successor; candidates included Rebeca Grynspan and Rafael Grossi (Al Jazeera, 15 Nov 2026) **[verify outcome]** |
| Trusteeship Council | Suspended since 1994 (Palau, the last trust territory, became independent) |

### 4.3 Peace and security: successes
- **Preventing a third world war** by providing a forum for superpower diplomacy (Cuban crisis talks).
- **Peacekeeping**: first mission UNTSO (1948) and UNMOGIP in Kashmir (1949); UNEF (1956); about 70+ operations; Nobel Peace Prize (1988). Successful transitions in **Namibia** (1989–90), Mozambique, El Salvador, Cambodia (1992–93), Timor-Leste. **Pakistan is one of the largest troop contributors** (over 235,000 personnel since 1960 [verify]).
- **Decolonisation**: Declaration on the Granting of Independence (GA Resolution 1514, 1960); membership grew from 51 to 193.
- **Arms control**: NPT (1968), Chemical Weapons Convention (1993), Comprehensive Test Ban Treaty (1996, not yet in force).
- **International law**: UNCLOS (1982); International Criminal Court (1998, outside the UN but linked); tribunals for Yugoslavia and Rwanda.
- **Human rights**: UDHR (1948) and treaties; Human Rights Council.
- **Gaza**: Security Council Resolution **2803** (17 November 2025) after the October 2025 ceasefire.

### 4.4 Development and humanitarian successes
UNDP; UNICEF; WHO (smallpox eradicated 1980); WFP (Nobel 2020); UNHCR (Nobel 1954, 1981); FAO; **Millennium Development Goals** (2000–15) and **Sustainable Development Goals** (2015–30: 17 goals); **Paris Agreement** (2015) under the UNFCCC; disaster relief (Pakistan's 2022 floods appeal).

### 4.5 Limitations
1. **Veto paralysis**: Syria (Russia and China's vetoes), Ukraine (Russia), Gaza (US vetoes before 2025).
2. **Failures to prevent atrocities**: **Rwanda** (1994), **Srebrenica** (1995), Darfur, Myanmar's Rohingya, Sudan (since 2023).
3. **Unimplemented resolutions**: **Kashmir** (Res. 47 of 1948 calling for a plebiscite), Palestine (Res. 242 of 1967, 338).
4. **Great powers bypass it**: NATO's Kosovo campaign (1999), US invasion of Iraq (2003), the 2026 Iran war.
5. **Unrepresentative Security Council**: no permanent seat for Africa or Latin America; reform deadlock between the **G4** (India, Germany, Japan, Brazil) and **Uniting for Consensus** (including Pakistan, Italy, Mexico).
6. **Funding crisis**: US cuts in 2025–26; the "UN80" reform initiative cut budgets and posts [verify scale].
7. **Bureaucracy and inefficiency**; peacekeeping abuses (cholera in Haiti, 2010).
8. **SDGs off track** (most targets behind schedule by 2025).

**Judgement**: the UN is indispensable as a forum, norm-setter and humanitarian actor, but its security role is limited by great-power politics; "the UN was not created to take humanity to heaven, but to save it from hell" (Dag Hammarskjöld, paraphrase).

## 5. Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty and a nuclear-free South Asia (2022)

### 5.1 The nuclear race after 1945
US (1945), USSR (1949), UK (1952), France (1960), China (1964); India's "peaceful" test (1974) and 1998 tests; Pakistan's tests (**28 and 30 May 1998**); North Korea (2006 onward); Israel (undeclared).

### 5.2 The NPT
- Opened for signature **1 July 1968**; in force **5 March 1970**; extended **indefinitely** in 1995; **191 parties** (North Korea announced withdrawal in 2003); **India, Pakistan and Israel never joined**; South Sudan has not joined.
- **Three pillars**: (1) **non-proliferation**: non-nuclear states will not acquire weapons; nuclear-weapon states (those that tested before 1 January 1967: US, USSR/Russia, UK, France, China) will not transfer them; (2) **disarmament** (Art. VI): nuclear states will pursue negotiations in good faith; (3) **peaceful use** of nuclear energy under **IAEA safeguards** (Art. IV).
- **Achievements**: limited the number of nuclear states to nine (President Kennedy had feared 15–25 by the 1970s); South Africa, Ukraine, Belarus, Kazakhstan gave up weapons; safeguards and inspections; nuclear-weapon-free zones (Latin America: Tlatelolco 1967; South Pacific; Southeast Asia; Africa: Pelindaba; Central Asia, 2006).
- **Weaknesses**: discriminatory ("nuclear apartheid": India's critique); weak disarmament by nuclear states; **New START expired (5 February 2026)** with no replacement; Review Conferences failed in 2015, 2022 and **May 2026**; withdrawal (North Korea); Iran's dispute and the 2025–26 strikes; the US–India civil nuclear deal (2005–08) and NSG waiver (2008) seen in Pakistan as undermining the regime.

### 5.3 What more is needed for a nuclear-free South Asia?
- **Pakistan's proposals** (1970s–90s): a South Asian nuclear-weapon-free zone (UN General Assembly resolutions from 1974), simultaneous accession to the NPT, mutual inspections, a bilateral test ban, a **Strategic Restraint Regime** (1998 onward: nuclear and missile restraint, conflict resolution, conventional balance). India rejected these, citing China.
- **Realities**: India (about 190 warheads) and Pakistan (about 170) (SIPRI Yearbook 2026); China (about 620); Pakistan's "full-spectrum deterrence" and short-range systems (Nasr); India's no-first-use doctrine (with qualifications) vs Pakistan's first-use option; the May 2025 crisis between two nuclear states.
- **Steps**: crisis communication (hotlines; the 1988 agreement on not attacking nuclear facilities, lists exchanged every 1 January since 1992; 2005 agreement on pre-notification of ballistic missile tests); confidence-building measures; resolving Kashmir and water disputes; a bilateral moratorium on testing (both maintain unilateral moratoria); limits on deployments; non-discriminatory access to the NSG (Pakistan seeks criteria-based entry); global disarmament progress (Art. VI); involving China in a trilateral framework.
- **Judgement**: a nuclear-free South Asia is unrealistic soon; **strategic stability** and risk reduction are the achievable aims.
