## 1. Background: five republics

| Regime | Dates | Note |
|---|---|---|
| First Republic | 1792–1804 | After the Revolution of 1789 |
| Napoleonic Empire, restored monarchy | 1804–48 | Napoleonic Code (1804) |
| Second Republic | 1848–52 | Universal male suffrage |
| Second Empire | 1852–70 | Napoleon III |
| Third Republic | 1870–1940 | Weak executive, unstable governments |
| Vichy | 1940–44 | Collaborationist |
| **Fourth Republic** | 1946–58 | **Parliamentary**; 24 governments in 12 years; collapsed over the Algerian war |
| **Fifth Republic** | **1958–** | **Charles de Gaulle**'s constitution: strong presidency |

The Fifth Republic was a reaction to the Fourth's "regime of parties" and instability: de Gaulle's Bayeux speech (1946) called for a strong head of state above parties.

---

## 2. The Constitution of the Fifth Republic

- Adopted by referendum on 28 September 1958 (about 80% yes); promulgated **4 October 1958**. Drafted mainly by **Michel Debré**.
- **Written and rigid**: amendment (Art. 89) by referendum, or by Parliament meeting as a **Congress** at Versailles with a **three-fifths** majority. Amended about 25 times.
- **Key amendments**: **1962** (referendum): direct popular election of the President; **2000** (referendum): presidential term cut from seven to **five years** (from 2002), aligning it with the Assembly's term; **2008**: limit of **two consecutive terms**, citizens' right to challenge laws before the Constitutional Council (**QPC**, from 2010), more power for Parliament; **2024**: guaranteed freedom to have an abortion (Art. 34; adopted by Congress on 4 March 2024).
- **Preamble** refers to the 1789 Declaration of the Rights of Man, the 1946 preamble (social rights) and the 2004 Environment Charter.
- **Principles**: France is an "indivisible, secular (*laïque*), democratic and social Republic" (Art. 1); national sovereignty belongs to the people (Art. 3); unitary but decentralised (2003).

## 3. A semi-presidential (mixed) system

Maurice **Duverger** (1980) defined semi-presidentialism as: (1) a president elected by universal suffrage, (2) with considerable powers, (3) alongside a prime minister and ministers who depend on the confidence of the legislature.

- **Dual executive**: President (head of state) + Prime Minister (head of government).
- When the President's party controls the National Assembly, the system works like a strong presidential one ("presidential majority").
- When it does not, **cohabitation**: the President must appoint a PM acceptable to the Assembly, and power shifts to the PM in domestic policy. Cases: **1986–88** (Mitterrand–Chirac), **1993–95** (Mitterrand–Balladur), **1997–2002** (Chirac–Jospin).
- Since 2024 a third situation: **no majority at all** (a hung Assembly) and unstable minority governments.

---

## 4. The President

### 4.1 Election
Directly elected (CSS 2025 MCQ) by **two-round** majority system: if no candidate wins 50% in the first round, the top two meet two weeks later. Five-year term; at most two consecutive terms.

### 4.2 Powers
**Without countersignature** (personal powers):
- Appoints the **Prime Minister** (Art. 8) (cannot dismiss him formally, but in practice PMs resign when asked if the President has a majority).
- **Dissolves the National Assembly** (Art. 12), but not again within a year of an election after dissolution.
- Submits bills to a **referendum** (Art. 11).
- **Emergency powers** (Art. 16) in a grave threat: used once, in 1961 (Algiers generals' putsch).
- Sends messages to Parliament; refers laws to the Constitutional Council; appoints three of its members, including its president.

**With countersignature** (shared): presides over the **Council of Ministers**; signs ordinances and decrees; appoints senior civil and military officials; promulgates laws within 15 days and can ask for reconsideration; **commander-in-chief** and head of the nuclear deterrent; negotiates and ratifies treaties (foreign and defence policy is the President's "reserved domain" by practice).

**Position**: "republican monarch"; guardian of the constitution and arbiter (Art. 5); the centre of power when he has a majority.

### 4.3 Current President
**Emmanuel Macron**, elected 2017, re-elected 2022 (defeated Marine Le Pen in the run-off both times). His term ends in **May 2027**; he cannot run again. The **2027 presidential election** (first round expected April 2027) is the next big event; candidates were declaring by late 2026 (e.g. Éric Zemmour, November 2026).

## 5. The Prime Minister and Government

- **Matignon**. The PM "directs the action of the Government" (Art. 21); the Government "determines and conducts the policy of the Nation" (Art. 20) and is **responsible to Parliament**.
- Ministers cannot be members of Parliament at the same time (incompatibility, Art. 23): a separation element.
- **Tools of "rationalised parliamentarism"** that strengthen the government:
  - Controls the **parliamentary agenda** (partly).
  - **Article 44.3** ("package vote"): forces a single vote on a whole text.
  - **Article 49.3**: the government can pass a bill **without a vote** unless a **motion of censure** is adopted within 24 hours by an absolute majority (289 deputies). Élisabeth Borne used it for the 2023 pension reform (retirement age 62 → 64).
  - **Art. 34/37**: Parliament legislates only in listed domains; everything else is regulation by decree.
- **Censure**: the Assembly can bring down the government by an absolute majority (Art. 49.2). This happened in **1962** (Pompidou) and then on **4 December 2024** (Michel Barnier, after using 49.3 on the social security budget). François Bayrou's government fell on a **confidence vote** on 8 September 2025.

### 5.1 Current Prime Minister
**Sébastien Lecornu**: appointed 9 September 2025; resigned 6 October 2025 after his first cabinet lasted about 14 hours; **reappointed 10 October 2025**; a new Lecornu government was announced on **26 February 2026** (official government website, page updated August 2026). A November 2026 headline mentioned "a new government"; I could not confirm a change of PM, so check before the exam **[verify]**.

Sequence since 2024: Gabriel Attal (Jan–Sept 2024) → Michel Barnier (Sept–Dec 2024) → François Bayrou (Dec 2024–Sept 2025) → Sébastien Lecornu (Sept 2025–). Five governments in two years: the Fifth Republic's worst instability.

## 6. Parliament

### 6.1 National Assembly (2018 question)

**Composition**
- **577 deputies** elected for **five years** in single-member constituencies by a **two-round system**: a candidate wins in round one with an absolute majority and at least 25% of registered voters; otherwise candidates with at least 12.5% of registered voters go to round two, where a plurality wins.
- Includes deputies for overseas departments and French citizens abroad.
- Can be dissolved by the President (dissolutions: 1962, 1968, 1981, 1988, 1997, **2024**).
- Presided by the President of the National Assembly (Yaël Braun-Pivet since 2022 [verify after 2024]).

**Powers**
1. **Legislation**: votes laws (with the Senate); has the **final word** if the two houses disagree (Art. 45, after a joint committee).
2. **Budget**: votes finance and social security laws (with time limits; the government can enact by ordinance if Parliament fails to act in 70 days).
3. **Control of government**: questions to the government (weekly), committees of inquiry, **motion of censure**, confidence votes.
4. Ratifies some treaties; authorises war declarations and long military deployments (over four months).
5. With the Senate, amends the constitution (Congress) and can remove the President for a "breach of duty manifestly incompatible with the exercise of his mandate" (High Court, Art. 68).

**Position**
- Weaker than the Fourth Republic's Assembly and than the UK Commons, due to rationalised parliamentarism (Art. 34, 37, 44.3, 49.3), the President's dissolution power and limited committees (eight standing committees).
- But stronger since **2008** (agenda shared; committee text debated in plenary) and since **2022–24**, when no party has had a majority: governments must negotiate, and censure is a real threat.

**2024 snap election** (30 June and 7 July 2024, after Macron dissolved the Assembly following the RN's European election win): no majority. Approximate seats: left alliance **New Popular Front** about 180; Macron's **Ensemble** about 160; **RN** and allies about 140; Les Républicains about 45 [figures approximate].

### 6.2 Senate
- **348 senators**, elected **indirectly** for **six years** by about 160,000 *grands électeurs* (mostly municipal councillors), half renewed every three years (last in September 2023; next September 2026 [verify outcome]).
- Represents territorial communities; dominated by the centre-right (LR); President of the Senate (Gérard Larcher) is the interim President of the Republic.
- Cannot bring down the government; can delay; equal power on constitutional amendments and organic laws relating to the Senate.

## 7. The Judiciary (2016 question)

### 7.1 Features
1. **Dual court system**:
   - **Judicial order** (ordinary courts): civil and criminal; first-instance courts, Courts of Appeal, and the **Court of Cassation** (the highest court, which reviews points of law and sends cases back).
   - **Administrative order**: disputes between citizens and the state: administrative tribunals, administrative courts of appeal, and the **Council of State** (*Conseil d'État*), which also advises the government on bills.
   - **Tribunal of Conflicts** decides which order has jurisdiction.
2. **Constitutional Council** (*Conseil constitutionnel*): **nine members** for nine-year non-renewable terms, three each appointed by the President of the Republic, the President of the National Assembly and the President of the Senate (former Presidents are ex-officio members). Reviews laws **before promulgation** (a priori) on referral by the President, PM, presiding officers or 60 deputies or senators; since **2010** also reviews laws **after** promulgation through the **QPC** raised by litigants. Supervises elections and referendums.
3. **Civil law** tradition: codified law (Napoleonic Code, 1804); judges apply codes; precedent is persuasive but not binding.
4. **Inquisitorial procedure**: the judge (and the examining magistrate, *juge d'instruction*, in serious cases) leads the investigation.
5. **Career judiciary**: judges trained at the National School for the Judiciary (ENM, Bordeaux) after a competitive exam; prosecutors and judges belong to one corps (*magistrature*).
6. **High Council of the Judiciary** (CSM): appointments and discipline (Art. 64–65); the President is "guarantor of the independence of the judicial authority".
7. **Court of Justice of the Republic** tries ministers for offences in office; **High Court** for presidential removal.

### 7.2 Comparison

| Point | France | Britain | Pakistan |
|---|---|---|---|
| Legal tradition | Civil (codified) law | Common law (precedent) | Common law + Islamic law elements |
| Procedure | Inquisitorial | Adversarial | Adversarial |
| Court structure | Dual: ordinary + administrative | Single hierarchy (administrative cases in ordinary courts and tribunals) | Single hierarchy + Federal Shariat Court + (since 2025) Federal Constitutional Court; service tribunals |
| Apex court | Court of Cassation; Council of State; Constitutional Council | Supreme Court of the UK (2009) | Supreme Court; Federal Constitutional Court (2025) for constitutional matters |
| Constitutional review | Constitutional Council: a priori + QPC | None over Acts of Parliament (declarations of incompatibility) | Full judicial review (Arts. 184(3), 199; now FCC) |
| Judges | Career magistrates via exam | Senior lawyers appointed by Judicial Appointments Commission | Lawyers and judicial officers; Judicial Commission (Art. 175A, restructured by the 26th Amendment) |
| Independence | CSM; but prosecutors linked to the Justice Ministry | Strong convention, Constitutional Reform Act 2005 | Formal guarantees; contested by the 26th and 27th Amendments |

## 8. Parties and the Party System

- **Multi-party**, historically organised into **two blocs** (left and right) by the two-round system ("bipolar multipartism").
- Since 2017: collapse of the traditional parties (Socialists, Republicans), rise of **Macron's centre** and the **far right**: now **tripolar** (left, centre, far right).

## 9. Semi-presidentialism: effectiveness for stability and against authoritarianism (2025)

### 9.1 For stability
- **1958–2022**: remarkable stability compared with the Fourth Republic: strong presidents, disciplined majorities, Art. 49.3, dissolution power, the five-year alignment of presidential and Assembly terms (since 2002) usually giving presidents majorities.
- **Cohabitation** worked peacefully three times: the system adapted without crisis.
- Survived 1968's unrest, Algeria, de Gaulle's resignation (1969).

### 9.2 Against stability
- **2022–26**: relative majority (2022) then **hung Assembly** (2024); five governments in two years; budgets passed by 49.3 or not at all; censure of Barnier (2024); confidence defeat of Bayrou (2025); Lecornu's resignation and reappointment (2025).
- Dual executive confuses responsibility; a president who cannot be removed by Parliament but a PM who can.

### 9.3 Safeguards against authoritarianism
Constitutional Council; term limits (two consecutive terms since 2008); five-year term; independent judiciary and Council of State; bicameral Parliament and a Senate not controlled by the President; motion of censure; free press and strong civil society (yellow vests 2018–19; pension protests 2023); EU and ECHR constraints; cohabitation.

### 9.4 Risks
Art. 16 emergency powers; presidential dominance of foreign policy; Art. 49.3 overuse; the "republican monarchy"; a far-right president with a majority could use these tools.

**Judgement**: the system has avoided authoritarianism through institutional checks and democratic culture, and delivered stability when the President has a majority; it struggles when the Assembly is fragmented, as since 2024.
