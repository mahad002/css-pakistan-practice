# 04. Political System of Germany

Asked: party systems of France and Germany 2019; party systems of the USA and Germany 2026. MCQs: Bundestag (2024, 2026); Grundgesetz (2025); Germany as a federal parliamentary democracy (2024); proportional representation (2026). Current facts as of **24 Nov 2026**.

---

## 1. Background

- German unification under Prussia (1871); **Weimar Republic** (1919–33): proportional representation without threshold, fragmented parliament, presidential emergency powers (**Article 48**), hyperinflation and depression; Hitler's appointment (1933).
- After 1945: occupation; division into the **Federal Republic** (West) and the **German Democratic Republic** (East, 1949–90).
- **Reunification on 3 October 1990**: the East's five Länder joined the Federal Republic under the Basic Law.
- Design principle: **avoid Weimar's mistakes** (fragmentation, weak governments, enemies of democracy using democratic means).

---

## 2. The Basic Law (Grundgesetz)

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Name | *Grundgesetz* (Basic Law), not "constitution", because it was meant to be provisional until reunification (CSS 2025 MCQ) |
| Adopted | **23 May 1949** by the Parliamentary Council (Bonn) |
| Rights first | Articles 1–19 list basic rights. **Article 1(1)**: "Human dignity shall be inviolable" |
| Principles (Art. 20) | Democratic and social **federal** state; popular sovereignty; separation of powers; rule of law; right to resist those who abolish the order |
| Amendment (Art. 79) | Two-thirds of the **Bundestag** and two-thirds of the **Bundesrat** |
| **Eternity clause** (Art. 79(3)) | Amendments affecting the federal division into Länder, Länder participation in legislation, or the principles of Articles 1 and 20 are **inadmissible** |
| **Militant (defensive) democracy** | Parties that seek to undermine the free democratic basic order can be banned by the Federal Constitutional Court (Art. 21): the neo-Nazi SRP (1952) and Communist KPD (1956) were banned; the NPD was not (2017: anti-constitutional but too insignificant) |
| Parties | Art. 21: parties "participate in the formation of the political will of the people"; internal democracy and disclosure of funding required |
| European integration | Art. 23: Germany participates in the EU |

## 3. Federalism

- **16 Länder** (states), including three city-states (Berlin, Hamburg, Bremen); each has a constitution, parliament (Landtag) and government headed by a Minister-President.
- **Distribution of powers**: exclusive federal (foreign affairs, defence, citizenship, currency); concurrent (civil and criminal law, economy, labour); Länder residual powers (education, culture, police, local government).
- **Administrative (executive) federalism**: the federation makes most laws; the **Länder implement** them. Hence "**cooperative federalism**" and "interlocking politics" (*Politikverflechtung*, Fritz Scharpf).
- **Fiscal equalisation** between rich and poor Länder.
- **Federalism reforms** 2006 and 2009 (debt brake in Art. 109/115; 2025 reform loosened it for defence and created a €500bn infrastructure fund [verify size]).
- Compared with Pakistan: Germany's Länder take part in federal legislation through the **Bundesrat**, made of Land governments; Pakistan's Senate is elected by provincial assemblies but not by provincial governments; both have revenue-sharing and an apex constitutional court.

## 4. The Executive

### 4.1 Federal President (Bundespräsident)
- Elected for **five years** (maximum two consecutive terms) by the **Federal Convention** (*Bundesversammlung*): all Bundestag members plus an equal number of delegates chosen by Land parliaments.
- **Largely ceremonial**: signs laws (can refuse if clearly unconstitutional: done a handful of times), proposes the Chancellor candidate, appoints ministers and judges on proposal, dissolves the Bundestag in limited cases (Art. 63(4), 68), represents Germany abroad.
- Current: **Frank-Walter Steinmeier** (since 2017; re-elected 2022). His second term ends in **March 2027**, so a Federal Convention will meet in early 2027 to elect a successor **[verify date]**.

### 4.2 Federal Chancellor (Bundeskanzler)
- Elected by the **Bundestag** by an **absolute majority** of its members (the "Chancellor majority"), on the President's proposal; if that fails, further ballots (Art. 63).
- **Three principles** (Art. 65):
  1. **Chancellor principle**: the Chancellor sets the guidelines of policy (*Richtlinienkompetenz*) and is responsible for them.
  2. **Departmental principle**: each minister runs his ministry independently within those guidelines.
  3. **Cabinet principle**: disputes between ministers are decided by the cabinet.
- **Constructive vote of no confidence** (Art. 67): the Bundestag can remove the Chancellor **only by electing a successor** at the same time. Prevents "negative majorities" bringing down governments without an alternative (Weimar lesson). Used successfully once (1982: **Helmut Kohl** replaced **Helmut Schmidt**); failed in 1972 (against Willy Brandt).
- **Confidence vote** (Art. 68): the Chancellor may ask for confidence; if he loses, the President may dissolve the Bundestag within 21 days. Used to call early elections: 1972, 1982, 2005, and **December 2024** (Olaf Scholz lost a confidence vote on 16 December 2024 after his "traffic-light" coalition collapsed; election **23 February 2025**).
- This makes Germany a "**chancellor democracy**" (*Kanzlerdemokratie*): strong, stable chancellors (Adenauer 14 years, Kohl 16, Merkel 16).

### 4.3 Current Chancellor
**Friedrich Merz** (CDU). On **6 May 2025** he **failed in the first ballot** (310 votes, six short of the 316 needed), the first such failure in post-war history, then was elected the same day in a second ballot. Leads a **CDU/CSU–SPD coalition** ("black-red"); Vice-Chancellor and Finance Minister **Lars Klingbeil** (SPD).

## 5. The Legislature

### 5.1 Bundestag (Federal Diet)
- The lower house and the only directly elected federal body (CSS 2024, 2026 MCQs: Germany's parliament is the Bundestag; its lower house is the Bundestag).
- **Four-year** term.
- **Electoral system**: **mixed-member proportional** ("personalised proportional representation"). Each voter has **two votes**: the **first vote** for a constituency candidate (299 constituencies, FPTP) and the **second vote** for a party list. The second vote decides each party's share of seats.
- **Threshold**: **5%** of second votes, or winning at least **three constituencies** (the "basic mandate clause", which the Constitutional Court restored in 2024 after the 2023 reform tried to abolish it).
- **2023 electoral reform**: size **capped at 630** seats; no more overhang and balancing seats. A constituency winner may not take his seat if his party's second-vote share does not cover it (in 2025, 23 constituency winners were not seated [verify]).
- Functions: elects the Chancellor; legislates; controls government (questions, committees of inquiry, the Parliamentary Commissioner for the Armed Forces); approves budgets and military deployments ("parliamentary army").
- Strong **committees** and parliamentary groups (*Fraktionen*); party discipline.

### 5.2 Bundesrat (Federal Council)
- Represents the **Länder governments**, not voters. **69 members** (each Land has **3 to 6 votes** according to population); members are Land ministers, who vote **en bloc** on their government's instructions.
- **Consent laws** (*Zustimmungsgesetze*): laws affecting Länder administration or finances need Bundesrat approval (absolute veto); about a third to a half of laws.
- **Objection laws** (*Einspruchsgesetze*): the Bundesrat can object; the Bundestag can override.
- **Mediation Committee** resolves disputes.
- Constitutional amendments need two-thirds in both.
- Different majorities in Bundestag and Bundesrat can block federal policy (a form of divided government).

---

## 6. The Judiciary

- **Federal Constitutional Court** (*Bundesverfassungsgericht*), **Karlsruhe**, founded 1951: 16 judges in two senates; elected half by the Bundestag and half by the Bundesrat, each by **two-thirds**; **12-year** non-renewable terms.
- **Powers**: abstract and concrete review of laws; **constitutional complaints** by any citizen (most cases); disputes between federal organs and between federation and Länder; **party bans**; election disputes; impeachment of the President.
- **Landmark rulings**: KPD ban (1956); Lisbon Treaty ruling (2009: limits to EU integration); climate ruling (2021: insufficient climate law violated future generations' rights); **debt brake ruling (November 2023)**: reallocating €60bn of unused COVID credit to climate funds was unconstitutional, causing a budget crisis that helped bring down the Scholz coalition.
- Other courts: five federal supreme courts (ordinary, administrative, financial, labour, social); civil law tradition.
- The Court is among the most trusted institutions in Germany and a model for others (South Africa, South Korea).

### 7.2 Features
- **Multi-party** but historically **stable**: from the 1960s to 1980s a "two-and-a-half party system" (CDU/CSU, SPD, and the FDP as kingmaker); then Greens (1983), PDS/Linke (1990s), AfD (2017 entry).
- **Coalition governments** are the norm; coalition agreements are detailed contracts.
- **Strong, programmatic, membership-based** parties with think tanks (party foundations: Konrad Adenauer Foundation, Friedrich Ebert Foundation).
- **Constitutional status** (Art. 21) and **public funding** (matching funds based on votes and donations).
- **Party discipline** in parliament.
- "**Party state**" (*Parteienstaat*): parties dominate recruitment to public office.
- **Militant democracy**: party bans possible; in May 2025 the domestic intelligence agency classified the AfD as "confirmed right-wing extremist" (suspended pending court challenge) **[verify status]**.
- **The "firewall"** (*Brandmauer*): mainstream parties refuse to cooperate with the AfD. Under pressure since the AfD's **landslide win in the Saxony-Anhalt state election (September 2026)**.
- **Fragmentation**: the two "people's parties" (CDU/CSU and SPD) won over 80% of votes in the 1970s but only about 45% in 2025.

### 7.3 French vs German party systems (2019)

| Point | France | Germany |
|---|---|---|
| Electoral system | Two-round majority in single-member seats | Mixed-member proportional with 5% threshold |
| Number | Multi-party, organised in blocs; now tripolar | Multi-party, coalition-based |
| Stability | Volatile: new parties created around leaders (Macron's party in 2016) | Historically stable parties with long histories (SPD 1863) |
| Organisation | Weak memberships; personality-driven | Mass membership, strong organisation, foundations |
| Constitutional status | Art. 4: parties "contribute to the exercise of suffrage" | Art. 21: detailed; bans possible |
| Funding | Public funding, strict caps | Generous public funding |
| Role of executive | Presidential elections dominate party life | Parliamentary: parties choose the Chancellor via coalitions |
| Extremes | RN is a major force, near power | AfD second-largest party, but isolated by the firewall |
| Result | Bipolar alternation until 2017; now fragmentation and hung parliament | Coalition stability (constructive no-confidence) but slower government formation |

### 7.4 US vs German party systems (2026)

| Point | USA | Germany |
|---|---|---|
| Type | **Two-party** | **Multi-party** (six groups in the Bundestag) |
| Cause | FPTP and Electoral College (Duverger's law) | MMP proportional representation with 5% threshold |
| Organisation | Decentralised, weak national parties; candidates build their own campaigns | Centralised, programmatic, membership parties |
| Candidate selection | **Primaries** open to voters | Party members and delegates choose candidates and rank lists |
| Discipline | Traditionally weak; rising with polarisation | Strong parliamentary discipline |
| Ideology | Broad "big tents"; polarised since the 1990s | Clearer programmes; coalition bargaining |
| Money | Private donations, super PACs (*Citizens United*) | Mostly public funding; transparency rules |
| Constitutional status | Not mentioned in the Constitution | Art. 21 |
| Government formation | Winner controls the presidency; Congress may be held by the other party (divided government from January 2027) | Coalition agreements; constructive no-confidence |
| Effect on **stability** | Stable two-party competition but **gridlock** and **shutdowns** under divided government | Stable chancellors and long governments, though coalitions can collapse (2024) |
| Effect on **representation** | Third parties and minorities under-represented; winner-takes-all | **Proportional**: seats closely match votes; small parties represented if they pass 5%; but 5% threshold excluded about 14% of votes in 2025 [verify] |
| Extremism | Absorbed inside the major parties (MAGA within the Republicans) | Separate parties (AfD), contained by the firewall |

**Judgement**: the US system gives clear choices and accountability but polarisation and gridlock; the German system gives broader representation and consensual, stable governments, but at the price of slow coalition-building and the challenge of containing a large far-right party.
