# 06. Political System of Turkey (Türkiye)

Asked: Turkish model of democracy and the army's special position 2016, 2021; military in Turkish politics 2019, 2020; leadership structures of Iran and Turkey 2025 (comparison in `07-Iran.md` §9). MCQs: the 2017 amendment that moved Turkey to a presidential system (2025). Current facts as of **24 Nov 2026**.

The country's official English name has been **Türkiye** since 2022 (UN registration); both names are acceptable in an answer.

---

## 1. Background: from empire to republic

- **Ottoman Empire** (c. 1299–1922); Tanzimat reforms (1839–76); first constitution (1876); Young Turk revolution (1908); defeat in World War I; Treaty of Sèvres (1920) partition plan.
- **War of Independence** (1919–23) under **Mustafa Kemal (Atatürk)**; **Treaty of Lausanne** (1923) recognised Turkey's borders.
- **Republic proclaimed 29 October 1923**; capital Ankara; Atatürk president until 1938.
- **Caliphate abolished 3 March 1924**; Sharia courts closed; Swiss civil code (1926); Latin alphabet (1928); women's suffrage (1934); secularism written into the constitution (1937).

### Kemalism: the "six arrows"
Republicanism; **nationalism**; **populism**; **statism** (state-led economy); **secularism** (*laiklik*: state control of religion through the Directorate of Religious Affairs, Diyanet, created 1924); **reformism/revolutionism**.

### Democratic history and coups
| Year | Event |
|---|---|
| 1946–50 | Multi-party politics; Democrat Party (Adnan Menderes) won in 1950 |
| **1960** | **Coup**: Menderes overthrown and hanged (1961); new constitution (1961) with a National Security Council and Constitutional Court |
| **1971** | **Coup by memorandum**: government forced out |
| **1980** | **Coup** (12 September, General Kenan Evren): parties banned, thousands arrested; **1982 Constitution** |
| **1997** | "**Post-modern coup**": the military's National Security Council forced Necmettin Erbakan's Islamist-led government to resign (28 February process) |
| 2007 | "**E-memorandum**": the General Staff warned on its website against Abdullah Gül's presidential candidacy; the AKP called early elections and won |
| 2008–12 | Ergenekon and Sledgehammer (Balyoz) trials of officers accused of coup plots (later many convictions quashed) |
| **15 July 2016** | **Failed coup attempt** by a faction of the army; about 250 people killed; the government blamed the **Gülen movement** (FETÖ); state of emergency until July 2018; about 150,000 public employees dismissed or suspended [verify] |

## 2. The Constitution

- **1982 Constitution**, drafted under military rule; approved by referendum (91%). Originally gave the military and the presidency strong guardianship roles; "irrevocable" articles (Arts. 1–3: republic; democratic, secular, social state under rule of law; unitary state; Turkish official language; Ankara capital) cannot be amended.
- **Amended many times**, especially during EU accession reforms (2001–04): death penalty abolished (2004), National Security Council civilianised, Kurdish-language broadcasting allowed.
- **2007** referendum: **direct election of the President** (first held 2014).
- **2010** referendum: judicial reorganisation; military officers made triable in civilian courts.
- **2017 referendum (16 April 2017)**: **51.4% yes**. Replaced the parliamentary system with an **executive presidency** from the **June 2018** elections (CSS 2025 MCQ). Abolished the office of Prime Minister; increased parliament from 550 to **600** seats; synchronised presidential and parliamentary elections every **five years**; gave the President decree powers and greater control of judicial appointments.
- **New constitution debate**: Erdoğan appointed a drafting team in May 2025; the AKP talks of a referendum; critics fear it aims to let him run again beyond 2028. The ruling alliance lacks the 360 votes needed to send amendments to a referendum (400 to pass directly) [verify current arithmetic].

## 3. The Executive: President

### 3.1 Election
Directly elected for **five years**; two-round majority system; **two-term limit**, but if parliament calls an **early election** during a president's second term, he may run again (a route Erdoğan could use before 2028).

### 3.2 Powers (since 2018)
- Head of state **and** government (the PM post is gone).
- Appoints and dismisses **vice presidents and ministers** (who need not be, and cannot remain, MPs) without parliamentary approval.
- Issues **presidential decrees** with force of law on executive matters (cannot cover fundamental rights; void if parliament passes a law on the same subject).
- Prepares and submits the **budget**.
- Declares **state of emergency** (subject to parliament).
- Appoints senior officials, university rectors, many judges: **4 of the 13 members** of the Council of Judges and Prosecutors (HSK) and **12 of the 15** Constitutional Court judges [verify numbers].
- Can **dissolve parliament**, which triggers simultaneous presidential and parliamentary elections.
- Commander-in-chief; heads the National Security Council.
- Can remain **party leader** (previously the President had to be neutral).

### 3.3 Checks
Parliament can override a veto by absolute majority, open an investigation of the President by three-fifths, and send him to trial before the Constitutional Court by two-thirds; parliament can call early elections by three-fifths. In practice, with a presidential majority, checks are weak.

### 3.4 Current
**Recep Tayyip Erdoğan** (AKP): Prime Minister 2003–14; President since **28 August 2014**; re-elected in **May 2023** (run-off: 52.2% against Kemal Kılıçdaroğlu). Vice President **Cevdet Yılmaz**. Next scheduled elections: **2028**.

## 4. The Legislature: Grand National Assembly (TBMM)

- **Unicameral**; **600 members**; five-year term.
- **Electoral system**: party-list **proportional representation** (D'Hondt) in 87 multi-member districts; national threshold **7%** (lowered from 10% in 2022; alliance votes count together).
- Powers: legislation; budget approval; ratify treaties; declare war; approve emergencies; override vetoes; investigate the President (high thresholds). Lost the power to hold the government accountable through confidence votes in 2018; written questions to the vice president and ministers remain.
- **2023 results** (approximate seats): **AKP 268**, **CHP 169**, pro-Kurdish Green Left/YSP (now **DEM Party**) **61**, **MHP 50**, **İYİ 43**, others few. The ruling **People's Alliance** (AKP + MHP + small allies) holds a majority.

---

## 5. The Judiciary

- **Constitutional Court** (15 members): reviews laws and decrees; individual applications for rights violations (since 2012); can ban parties (closed several Kurdish and Islamist parties; in 2008 narrowly declined to close the AKP but cut its funding).
- **Court of Cassation** (Yargıtay): top appeals court for civil and criminal cases.
- **Council of State** (Danıştay): top administrative court.
- **Council of Judges and Prosecutors** (HSK): appointments and discipline (13 members; 4 appointed by the President, 7 by parliament, plus the Justice Minister and deputy).
- Concerns: judicial independence weakened after 2016 (about 4,000 judges and prosecutors dismissed [verify]); lower courts defying Constitutional Court rulings; the European Court of Human Rights' rulings on Selahattin Demirtaş and Osman Kavala not implemented (Council of Europe infringement proceedings against Turkey, 2022).

## 7. The Military in Turkish Politics (2016, 2019, 2020, 2021)

### 7.1 Why the army had a special position
1. **Founding role**: the republic was created by soldiers; Atatürk was a general.
2. **Guardian of Kemalism**: the army saw itself as protector of the secular, unitary republic against Islamism, communism and Kurdish separatism.
3. **Constitutional and legal tools**: the **National Security Council** (MGK, 1961; strengthened 1982) gave generals a formal say in policy; the Internal Service Law (Art. 35) tasked the army with "protecting and preserving the Turkish Republic"; military courts; military seats on higher education and broadcasting boards.
4. **Public trust**: the army was long the most trusted institution.
5. **NATO** membership (1952) and the Cold War gave it US backing.
6. **Economic power**: OYAK (army pension fund) conglomerate.

### 7.2 Interventions
1960, 1971, 1980 coups; 1997 post-modern coup; 2007 e-memorandum (see §1).

### 7.3 Civilianisation (2002–2016)
- **EU accession reforms** (2001–04): MGK made a civilian-majority advisory body with a civilian secretary-general; military removed from higher education and broadcasting boards; defence budget placed under parliamentary audit.
- AKP's electoral strength and popular support.
- Ergenekon and Sledgehammer trials weakened the officer corps.
- 2010 referendum: officers triable in civilian courts; 1980 coup leaders lost immunity (Kenan Evren convicted in 2014).

### 7.4 After the 2016 coup attempt
- Mass purges of officers (about 40% of generals dismissed [verify]); military academies closed and replaced by a National Defence University; the gendarmerie and coast guard moved to the Interior Ministry; the General Staff placed under the Defence Ministry; the President given control of the Supreme Military Council (YAŞ).
- **Result**: full civilian (presidential) control over the military. Critics say "civilian control" became **personal control** by the President rather than democratic control.

### 7.5 Evaluation
- *Positive view of the army's past role*: kept secularism, unity and Western orientation; withdrew after each coup and returned power to civilians.
- *Negative view*: interrupted democracy four times; human rights abuses (1980); suppressed Kurds and Islamists; "tutelary democracy".
- *Today*: the army is out of politics as a guardian, but power is concentrated in the presidency: the balance moved from military tutelage to executive dominance.

### 7.6 Lessons for Pakistan
Civilian supremacy came in Turkey through **electoral strength, economic performance and EU-driven legal reforms**, and was completed after a failed coup that rallied public opinion; but without strong checks it produced a dominant executive.

## 8. The "Turkish model" of democracy (2016, 2021)

**Features claimed (2000s)**
- A Muslim-majority **democracy** with competitive elections.
- **Secular state** and pious society: the AKP's "conservative democracy" combined Islamic values with democracy and a market economy ("Muslim democracy").
- **Market economy** and strong growth (2002–13).
- **NATO** member and EU candidate (since 1999; negotiations opened 2005; frozen since 2016–18).
- Seen as a model for Arab Spring countries (2011).

**Weaknesses and criticisms**
- History of **military tutelage** (above).
- **Kurdish question**: PKK insurgency since 1984 (about 40,000 deaths); peace process 2013–15 collapsed; a new process from **February 2025** when PKK leader Abdullah Öcalan called on the group to disarm; the PKK announced its dissolution in **May 2025** and began symbolic disarmament in July 2025.
- **Democratic backsliding** since about 2013: Gezi Park protests crushed (2013); media control; post-2016 purges; executive presidency (2018); jailing of opposition figures (HDP's Selahattin Demirtaş since 2016; **Istanbul Mayor Ekrem İmamoğlu arrested in March 2025**, trial began **9 March 2026**, still jailed); Freedom House rates Turkey "**Not Free**" (since 2018).
- **Economic crisis**: currency collapse and inflation (peaked at about 85% in October 2022) [verify current rate].

**Judgement**: Turkey has moved from "tutelary democracy" (army as guardian) to "**competitive authoritarianism**" (Levitsky and Way): elections are real and the opposition can win (CHP's 2019 and 2024 local wins), but the playing field is uneven.
