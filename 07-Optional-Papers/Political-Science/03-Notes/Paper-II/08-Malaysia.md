# 08. Political System of Malaysia

Asked: "A strong political system needs a very strong political economy" with reference to China and Malaysia 2017, 2021 (repeat). Malaysia is the least-asked country, but the China–Malaysia question has appeared twice. Facts as of **24 Nov 2026**.

---

## 1. Background

- British colonial rule over the Malay states, the Straits Settlements (Penang, Malacca, Singapore), and Borneo territories (Sabah, Sarawak).
- Large Chinese and Indian immigration under colonial rule created a **plural society**: today roughly **Bumiputera** (Malays and indigenous peoples) about 70%, **Chinese** about 22%, **Indians** about 7% [verify latest census shares].
- **Malayan Emergency** (1948–60): communist insurgency defeated.
- **Independence of Malaya: 31 August 1957** (Tunku Abdul Rahman, first PM). Based on an inter-ethnic bargain: Malay political primacy and special position in return for citizenship for Chinese and Indians.
- **Malaysia formed on 16 September 1963** (Malaya + Singapore + Sabah + Sarawak). **Singapore separated on 9 August 1965**.
- **13 May 1969 riots** after an election setback for the ruling Alliance → emergency rule → **New Economic Policy** (NEP, 1971).

---

## 2. The Constitution

- **Federal Constitution of 1957** (drafted by the Reid Commission), extended in 1963. Supreme law (Art. 4). Amended often (about 60 amendment acts) because the ruling coalition long held a two-thirds majority.
- **Key provisions**:
  - **Art. 3**: Islam is the religion of the Federation; other religions may be practised in peace.
  - **Art. 152**: Malay is the national language.
  - **Art. 153**: the **special position of the Malays and the natives of Sabah and Sarawak** (quotas in public service, education, permits), balanced by the "legitimate interests of other communities".
  - **Art. 160**: defines a "Malay" as a Muslim who habitually speaks Malay and follows Malay custom.
  - **Art. 121(1A)** (1988): civil courts have no jurisdiction over matters within the jurisdiction of **Syariah courts**.
  - Fundamental liberties (Arts. 5–13), limited by security laws (e.g. the old Internal Security Act, repealed 2012; the Sedition Act).
  - Special autonomy for **Sabah and Sarawak** (Malaysia Agreement 1963): immigration control, native law; the 2021 amendment restored their status as equal partners.

---

## 3. A Federal, Parliamentary, Constitutional (Elective) Monarchy

### 3.1 Federalism
- **13 states** and **3 federal territories** (Kuala Lumpur, Labuan, Putrajaya).
- Nine states have **hereditary Malay rulers** (sultans; the ruler of Negeri Sembilan is the Yang di-Pertuan Besar; Perlis has a Raja); four states (Penang, Malacca, Sabah, Sarawak) have governors appointed by the King.
- Federal, state and concurrent lists (Ninth Schedule): the federation is highly **centralised** (finance, security, education, most taxes), while states control land, Islam, Malay custom, local government.

### 3.2 The King: Yang di-Pertuan Agong
- **Elected** by the **Conference of Rulers** (the nine hereditary rulers) from among themselves for **five years**, by a rotation convention: a unique **elective monarchy** (CSS-style MCQ fact).
- Current: **Sultan Ibrahim** of Johor, the **17th Agong**, since **31 January 2024**.
- Acts on the advice of the Cabinet, except **discretionary powers** (Art. 40(2)): appointing a Prime Minister who in his judgement commands the confidence of the majority; withholding consent to dissolve Parliament; calling a meeting of the Conference of Rulers on the rulers' privileges.
- These discretionary powers became decisive during the 2020–22 crisis, when the King chose three PMs (Muhyiddin Yassin 2020, Ismail Sabri 2021, Anwar Ibrahim 2022).
- Head of Islam in his own state and in the federal territories; supreme commander of the armed forces.

### 3.3 Conference of Rulers
The nine rulers and four governors: elects the King and deputy; must consent to laws affecting the rulers' privileges, Art. 153 and Malay special position, and Islam.

---

## 4. The Executive

- **Prime Minister**: appointed by the King as the member of the Dewan Rakyat who commands a majority; must be a citizen born in Malaysia; Cabinet collectively responsible to Parliament (**Westminster** model).
- **Current PM: Anwar Ibrahim** (PKR, Pakatan Harapan), the **10th PM**, since **24 November 2022**, heading a **"unity government"**: Pakatan Harapan (PKR, DAP, Amanah) + **Barisan Nasional** (UMNO and allies) + Sarawak's GPS + Sabah's GRS + smaller parties.
- Anwar's career: deputy PM under Mahathir, sacked and jailed (1998, the **Reformasi** movement), released 2004, jailed again 2015–18, pardoned in 2018.

---

## 5. The Legislature: Parliament

| House | Composition | Term | Powers |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Dewan Rakyat** (House of Representatives) | **222** members elected by **FPTP** in single-member constituencies | Five years (automatic dissolution five years after first sitting) | Main legislative body; money bills; confidence of the government |
| **Dewan Negara** (Senate) | **70** senators: **26** elected by state assemblies (two each) + **44** appointed by the King on the PM's advice (including four for federal territories) | Three years, maximum two terms | Delays bills (one year for ordinary bills, one month for money bills) |

- **Anti-hopping law** (2022 constitutional amendment): MPs who switch parties lose their seats, a response to the 2020 defections ("Sheraton Move").
- Voting age lowered to **18** with automatic registration ("Undi18", effective 2022).

---

## 6. The Judiciary

- **Federal Court** (apex court; headed by the Chief Justice), **Court of Appeal**, two **High Courts** (Malaya; Sabah and Sarawak), subordinate courts.
- **Syariah courts** run by states for Muslims in personal and family law; disputes over jurisdiction (conversion, custody cases).
- **1988 judicial crisis**: Mahathir's government removed the Lord President **Salleh Abas** and two judges, seen as a blow to judicial independence; the 2022 government's ex gratia payments acknowledged the injustice [verify].
- **Judicial Appointments Commission** (2009).
- High-profile cases: **Najib Razak**, former PM, convicted in 2020 (SRC International, linked to the **1MDB** scandal), jailed in 2022; sentence halved by the Pardons Board in 2024; further 1MDB trial proceedings and verdicts in 2025 **[verify the latest outcome]**.

## 7. Parties and Coalitions

| Coalition / party | Base and position |
|---|---|
| **Barisan Nasional** (BN; formerly the Alliance, 1955–73) | **UMNO** (1946, Malay nationalist) + MCA (Chinese) + MIC (Indian). Ruled continuously **1957–2018**: one of the world's longest-ruling coalitions |
| **Pakatan Harapan** (PH) | **PKR** (Anwar, multi-ethnic reformist), **DAP** (mainly Chinese, social democratic), **Amanah** (progressive Islamist) |
| **Perikatan Nasional** (PN) | **Bersatu** (Muhyiddin Yassin; Malay nationalist) + **PAS** (Pan-Malaysian Islamic Party, Islamist; won **49 seats** in 2022, the largest single party in the Dewan Rakyat) |
| **GPS** (Gabungan Parti Sarawak) | Sarawak regional coalition |
| **GRS** (Gabungan Rakyat Sabah) | Sabah regional coalition; won the most seats (29 of 73) in the **November 2025 Sabah election**; PH won only one of 22 seats it contested |

**Party system**: from a dominant-coalition system (BN, 1957–2018) to a competitive, fragmented multi-coalition system. Politics organised by **ethnicity and religion** (consociational features: an elite bargain among ethnic parties).

### Key elections
| Election | Result |
|---|---|
| GE14 (9 May 2018) | **First change of government**: Pakatan Harapan won; Mahathir (aged 92) became PM again |
| 2020 | "Sheraton Move": defections brought down PH; Muhyiddin became PM |

## 8. Political Economy

### 8.1 Development model
- From tin and rubber to **export-led manufacturing** (electronics, semiconductors; Penang a global chip-packaging hub), palm oil, oil and gas (Petronas).
- **New Economic Policy** (1971–90): two aims: eradicate poverty regardless of race, and **restructure society** so that race is no longer identified with economic function; target of **30% Bumiputera ownership** of corporate equity; quotas in education, jobs, licences. Poverty fell from about 49% (1970) to under 6% by 2000 [verify]; a Malay middle class emerged; but it bred cronyism and resentment.
- Successor policies: National Development Policy (1991), **Vision 2020** (Mahathir, 1991: developed-nation status by 2020), New Economic Model (2010), Shared Prosperity Vision 2030, **Madani Economy** (Anwar, 2023), **13th Malaysia Plan** (2026–30).
- **Mahathir** (PM 1981–2003, 2018–20): "Look East" policy (Japan, Korea), heavy industry (Proton cars), privatisation, megaprojects (Petronas Towers, Putrajaya).
- **1997–98 Asian financial crisis**: Mahathir rejected IMF programmes and imposed **capital controls** and a fixed ringgit (1998), recovering relatively fast: a case against IMF orthodoxy.
- **1MDB scandal** (2015 onward): billions stolen from a state investment fund; a key cause of BN's 2018 defeat.
- Upper-middle-income economy; GDP per capita about $12,000–13,000 [verify]; aiming for high-income status.

### 8.2 "A strong political system needs a strong political economy": China and Malaysia (2017, 2021)

**Argument**: political stability and legitimacy in both states have rested on economic performance (**performance legitimacy**), and economic success in turn depended on a strong, capable state.

| Point | China | Malaysia |
|---|---|---|
| Political system | One-party Leninist state (CPC) | Parliamentary democracy with a long-dominant coalition (BN until 2018) |
| Economic model | "**Socialism with Chinese characteristics**": reform and opening from **1978** (Deng Xiaoping); state capitalism; special economic zones; WTO entry 2001; Belt and Road (2013) | Export-led growth, FDI, state enterprises (Petronas, Khazanah), NEP affirmative action |
| How economy strengthened the polity | Growth averaging about 9–10% a year (1978–2010) lifted about 800 million out of poverty, giving the CPC legitimacy after Tiananmen (1989) | Growth and NEP redistribution kept Malay support for UMNO and bought inter-ethnic peace after 1969 |
| How the polity shaped the economy | Long-term planning (Five-Year Plans), infrastructure, stable policy, suppression of labour unrest | Stable coalition, long-term plans (Malaysia Plans, Vision 2020), developmental state |
| Stress test | 2008 crisis: huge stimulus; slowing growth since 2015; property crisis (Evergrande 2021); Xi's emphasis on "common prosperity" and security | 1997 crisis: capital controls; Reformasi; 1MDB corruption → BN's defeat in 2018 |
| Weak points | Debt, ageing, inequality, US tech restrictions | Cronyism, middle-income trap, ethnic quotas, brain drain |
| Lesson | When growth slows, the regime leans more on nationalism and control | When economic governance failed (1MDB), voters changed the government peacefully |

**Counter-argument**: strong political economies also exist in weak or unstable systems (e.g. India's growth despite coalition politics); and political institutions (rule of law, accountability) are needed for sustained growth (Acemoglu and Robinson, *Why Nations Fail*, 2012). Pakistan's lesson: frequent political instability and a weak economy reinforce each other.
