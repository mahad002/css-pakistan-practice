## Answer 1 — Ibn Khaldun: asabiyya and the rise and fall of states
*Question (CSS 2021, 2018, 2025 variants): "Ibn Khaldun, while acknowledging external factors, emphasises internal systemic factors as decisive for the decline of states." Elaborate.*

**Outline**
1. Introduction: Ibn Khaldun and the thesis
2. Context: a life amid falling dynasties
3. The new science of *'umran* and the origin of the state
4. Asabiyya: the engine of state formation
5. The life cycle: three generations, five stages
6. Internal factors of decline
7. The place of external factors
8. Critical evaluation
9. Relevance to Pakistan and today
10. Conclusion

**1. Introduction**
Abd al-Rahman Ibn Khaldun (1332–1406) wrote the *Muqaddimah* (1377) as the introduction to a universal history. In it he founded what he called *'ilm al-'umran*, the science of human social organisation. His central claim is that states rise through group solidarity (*asabiyya*) and decline through forces generated inside them: luxury, the loss of solidarity, injustice and fiscal exhaustion. External conquest, in his view, is usually only the final blow to a state already hollow within. This answer explains that argument and assesses it.

**2. Context**
Ibn Khaldun served and fled from a series of North African and Andalusian courts (Fez, Granada, Bougie, Tlemcen), lost his parents to the Black Death (1348–49) and later served as a judge in Mamluk Cairo. He watched Marinid, Hafsid and Nasrid dynasties rise and crumble within a few generations. His theory is therefore empirical: it generalises from what he saw.

**3. The new science and the origin of the state**
He criticised historians for accepting reports without testing them against the "nature of civilisation". Humans are social because they need cooperation for food and defence; but aggression among them makes a restraining authority (*wazi'*) necessary. This authority becomes royal power (*mulk*), and it goes to the group with the strongest solidarity.

**4. Asabiyya: the engine**
*Asabiyya* is group feeling: the cohesion that makes people defend one another and act together. It arises from blood ties, clientship and alliance, and it is strongest among desert or rural peoples (*badawa*), whose hard life makes them brave, self-reliant and mutually dependent. **Religion** multiplies it by removing envy and uniting the group behind one goal, as with the early Arab Muslims. A group with superior asabiyya conquers settled, softer societies and founds a dynasty.

**5. The life cycle**
A dynasty normally lasts **three generations, about 120 years**:
- the **builders**, who keep desert virtues and share power;
- the **maintainers**, who imitate their fathers from memory, not experience;
- the **destroyers**, born to luxury, who think power is their birthright.

He also describes **five stages**: (1) conquest, when the ruler is one of the group; (2) autocracy, when he excludes his kinsmen and relies on clients and mercenaries; (3) leisure, when the fruits of power are enjoyed through taxes and building; (4) contentment, when rulers imitate predecessors; (5) waste and squandering, when extravagance, bad advisers and oppression lead to senility and collapse.

*Diagram:* a circle — Conquest → Autocracy → Leisure → Contentment → Waste → (new group with fresh asabiyya) → Conquest, with "asabiyya high" at the start and "asabiyya low" at the end.

**6. Internal factors of decline**
1. **Erosion of asabiyya**: sedentary life and luxury soften the ruling group; the ruler's monopoly of glory alienates his kin.
2. **Luxury and rising expenditure**: the court's habits outgrow its income.
3. **Fiscal over-extraction**: in the early stage, low tax rates on many producers yield large revenues; in the late stage, high rates on a shrinking economy yield little — an insight later compared to the Laffer curve.
4. **Injustice (*zulm*)**: seizing property and forced labour destroy the incentive to work; "injustice ruins civilisation".
5. **Reliance on mercenaries**, whose loyalty is bought, not felt.
6. **The ruler's isolation** and suspicion of his own group.
7. **State trading** that crowds out subjects.
8. **Natural senility**: a dynasty, like a person, ages.

**7. External factors**
Ibn Khaldun does not deny conquest, plague or foreign invasion. But a newcomer can only overthrow a dynasty whose asabiyya and finances are exhausted. The challenger usually comes from the periphery with fresh solidarity. External force is the **occasion**; internal decay is the **cause**.

**7a. Ibn Khaldun and other cycle theories**
Ibn Khaldun belongs to a long line of thinkers who saw political life as cyclical, and comparing him with them sharpens his originality.
- **Polybius** (2nd century BC) described *anacyclosis*: monarchy → tyranny → aristocracy → oligarchy → democracy → mob rule → monarchy. His engine is moral corruption of each ruling form; Ibn Khaldun's engine is social: the strength or weakness of group solidarity.
- **Machiavelli** borrowed Polybius in the *Discourses* and, like Ibn Khaldun, thought that founders have *virtù* that their heirs lose; but he looked for institutional remedies (a mixed republic, citizen armies) where Ibn Khaldun saw an almost natural law.
- **Giambattista Vico** (*New Science*, 1725) traced an age of gods, of heroes and of men, ending in a "barbarism of reflection" — close to Ibn Khaldun's softening of sedentary culture.
- **Oswald Spengler** and **Arnold Toynbee** (20th century) wrote of civilisations rising and decaying; Toynbee's "challenge and response" and his admiration of the *Muqaddimah* show the debt.

What distinguishes Ibn Khaldun is that he joined **sociology** (asabiyya), **economics** (labour, taxes, prices) and **politics** (royal authority) into one causal account, and that he grounded it in observation rather than speculation. He also distinguished three kinds of rule — natural kingship by force and desire, rational rule by human law, and the caliphate under the shari'a — and showed how the first two decay when the solidarity and justice that sustain them disappear. His economic insight deserves emphasis in an answer: he argued that labour is the source of value, that low taxes stimulate enterprise, that the state's entry into commerce ruins merchants and its own revenue, and that the collapse of a dynasty drags down its cities because public spending is the "great market" on which urban prosperity depends. These are internal mechanisms; they explain why a dynasty can look powerful from outside while its foundations are already rotten.

**8. Critical evaluation**
*Strengths*: an empirical, causal method four centuries before Montesquieu and Comte; attention to economics (labour, taxes, division of labour); a sociological concept (solidarity) still used by Ernest Gellner and Peter Turchin; Arnold Toynbee called the *Muqaddimah* the greatest work of its kind.
*Weaknesses*: **cyclical determinism** — modern bureaucratic and constitutional states (Britain, the United States) have outlasted three generations because institutions, not kin solidarity, hold them together; the model generalises from Berber and Arab dynasties; it neglects ideas and leadership beyond asabiyya and religion; climate determinism. Some external shocks (the Mongol sack of Baghdad in 1258) were decisive in themselves.

**9. Relevance**
- **Dynastic parties** in Pakistan show the third-generation problem: cohesion built by a founder fades in heirs.
- **Institutional cohesion** of the military functions as a modern asabiyya.
- **Fiscal crisis**: a narrow tax base, heavy indirect taxes and elite exemptions echo his warning about over-taxation and injustice.
- **State collapse from the periphery**: the Taliban's return in 2021 against an aid-dependent Kabul government fits his pattern of a cohesive peripheral group defeating a decayed centre.
- **Nation-building**: national cohesion is the asabiyya of the modern state.

**10. Conclusion**
Ibn Khaldun's insistence on internal causes is his most lasting lesson: states fall when solidarity, justice and fiscal health decay, and outsiders merely finish the work. His cycle is too rigid for institutionalised modern states, but his diagnosis — that luxury, injustice and alienation of the core group destroy power — remains a warning to any polity, including Pakistan.

## Answer 2 — Social contract: Hobbes, Locke and Rousseau
*Question (CSS 2020, 2017, 2018, 2021 variants): Critically analyse the social contract theory of Hobbes, Locke and Rousseau.*

**Outline**
1. Introduction: what social contract theory claims
2. Common framework
3. Hobbes: fear and the Leviathan
4. Locke: rights and limited government
5. Rousseau: the general will
6. Comparative table
7. General criticisms
8. Value today
9. Conclusion

**1. Introduction**
Social contract theory explains political authority as the product of an agreement among individuals who leave a pre-political "state of nature". It replaced divine right as the basis of legitimacy in early modern Europe. Its three classic versions — Hobbes (*Leviathan*, 1651), Locke (*Second Treatise*, 1689) and Rousseau (*The Social Contract*, 1762) — use the same device to reach opposite conclusions: absolutism, constitutional liberalism and popular sovereignty.

**2. Common framework**
All three begin with free and equal individuals; describe a state of nature; identify a problem that the state solves; make consent the ground of obligation; and treat the state as a human creation (an MCQ in CSS 2026 asked exactly this).

**3. Hobbes**
Writing during the English Civil War, Hobbes saw humans as driven by appetite and the fear of violent death, seeking "power after power". Because they are roughly equal, competition, diffidence and glory produce a "war of every man against every man", in which life is "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short". Reason suggests laws of nature: seek peace, give up the right to all things if others do, keep covenants. But "covenants without the sword are but words". So each person covenants with every other to authorise a sovereign — one man or an assembly — who is not a party to the contract and therefore cannot breach it. Sovereignty is absolute, indivisible and inalienable. The only reserved right is self-preservation: a subject may resist an attempt to kill him, and obedience lasts only as long as the sovereign can protect.

**4. Locke**
Locke wrote to justify the Glorious Revolution of 1688. His state of nature is governed by the law of nature (reason) and is broadly peaceful; individuals possess natural rights to life, liberty and estate, and acquire property by mixing labour with nature. But it has three inconveniences: no settled law, no impartial judge and no power to enforce judgments. People therefore consent to form a civil society, which then entrusts power to a government. Government is limited by its trust: it must rule by known laws, cannot take property without consent, and separates legislative, executive and federative powers. If it betrays its trust, power reverts to the people, who may resist — the right of revolution.

**5. Rousseau**
Rousseau began from a naturally good human being corrupted by society and private property ("the first man who enclosed a piece of ground… was the real founder of civil society"). "Man is born free, and everywhere he is in chains." The legitimate contract requires each to give himself wholly to the community, so that, obeying all, each obeys only himself. The result is a sovereign people directed by the **general will**, which aims at the common good and differs from the will of all (the sum of private interests). Sovereignty is inalienable, indivisible and cannot be represented; government is merely the people's agent; whoever refuses to obey "shall be forced to be free".

**6. Comparison**

| Point | Hobbes | Locke | Rousseau |
|---|---|---|---|
| Human nature | Selfish, fearful | Rational, social | Good, corrupted by society |
| State of nature | War | Peace with inconveniences | Idyllic, then corrupted |
| Purpose of contract | Security | Protect property and rights | Freedom and equality through community |
| Rights given up | All except self-preservation | Only to judge and punish | All, to the community |
| Sovereign | Absolute ruler | Limited government; people ultimately | The people (general will) |
| Revolution | No collective right | Yes, if trust broken | People may change government any time |
| Ideal | Monarchy | Constitutional government | Direct democracy in a small state |
| Legacy | Realism, strong state | Liberal constitutionalism | Popular sovereignty, French Revolution |

**7. General criticisms**
1. **Unhistorical**: there is no evidence of such contracts (Hume; Bentham dismissed natural rights as "nonsense upon stilts").
2. **Logical circularity**: a contract presupposes the obligation to keep promises, which only an established society enforces.
3. **Wrong theory of the state**: Hegel and Burke saw the state as an organic, historical growth; Maine showed that societies moved "from status to contract", not the reverse.
4. **Consent is fictional**: Locke's "tacit consent" by using a country's roads is not real consent.
5. **Dangerous conclusions**: Hobbes justifies absolutism; Rousseau's general will was claimed by the Jacobins and, critics such as J.L. Talmon argue, by modern dictatorships; Locke's theory protected property-owners.
6. **Individualism**: communitarians argue that people are formed by communities before they can contract.

**8. Value today**
Despite these flaws, the theory gave modern politics its core idea: legitimacy rests on the consent of the governed. The US Declaration of Independence (1776) is Lockean; the French Declaration of 1789 calls law "the expression of the general will"; realists in international relations describe the world as a Hobbesian anarchy. John Rawls revived the contract as a thought experiment (the "veil of ignorance") to derive principles of justice. In Pakistan, debates about military rule (order versus rights), the 18th Amendment as a "new social contract" between centre and provinces, and the people's right to elect governments all draw on this tradition.

**8a. Which theory fits Pakistan's experience?**
Each theory illuminates a different part of Pakistan's history.
- **Hobbes** explains the appeal of strong government in moments of disorder: every military ruler (1958, 1977, 1999) justified intervention as rescue from chaos, and many citizens accepted it as the price of order. His principle that "the end of obedience is protection" also explains the loss of public trust when the state fails to protect citizens from terrorism, as in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and Balochistan.
- **Locke** explains the constitutional settlement that elected representatives keep returning to: limited government, fundamental rights (Articles 8–28), the right to property, and the idea that government is a trust. The Lawyers' Movement (2007–09) was a Lockean protest against a government that had broken its trust with the judiciary.
- **Rousseau** explains the populist strand in Pakistani politics, in which leaders claim to embody the people's true will against "corrupt" elites and institutions, and the tension this creates with representative institutions and courts.
- **The Objectives Resolution** adds a fourth element absent in all three: the people's authority is a trust under divine sovereignty, so the contract is not purely human.

The lesson is that Pakistan needs Hobbes's effective state, Locke's limits and Rousseau's participation together. When order is pursued without limits, dictatorship follows; when limits exist without effective authority, governance fails; when "the people's will" is invoked without institutions, polarisation grows. A modern reading of the contract — consent expressed through free elections, rights enforced by independent courts, and a capable state — is the synthesis the 1973 Constitution attempts.

A further criticism worth adding is the **feminist** one (Carole Pateman, *The Sexual Contract*, 1988): the classic contract assumed male heads of households and silently excluded women from the original agreement. A complete answer notes this to show awareness of modern scholarship.

**9. Conclusion**
Hobbes asked how to escape chaos, Locke how to protect rights, Rousseau how to remain free in society. As history the social contract is a fiction; as a test of legitimacy it remains indispensable. The best synthesis takes Hobbes's insistence on effective authority, Locke's limits on power and Rousseau's demand that law express the people's will.

## Answer 3 — Plato's and Aristotle's ideal state compared
*Question (CSS 2025): Write a comparative analysis of Aristotle's and Plato's concept of the "ideal city-state".*

**Outline**
1. Introduction
2. Common background
3. Plato's ideal state
4. Aristotle's ideal and best practicable state
5. Points of comparison (table and discussion)
6. Evaluation
7. Relevance
8. Conclusion

**1. Introduction**
Plato (c. 427–347 BC) and his pupil Aristotle (384–322 BC) both sought the best form of the Greek *polis*, but they reached it by different routes. Plato reasoned deductively from the idea of justice to a city ruled by philosopher-kings; Aristotle reasoned inductively from 158 constitutions to a mixed, law-governed state resting on the middle class. Plato is the idealist, Aristotle the realist.

**2. Common background**
Both wrote after Athens' defeat in the Peloponnesian War and the execution of Socrates (399 BC), which discredited direct democracy in their eyes. Both saw the state as natural and moral, aimed at the good life, and both wanted a small, self-sufficient city with public education.

**3. Plato's ideal state (*Republic*)**
- **Justice** is each class doing its own work: rulers (reason, wisdom), auxiliaries (spirit, courage), producers (appetite, temperance).
- **Philosopher-kings** rule because ruling is knowledge of the Good; they are above written law.
- **State education** for the guardian classes, culminating in dialectic; rule begins at about fifty.
- **Communism of property and family** for guardians to remove greed and nepotism.
- **Equality of women** among the guardians.
- **Myth of the metals** to legitimise the class order.
- In the later *Laws* Plato accepted a "second-best" state ruled by law with a mixed constitution.

**4. Aristotle's ideal and best practicable state (*Politics*)**
- The state grows naturally from household and village; it exists "for the sake of the good life".
- **Ideal**: rule by the truly virtuous (monarchy or aristocracy) in a small city where citizens have leisure; farmers and artisans are not citizens; public education trains virtue.
- **Best practicable**: **polity** — a mixture of oligarchy and democracy ruled by a large **middle class**, which is neither arrogant nor envious and therefore stable.
- **Rule of law**: "law is reason free from passion".
- **Private property, common use**; the family preserved.
- **Citizenship** as sharing in deliberation and judgment.

**5. Comparison**

| Point | Plato | Aristotle |
|---|---|---|
| Method | Deductive, philosophical | Inductive, empirical |
| Rulers | Philosopher-kings | The virtuous; in practice the middle class |
| Law | Knowledge above law (*Republic*) | Sovereignty of law |
| Property | Communism for guardians | Private ownership, common use |
| Family | Abolished for guardians | Natural and necessary |
| Unity | Maximum unity | Excessive unity destroys the state |
| Women | Equal among guardians | Subordinate |
| Slavery | Hardly discussed | Natural slavery defended |
| Change | Decline through moral decay | Revolution from inequality; can be prevented |
| Character | Utopian blueprint | Practical, moderate design |

Aristotle's criticisms of Plato are central: communism means that "what is common to the greatest number has the least care bestowed upon it"; a state is a plurality, and making it too unified turns it into a household and then an individual; the ideal ignores human nature.

**5a. Points of agreement, and Aristotle's detailed critique**
It is a mistake to present the two thinkers only as opposites. They agree that:
1. the state is natural and prior to the individual in purpose;
2. its end is moral — the good life, not just security or wealth;
3. education is the state's most important function and must be public;
4. pure democracy, the rule of the poor majority for its own interest, is a corrupt form;
5. size matters: the city must be small enough for citizens to know one another;
6. rulers should rule for the common good, not their own.

Aristotle's critique of the *Republic* in Book II of the *Politics* is worth reproducing in outline because examiners reward it:
- **Unity**: Plato aims at too much unity. A state is by nature a plurality; push unity too far and it becomes a family, then an individual, and ceases to be a state.
- **Communism of wives and children**: if every child is everyone's child, no child is anyone's child; affection is "watered down" until it vanishes; crimes against kin (assault, even parricide) become more likely because no one knows who his relatives are.
- **Communism of property**: common ownership produces quarrels over effort and reward; people care least for what is common; private property allows the virtue of generosity.
- **Happiness**: Plato deprives the guardians of happiness and says the whole city will be happy — but a whole cannot be happy unless its parts are.
- **The producing class** is left vague; if it has private property and families, Plato has two cities in one, inevitably hostile.

Aristotle also accepted much from Plato's later *Laws*: the rule of law, a mixed constitution and moderation. Seen this way, Aristotle completes the realist turn that Plato himself began late in life. A strong answer makes this point: the debate between the *Republic* and the *Politics* is partly a debate inside Plato's own thought.

Both visions excluded large groups — Plato's producers had no political voice; Aristotle's citizens excluded women, slaves, foreigners and manual workers — so neither offers a model of inclusive citizenship. Modern readers take from Plato the critique of self-interested rule and from Aristotle the mechanics of stability.

**6. Evaluation**
Plato's strength is moral ambition: he saw that rulers' private interests corrupt government and that politics must educate. His weakness is the absence of consent, rights and checks — Karl Popper called the *Republic* a programme for a closed, totalitarian society. Aristotle's strength is realism: the middle class, rule of law and mixed government are still the recipe of stable constitutional democracy. His weaknesses are slavery, the exclusion of women and workers, and attachment to a city-state that Alexander's empire was already overtaking.

**7. Relevance**
- Meritocratic recruitment (the CSS exam itself) and conflict-of-interest rules echo Plato.
- Lipset's thesis that a broad middle class sustains democracy restates Aristotle; Pakistan's narrow middle class and elite capture help explain fragile democracy.
- Rule of law versus rule of men remains the core issue in Pakistan's constitutional debates.

**8. Conclusion**
Plato designed the best city for perfect rulers; Aristotle designed the best city for real people. Modern constitutional democracy is closer to Aristotle — law, a mixed constitution and a middle class — while borrowing Plato's insistence that government is a moral and educational task.

## Answer 4 — Fukuyama's "End of History"
*Question (CSS 2026): Explain Francis Fukuyama's concept of the "End of History". Give arguments for or against it.*

**Outline**
1. Introduction and thesis
2. Context
3. The concept explained
4. The two engines of history
5. The "last man"
6. Arguments in support
7. Arguments against
8. Fukuyama's later revisions
9. Relevance to Pakistan
10. Conclusion: a qualified rejection

**1. Introduction**
In 1989 Francis Fukuyama, then on the US State Department's Policy Planning Staff, argued in *The National Interest* that the world might be witnessing "the end point of mankind's ideological evolution and the universalisation of Western liberal democracy as the final form of human government". He expanded the argument in *The End of History and the Last Man* (1992). This answer argues that Fukuyama correctly identified the absence of a universal rival to liberal democracy, but wrongly implied a smooth convergence; history has returned in the form of authoritarian capitalism, nationalism and great-power war.

**2. Context**
The essay appeared as communist regimes collapsed in Eastern Europe; the Berlin Wall fell in November 1989 and the Soviet Union dissolved in December 1991. Fascism had been defeated in 1945, and now communism was failing.

**3. The concept**
"History" here is Hegel's History: the evolution of ideas about how to organise society. Fukuyama did not claim that events would stop. He claimed that liberal democracy — elected government, rule of law, rights, market economy — had no serious ideological competitor with universal appeal. Monarchy, fascism and communism had been tried and had failed.

**4. Two engines**
1. **Modern natural science and economic modernisation**: technology gives military and economic advantage and pushes societies towards market capitalism.
2. **The struggle for recognition** (from Hegel through Alexandre Kojève): humans have *thymos*, a desire for dignity. Liberal democracy satisfies it best by recognising every citizen equally (*isothymia*).

**5. The last man**
Borrowing from Nietzsche, Fukuyama worried that comfortable citizens of liberal democracies might become "last men" without great causes, and that *megalothymia* — the desire to be recognised as superior — might seek outlets in nationalism or extremism.

**6. Arguments in support**
- The number of electoral democracies rose sharply in the "third wave" (1974–2000s).
- Market economies spread everywhere, including China and Vietnam.
- No rival ideology attracts people across cultures as liberal democracy does; migrants still move towards democracies.
- Mass movements from the Arab Spring (2011) to Hong Kong (2019) and Iran's "Woman, Life, Freedom" protests (2022) demanded rights and dignity.
- Even authoritarian regimes claim to be democracies ("whole-process people's democracy" in China), paying tribute to the ideal.

**7. Arguments against**
1. **Clash of civilisations**: Samuel Huntington (1993, 1996) argued that culture and religion, not ideology, would shape conflict; liberal democracy is Western, not universal.
2. **Political Islam and 9/11** showed rival visions of order remain powerful.
3. **China** offers authoritarian capitalism that delivered rapid growth without democracy, weakening the link between markets and liberal politics.
4. **Russia's wars** in Georgia (2008) and Ukraine (2014, 2022) marked "the return of history".
5. **Democratic recession**: Freedom House has recorded declining global freedom for nearly two decades; populism has grown inside established democracies (Trump, Brexit, Orbán).
6. **Inequality and crisis**: the 2008 financial crash and rising inequality (Piketty) undermine faith in liberal capitalism.
7. **Derrida** (*Specters of Marx*, 1993) accused the thesis of ignoring misery under the "new world order".
8. **Islamic critique**: liberalism is one civilisation's model; religion remains a permanent source of meaning and law.

**7a. Huntington versus Fukuyama: the decisive comparison**
The most useful way to test the thesis is against its best-known rival.

| Point | Fukuyama (1989/1992) | Huntington (1993/1996) |
|---|---|---|
| Main unit | Ideologies and regimes | Civilisations (Western, Islamic, Sinic, Hindu, Orthodox, Latin American, Japanese, African) |
| Direction | Convergence on liberal democracy | Persistent cultural difference and conflict along "fault lines" |
| Role of religion | Declining in politics | Central and resurgent |
| Future conflict | Small, peripheral; boredom at the centre | Between civilisations, especially the West versus Islam and China |
| Prescription | Spread liberal institutions | The West should consolidate, not universalise |

Events since 1989 support parts of both: convergence in markets and technology (Fukuyama), but persistent cultural and religious identities, nationalism and civilisational rhetoric (Huntington). China's "civilisation-state" rhetoric and Russia's "Russian world" doctrine sound Huntingtonian; the global spread of demands for accountability sounds Fukuyaman.

**7b. A Muslim and Pakistani perspective**
From an Islamic standpoint, the thesis assumes that secular liberalism is humanity's final answer to the question of the good life. Muslim thinkers such as Iqbal argued instead for a spiritual democracy that combines accountability, consultation and justice with a moral foundation. Many Muslim societies, including Pakistan, accept elections, constitutions and rights while rejecting the idea that religion should be confined to private life. This suggests not the end of history but a plurality of modernities: societies adopt democratic procedures but give them their own cultural and religious content. A balanced answer can therefore agree that accountable, participatory government has become a near-universal aspiration while disagreeing that its liberal-secular form is the only legitimate end point.

**8. Later revisions**
Fukuyama himself refined the thesis. In *The Origins of Political Order* (2011) and *Political Order and Political Decay* (2014) he argued that good order needs a capable state, rule of law and accountability, and that states can decay through capture by elites and families. In *Identity* (2018) he warned that resentment and identity politics threaten liberal democracy. He maintains that no alternative has universal appeal.

**9. Relevance to Pakistan**
Pakistan illustrates both sides: elections are never abolished for long, showing democracy's legitimacy, yet military influence, dynastic parties and religious politics show the strength of non-liberal forces. Fukuyama's later triad — state capacity, rule of law, accountability — diagnoses Pakistan's weaknesses better than his 1989 optimism.

**10. Conclusion**
The thesis is best read as a normative claim, not a forecast. As a forecast of convergence it has failed; as a claim that no rival model commands universal legitimacy, it survives. I therefore reject the strong version and accept the weak one: history has not ended, but the demand for dignity that Fukuyama identified keeps pushing societies towards accountable government.

## Answer 5 — Confederation, federation and the prerequisites of an effective federation
*Question (CSS 2026; 2016): Differentiate between confederation and federation. Explain the basic principles and prerequisites of an effective federation.*

**Outline**
1. Introduction
2. Confederation: meaning, examples, features
3. Federation: meaning, examples, features
4. Differences (table)
5. Basic principles of federalism
6. Prerequisites of an effective federation
7. Pakistan's experience
8. Conclusion

**1. Introduction**
Both confederation and federation divide authority between a centre and units, but they differ on the question of sovereignty. In a confederation sovereign states delegate limited powers to a common body by treaty; in a federation a constitution creates a sovereign state in which central and regional governments are each supreme in their own spheres. Federations last; confederations usually dissolve or become federations.

**2. Confederation**
A confederation is a union of sovereign states created by treaty for limited common purposes, usually defence and foreign relations. Examples: the United States under the Articles of Confederation (1781–89), the Swiss Confederation before 1848, the German Confederation (1815–66), Senegambia (1982–89), Serbia and Montenegro (2003–06); the EU has confederal features. Features: member sovereignty; right of secession; a congress of delegates; unanimity; no direct authority over citizens; no independent revenue.

**3. Federation**
K.C. Wheare defined federalism as "the method of dividing powers so that the general and regional governments are each, within a sphere, coordinate and independent". Dicey saw it as reconciling national unity with states' rights: the units desire "union, not unity". Examples: the USA, Switzerland, Germany, India, Australia, Malaysia, Pakistan.

**4. Differences**

| Point | Confederation | Federation |
|---|---|---|
| Basis | Treaty | Constitution |
| Sovereignty | With member states | With the federation (divided internally) |
| International personality | Each member | Single |
| Centre acts on | Governments | Citizens directly |
| Secession | Permitted | Not permitted |
| Revenue | Contributions | Independent taxing power |
| Decision rule | Unanimity | Majority under the constitution |
| Durability | Weak, temporary | Stable |

The American case is the classic illustration: the Articles of Confederation could not tax, raise an army or regulate commerce, so the Philadelphia Convention of 1787 replaced it with a federal constitution.

**5. Basic principles of federalism**
1. Constitutional division of powers between two levels (an MCQ in 2026 called it the most essential feature).
2. A written and rigid constitution.
3. Supremacy of the constitution.
4. An independent judiciary as umpire.
5. A bicameral legislature with an upper house representing units.
6. Dual government acting directly on citizens.
7. Fiscal arrangements that give each level resources.

**6. Prerequisites of an effective federation**
1. **Desire for union without unity** among the units.
2. **Geographical contiguity**: separated territories strain federations, as East and West Pakistan's 1,600 km gap showed.
3. **Common interests**: defence, economy, foreign policy.
4. **No dominating unit**: Wheare warned that one unit able to overawe the others destabilises a federation; Punjab's population share (about 53%) is Pakistan's version of this problem.
5. **Similarity of institutions** and a broadly shared political culture.
6. **Political maturity and a spirit of compromise**.
7. **Adequate financial resources** and fair revenue-sharing (the NFC in Pakistan).
8. **Strong intergovernmental institutions** (Council of Common Interests, National Economic Council).
9. **Independent judiciary** trusted by all units.
10. **Democracy**: federalism cannot work under centralised military rule.
11. **Capable leadership** committed to the federal bargain.

**6a. Types of federation and why federations fail**
Federations differ, and the differences explain success and failure.
- **Coming-together** federations (the USA, Switzerland, Australia) are formed when sovereign units unite and keep strong residual powers; **holding-together** federations (India, Belgium, Spain, Pakistan) are created when a centralised state devolves power to keep diverse regions together (Alfred Stepan's distinction). Holding-together federations tend to have stronger centres.
- **Dual federalism** gives each level separate spheres ("layer cake"); **cooperative federalism** has levels sharing tasks and money ("marble cake"), as in Germany, where the Länder implement most federal laws and sit in the Bundesrat.
- **Asymmetric federalism** gives some units special status (Quebec in Canada; Sabah and Sarawak in Malaysia).

Federations fail when one or more prerequisites collapse:
1. **A dominant unit or group**: the Yugoslav federation broke apart in 1991–92 amid fears of Serbian dominance; Czechoslovakia split peacefully in 1993.
2. **Geographical separation and economic exploitation**: the West Indies Federation (1958–62) and the first Pakistani federation (1947–71).
3. **Authoritarian centralisation**: the Soviet Union was federal on paper and unitary in practice, so when central control loosened, the republics left (1991).
4. **Lack of trusted arbiters**: where courts are seen as partisan, disputes escalate.
5. **Fiscal starvation** of units or of the centre.

Successful federations share features: democratic politics, fair fiscal transfers, strong intergovernmental bodies, a respected constitutional court, and parties that compete across all units. India has survived as a federation despite enormous diversity partly because national parties draw votes from many states and the Finance Commission redistributes revenue every five years; Germany succeeds through the Bundesrat and fiscal equalisation; Switzerland through extensive cantonal autonomy, direct democracy and power-sharing in the Federal Council.

**7. Pakistan's experience**
Pakistan's federation was over-centralised for decades: the Concurrent List, One Unit (1955–70), long military rule, governors' rule and a population-based NFC formula fed provincial grievances and contributed to the break-up of 1971. The 18th Amendment (2010) abolished the Concurrent List, strengthened the CCI and protected provinces' NFC share, while the 7th NFC Award (2009) added poverty, revenue and inverse density to population. Problems remain: weak local government, fiscal imbalance, Balochistan's alienation and the use of governor's rule (Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, October 2026).

**7a. Where does the European Union fit?**
The EU is the most important modern hybrid. Like a confederation, it rests on treaties among sovereign states, members can leave (Article 50, used by the UK in 2020), foreign and defence policy need unanimity, and the EU has no army or general taxing power. Like a federation, its law is supreme and directly effective in member states, its Court of Justice acts as an umpire, its Parliament is directly elected, the Commission acts directly on firms and citizens in competition and trade, and most members (21 from January 2026 [verify]) share a single currency managed by the European Central Bank. Scholars therefore call it *sui generis* or a "confederation with federal features". Its evolution — from a coal-and-steel community to a union with common citizenship — shows that the line between confederation and federation is a spectrum, and that integration can deepen step by step when members trust the institutions.

**8. Conclusion**
Confederations preserve sovereignty at the cost of effectiveness; federations combine effectiveness with diversity. Federations succeed not by constitutional text alone but by trust, fair resources, strong intergovernmental institutions and democracy — the conditions Pakistan's federation still needs to complete.

## Answer 6 — Western and Islamic concepts of sovereignty
*Question (CSS 2024): Sovereignty is the most essential element of statehood. Explain the differences between the Western and Islamic concepts of sovereignty.*

**Outline**
1. Introduction
2. Sovereignty as the essential element of the state
3. The Western concept: development and kinds
4. Austin's monistic theory and its critics
5. The Islamic concept
6. Comparison (table)
7. Points of convergence
8. Pakistan's synthesis
9. Conclusion

**1. Introduction**
Sovereignty is supreme authority within a territory and independence from external control. Without it, an organisation may have people, land and government but is not a state (a province or a colony is not sovereign). Western thought locates sovereignty in a human authority — monarch, parliament or people; Islamic thought locates it in God, with humans exercising delegated authority as a trust. The difference affects law, legitimacy and the limits of power.

**2. The essential element**
Of the four elements of the state (population, territory, government, sovereignty), sovereignty distinguishes the state from other associations. Internally it makes the state's law supreme; externally it underpins legal equality (UN Charter, Art. 2(1)) and non-intervention (Art. 2(7)).

**3. The Western concept**
- **Jean Bodin** (1576) defined sovereignty as supreme power over citizens, unrestrained by law (though bound by divine and natural law).
- **Hobbes** made it absolute and indivisible in the Leviathan.
- **Locke** limited it by natural rights and government by trust.
- **Rousseau** placed it in the people's general will: inalienable and indivisible.
- **Dicey** distinguished **legal sovereignty** (Parliament) from **political sovereignty** (the electorate).
- Modern constitutionalism places sovereignty in the constitution or the people (popular sovereignty), with limits through rights and courts.

**4. Austin and the pluralists**
John Austin (1832) defined the sovereign as a determinate human superior who receives habitual obedience and obeys no one; its commands are law; sovereignty is indivisible and unlimited. Pluralists (Laski, MacIver, Duguit) attacked this: society has many associations; federalism divides power; international law and rights limit states. Laski suggested abandoning the concept. Globalisation and supranational bodies (EU, WTO, IMF conditionality) have further eroded absolute sovereignty in practice.

**5. The Islamic concept**
- **Sovereignty belongs to Allah alone**: "Command belongs only to Allah" (12:40); "Say: O Allah, Owner of sovereignty, You give sovereignty to whom You will" (3:26).
- **Humans are vicegerents** (*khalifa*, 2:30) exercising delegated authority as an *amanah* (trust, 4:58).
- **Legal sovereignty** lies in the shari'a; human legislation operates within its limits through ijtihad.
- **Political authority** belongs to the community (Ummah), exercised through *bay'a* and *shura*; rulers are accountable to God and the people (Abu Bakr: "obey me as long as I obey God").
- **Maududi** called the result "theo-democracy"; **Iqbal** rejected theocracy and proposed ijtihad through an elected assembly.

**6. Comparison**

| Point | Western | Islamic |
|---|---|---|
| Ultimate holder | Monarch, parliament or people | Allah |
| Nature | Absolute (monists) or constitutionally limited | Delegated trust, limited by divine law |
| Source of law | Human will | Revelation + ijtihad |
| Changeability | Any law can change | Core principles fixed; details flexible |
| Accountability | To electorate (democracy) | To God and community |
| Rights | Granted or recognised by state | God-given, inviolable |
| Purpose | Order, will of people | Justice and welfare in both worlds |
| Relation of religion and state | Separated (secular) | Integrated |

**7. Convergence**
Both require a supreme authority to prevent chaos; democratic versions in both traditions root legitimacy in the people's consent; both accept the rule of law and limits on rulers; modern Western constitutionalism, like Islam, denies that any human ruler is absolute.

**7a. The Islamic concept in history and practice**
Islamic thinkers have translated divine sovereignty into different institutional forms:
- **The Rashidun model** (632–661): rulers chosen through consultation and pledge (*bay'a*), personally accountable, living simply, bound by the shari'a. Abu Bakr's inaugural address — "help me if I do right, correct me if I do wrong; obey me as long as I obey God" — is the classic statement of accountable delegated authority.
- **Classical jurists** (al-Mawardi, al-Ghazali) treated the caliph as executor of the shari'a and, under pressure of events, accepted rulers who held real power if they upheld the law.
- **Ibn Taymiyyah** stressed governance as a trust (Quran 4:58–59) and the appointment of the competent and trustworthy, whatever the form.
- **Modern states** show the range: Iran's *velayat-e faqih* places a jurist above elected bodies; Saudi Arabia combines monarchy with the claim that the Quran and Sunnah are its constitution; Pakistan combines divine sovereignty with parliamentary democracy and judicial review.

**7b. Tensions in reconciling the two concepts**
1. **Who interprets the divine law?** Ulama, elected legislators (Iqbal), or courts (the Federal Shariat Court)?
2. **Can a parliament repeal an Islamic provision?** In Pakistan, Article 2A and Art. 227 make this politically and legally fraught.
3. **Rights of non-Muslims and women** under constitutional equality (Art. 25) and Islamic provisions.
4. **External sovereignty**: in both traditions, globalisation, IMF conditionality and international law limit what states can do; Islamic thought has always seen the Ummah as wider than any one state, which sits uneasily with the Westphalian nation-state.
5. **Legitimacy of de facto power**: both Western theory (Austin's habitual obedience) and some classical Islamic writing (amirate by seizure) tend to legitimise whoever holds power, which military regimes in Pakistan exploited through the doctrine of necessity.

Recognising these tensions, rather than presenting the two concepts as simply opposed, earns higher marks.

**8. Pakistan's synthesis**
The Objectives Resolution (1949, now Art. 2A) declares that sovereignty belongs to Allah and that the authority delegated to the state through its people is a sacred trust to be exercised within His limits. The 1973 Constitution then builds a democratic, parliamentary federation in which Parliament legislates but no law may be repugnant to the Quran and Sunnah (Art. 227), with the Council of Islamic Ideology and Federal Shariat Court as mechanisms. Pakistan thus combines divine sovereignty, popular authority and constitutional supremacy, though the tensions among them are a recurring source of debate.

**9. Conclusion**
Western sovereignty answers "who has the final human say?"; Islamic sovereignty answers "whose law is ultimately supreme?". The first stresses the will of the people, the second the will of God mediated by the people. Pakistan's constitutional formula shows that the two can be combined: popular authority exercised as a trust under higher law.

## Answer 7 — Why SAARC could not replicate the EU
*Question (CSS 2025; 2023 variant): Why has SAARC been unable to replicate the success of the European Union despite geographical proximity and cultural ties among member states?*

**Outline**
1. Introduction and thesis
2. Two organisations at a glance
3. How the EU succeeded
4. Why SAARC failed: security, asymmetry, institutions, economics, politics, external factors
5. Comparison table
6. Is SAARC beyond repair? The way forward
7. Conclusion

**1. Introduction**
The European Union grew from six states pooling coal and steel in 1951 into a 27-member union with a single market, common institutions and, for most members, a single currency. The South Asian Association for Regional Cooperation, founded in Dhaka on 8 December 1985 by eight countries sharing history, geography and culture, has not held a summit since 2014. The thesis of this answer is that proximity and culture are not enough: integration needs a political settlement among the main powers, balanced power, strong institutions and economic complementarity — conditions present in post-war Europe and absent in South Asia.

**2. At a glance**
The EU: Schuman Declaration (1950), ECSC (1951), Treaties of Rome (1957), Single European Act (1986), Maastricht (1992), euro (1999), Lisbon (2009); supranational Commission, Parliament and Court; about 60% of members' trade within the bloc.
SAARC: eight members; secretariat in Kathmandu; unanimity; bilateral issues excluded; SAFTA in force since 2006; intra-regional trade about 5% of members' total; the 19th summit (Islamabad, 2016) postponed indefinitely after India withdrew.

**3. How the EU succeeded**
1. **Reconciliation of the main rivals**: France and Germany decided that integration would make war "materially impossible".
2. **External security umbrella**: NATO and the United States removed security competition; the Marshall Plan required cooperation.
3. **Supranational institutions** with real powers and majority voting.
4. **Economic complementarity** and dense intra-industry trade.
5. **Shared democratic values** (later formalised in the Copenhagen criteria).
6. **Visionary leadership** and a gradual, functional strategy ("spillover", in Ernst Haas's term).

**4. Why SAARC failed**
*(a) The India–Pakistan rivalry.* Kashmir, wars (1947–48, 1965, 1971, 1999), terrorism allegations and crises (2016, 2019, May 2025) have made the two largest members unwilling to cooperate. Summits have been hostage to bilateral tensions; India's boycott in 2016 froze the organisation.
*(b) Power asymmetry.* India accounts for about three-quarters of the region's population and GDP and borders every other member except Afghanistan. Smaller states fear domination (the 2015 blockade of Nepal is cited). Europe, by contrast, had several large powers balancing each other.
*(c) Institutional design.* The Charter requires unanimity and bars bilateral and contentious issues — so the disputes that matter most cannot be discussed. The secretariat is small and weak; there is no court or commission.
*(d) Economic structure.* Members export similar goods (textiles, agriculture), keep long "sensitive lists" under SAFTA and impose non-tariff barriers. Poor transport and energy links push trade through Dubai or Singapore. The World Bank has estimated that intra-regional trade could be about three times its actual level.
*(e) Political systems and trust.* Regime types range from democracies to military-dominated and theocratic governments; Afghanistan's Taliban government is largely unrecognised. Historical memories of partition and 1971 divide identities.
*(f) Extra-regional powers.* US, Chinese and Russian rivalries pull members in different directions; India prefers BIMSTEC, which excludes Pakistan; China's Belt and Road (CPEC) is seen by India as hostile.
*(g) Lack of a regional identity.* Nationalism, religion and history divide more than culture unites.

**5. Comparison**

| Factor | EU | SAARC |
|---|---|---|
| Core rivalry | Resolved (Franco-German) | Unresolved (India–Pakistan) |
| Power balance | Balanced | Asymmetric |
| Security umbrella | NATO | None |
| Institutions | Supranational, majority voting | Intergovernmental, unanimity, bilateral issues barred |
| Trade within bloc | About 60% | About 5% |
| Values | Democratic consensus | Mixed regimes |
| External support | Marshall Plan | Competing great powers |

**5a. Lessons from ASEAN and other regions**
The EU is not the only benchmark, and comparing SAARC with ASEAN is often more instructive because ASEAN also unites diverse, developing, sometimes hostile states.
- **ASEAN** (1967) began during the Vietnam War among states with territorial disputes (Malaysia–Philippines over Sabah) and very different regimes. It succeeded through the "**ASEAN way**": consensus, non-interference and informal diplomacy, but also by **putting economics first**: the ASEAN Free Trade Area (1992), the ASEAN Economic Community (2015), and integration into regional production networks led by Japanese and later Chinese investment. Intra-ASEAN trade is about a quarter of members' trade, five times SAARC's share. ASEAN also benefited from the absence of a single dominant member; Indonesia is large but does not overshadow the others as India does.
- **Mercosur** and the **African Union** show that integration can survive political turbulence if there are institutional anchors and economic incentives.
- **ECO** and **SCO**, to which Pakistan belongs, show the limits of organisations without economic complementarity and infrastructure.

Three lessons follow for South Asia. First, integration can begin before political disputes are resolved, provided summits are not cancelled whenever bilateral tension rises; ASEAN never suspended its meetings because of the Sabah dispute. Second, business networks and supply chains create constituencies for integration; South Asia's private sector has little stake in regional trade because of barriers. Third, a large member must reassure smaller ones; India's Gujral Doctrine (1996–97), which offered non-reciprocal concessions to neighbours, was a step in that direction but was not sustained.

The counter-argument is that ASEAN, too, is weak in hard crises (Myanmar since 2021, the South China Sea), so expectations for SAARC should be modest: a functional organisation that facilitates trade, energy and disaster cooperation would already be a large gain.

**6. Way forward**
1. Separate trade and connectivity from disputes (an "economics first" approach, as ASEAN did).
2. Implement SAFTA fully; cut sensitive lists and non-tariff barriers.
3. Build energy grids and transport corridors; sub-regional cooperation (BBIN; links with Central Asia via Pakistan and Afghanistan).
4. Create a dispute-avoidance mechanism and strengthen the secretariat.
5. Use civil society, business chambers and Track-2 diplomacy.
6. Revive the summit process; Bangladesh and Pakistan have spoken of this since 2025–26.
7. India–Pakistan dialogue remains the key: without it, regionalism will stay sub-regional.

**7. Conclusion**
SAARC's failure is not a failure of geography or culture but of politics. The EU began with a political decision by former enemies to bind themselves together under protective institutions; South Asia has never taken that decision. Until India and Pakistan can compartmentalise their disputes, SAARC will remain a forum rather than a union, and the region will keep paying the price of missed trade, energy and connectivity.

## Answer 8 — Checks and balances in the US political system
*Question (CSS 2019, 2021): How does the system of checks and balances work in the US political system? Explain with examples.*

**Outline**
1. Introduction
2. Philosophical origins
3. Constitutional design: separation of powers
4. Checks by each branch (table)
5. Examples in practice
6. Federalism and other checks
7. Evaluation: strengths and weaknesses
8. Current relevance (2025–27)
9. Conclusion

**1. Introduction**
The US Constitution of 1787 divides power among three branches and then gives each the means to restrain the others. James Madison explained the logic in *Federalist No. 51*: "Ambition must be made to counteract ambition." Checks and balances are the mechanism that turns separation of powers into limited government.

**2. Origins**
Montesquieu's *Spirit of the Laws* (1748) argued that liberty requires legislative, executive and judicial powers to be separate and that "power must check power". The framers, fearing both monarchy (George III) and the weak Articles of Confederation, designed a strong but restrained government.

**3. Design**
Article I vests legislative power in Congress (House of 435, Senate of 100); Article II vests executive power in a President elected for four years through the Electoral College; Article III vests judicial power in the Supreme Court. Each branch has a different electorate and term (two, four and six years; life for judges), so no single majority captures all.

**4. Checks by branch**

| Branch | On the executive | On the legislature | On the judiciary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Executive | — | Veto; special sessions | Nominates judges; pardons |
| Legislature | Overrides vetoes (two-thirds); impeachment; Senate confirms appointments and ratifies treaties (two-thirds); power of the purse; oversight; declares war | Bicameralism | Confirms or rejects judges; impeachment; sets jurisdiction; proposes amendments |
| Judiciary | Strikes down executive acts | Strikes down laws (judicial review) | — |

**5. Examples**
1. **Treaty of Versailles**: the Senate refused ratification (1919–20) despite President Wilson.
2. **War Powers Resolution** (1973): Congress overrode Nixon's veto to limit unauthorised wars.
3. ***United States v. Nixon*** (1974): the Supreme Court ordered the Watergate tapes released; Nixon resigned.
4. **Robert Bork**: the Senate rejected Reagan's Supreme Court nominee (1987).
5. **Impeachments**: the House impeached Andrew Johnson (1868), Bill Clinton (1998) and Donald Trump (2019, 2021); the Senate acquitted all — the high bar shows the check is serious but rarely decisive.
6. ***Youngstown Sheet & Tube v. Sawyer*** (1952): the Court stopped Truman's seizure of steel mills.
7. **Power of the purse**: government shutdowns (2013; 2018–19; October–November 2025, the longest).
8. **Tariffs**: in February 2026 the Supreme Court struck down tariffs imposed under emergency economic powers (IEEPA).
9. **Vetoes and overrides**: presidents have vetoed over 2,500 bills; Congress has overridden only about 4% [verify].

**6. Other checks**
Federalism divides power between the national government and 50 states (state attorneys-general sue federal agencies); the Bill of Rights protects citizens; elections every two years let voters create **divided government**; a free press and interest groups scrutinise power.

**7. Evaluation**
*Strengths*: prevents tyranny and hasty legislation; protects minorities; forces compromise; stable for over two centuries.
*Weaknesses*: **gridlock** and shutdowns; slow response to crises; under **unified government** the checks weaken because party loyalty overrides institutional rivalry; the growth of executive power ("imperial presidency"; *Trump v. United States*, 2024, on immunity) tilts the balance; politicised judicial appointments; the filibuster lets a Senate minority block majorities.

**7a. Madison's design and the party problem**
The framers expected each branch to defend its own powers out of institutional ambition. They did not anticipate disciplined national parties. When the same party controls the presidency and Congress, members often defend their party leader rather than their branch, so oversight weakens; when control is divided, the same partisanship produces obstruction. Political scientists such as Daryl Levinson and Richard Pildes have described this as "separation of parties, not powers". This is why the 2026 midterm result matters: it reactivates Congress's oversight and budget powers against the President. It also explains why the Supreme Court's role has grown — when Congress is gridlocked, courts become the main arena for settling disputes over executive power, from immigration to tariffs.

**8. Current relevance**
From January 2025 Republicans controlled the presidency and both houses; the Supreme Court was the main check (for example on tariffs). The **3 November 2026 midterms** gave Democrats both the House and the Senate, so from January 2027 the system returns to divided government: oversight hearings, budget battles and possible confrontation will test the checks again.

**8a. Comparison with Pakistan**
Pakistan's 1973 Constitution also separates powers, but its checks work differently because it is a parliamentary system.

| Check | United States | Pakistan |
|---|---|---|
| Executive–legislature | Separate election; veto and override; no confidence vote | Fusion: PM depends on National Assembly confidence (Art. 91, 95); dissolution possible (Art. 58) |
| Appointments | Senate confirms judges, cabinet, ambassadors | Judicial Commission and (since the 26th Amendment) a Special Parliamentary Committee for the Chief Justice; cabinet needs no confirmation |
| Upper house | Co-equal Senate | Senate cannot vote on money bills (Art. 73) |
| Judicial review | Supreme Court strikes down laws and orders | Supreme Court and High Courts; since November 2025 the Federal Constitutional Court decides constitutional cases |
| Removal of executive | Impeachment (two-thirds Senate) | Vote of no confidence (first successful: April 2022) |
| Federal check | 50 states, residual powers | Provinces with residual powers since the 18th Amendment; CCI; Senate |
| Unelected power | Civilian control of the military firmly established | Military influence outside constitutional checks |

The comparison shows that formal checks matter less than political conditions. In the US, institutional rivalry, competitive parties and an independent judiciary make the checks real; in Pakistan, the weak autonomy of Parliament (party discipline under Art. 63-A), the shifting independence of the judiciary (from the doctrine of necessity to activism to the 26th and 27th Amendments) and the military's role mean that checks often fail or are bypassed. A fair conclusion is that Pakistan has the architecture of checks and balances but not yet the political culture that sustains them.

**9. Conclusion**
Checks and balances make American government deliberately slow and contested. They have prevented any branch from dominating permanently, but their effectiveness depends on party competition and on each branch defending its constitutional role. For Pakistan, the lesson is that separation of powers works only when courts and legislatures are independent enough to say no.

## Answer 9 — Will the 18th Amendment end federal tension once and for all?
*Question (CSS 2025): Federalism has been a continuing cause of political tension in our country. Will the 18th Amendment solve this issue once and for all? Take a position.*

**Outline**
1. Introduction and position
2. Roots of federal tension in Pakistan
3. What the 18th Amendment changed
4. Achievements
5. Why tension persists: fiscal, political, administrative, ethnic, civil–military
6. Evidence from 2024–26
7. Way forward
8. Conclusion

**1. Introduction**
My position is that the 18th Amendment (April 2010) was the most important correction in Pakistan's federal history, but it cannot end federal tension "once and for all". It changed the legal structure of the federation; the deeper sources of tension — fiscal imbalance, ethnic grievance, political rivalry and civil–military centralisation — lie outside the constitutional text. Tension is also normal in federations; the aim is to manage it.

**2. Roots of tension**
- Over-centralisation in every constitution and long military rule.
- East–West disparity, parity and One Unit, which ended in 1971.
- Punjab's demographic and institutional weight.
- A Concurrent List that let the centre override provinces.
- Resource disputes: water, gas royalties, NFC formulas based almost only on population.
- Dismissal of provincial governments; Baloch insurgencies (1973–77; since 2004–06).

**3. What the 18th Amendment changed**
Passed unanimously after an all-party committee led by Raza Rabbani, it: abolished the Concurrent List (47 subjects devolved); devolved about 17 ministries; strengthened the Council of Common Interests (PM chairs; meetings every 90 days; secretariat); guaranteed that provinces' NFC share cannot fall (Art. 160(3A)); made oil and gas jointly owned (Art. 172(3)); allowed provincial borrowing and sales tax on services; required elected local government (Art. 140A); and restored parliamentary democracy by removing Art. 58(2)(b).

**4. Achievements**
- Answered the smaller provinces' main demands of four decades; Rabbani called it a new social contract.
- Provincial budgets and capacity expanded; provinces now run health, education and social policy.
- The CCI meets more regularly; disputes go to institutions rather than streets.
- It created a precedent of consensus amendment.

**5. Why tension persists**
1. **Fiscal federalism unsettled**: the 7th NFC Award (2009) gives provinces 57.5% of the divisible pool; no new award has been agreed since, and the federal government — carrying debt service and defence — runs large deficits. The IMF and the centre push provinces to raise their own taxes or share expenditures, while provinces defend the floor.
2. **The missing third tier**: provinces have not devolved power to districts; Punjab had no elected councils from 2019 until polls planned for 2027. "Provincial centralisation" replaced federal centralisation.
3. **Political rivalry**: when rival parties govern the centre and a province, conflicts escalate (PTI-ruled Khyber Pakhtunkhwa versus the federal government).
4. **Balochistan**: grievances over resources (Reko Diq, gas), development (Gwadar, CPEC), missing persons and security persist; insurgent violence continues.
5. **Water**: Sindh–Punjab disputes, illustrated by the Cholistan canals controversy halted after the CCI's 2025 decision.
6. **Re-centralisation**: federal ministries re-created under new names; curriculum disputes; calls to "revisit" the 18th Amendment.
7. **Civil–military centralisation**: security and major economic decisions (CPEC, the Special Investment Facilitation Council) are taken outside the federal bargain.
8. **Demands for new provinces** (South Punjab, Hazara, Karachi) challenge the four-province structure.

**5a. The counter-arguments**
A fair answer considers two opposing views.
*(a) "The 18th Amendment has already largely solved the problem."* Supporters point out that no province now seriously questions the federation's legitimacy as East Pakistan did in 1970–71; disputes are fought in the CCI, courts and NFC talks rather than through secession; smaller provinces' representatives (from the PPP, ANP, Baloch and Pashtun nationalist parties) helped draft and still defend the amendment; and provincial governments have taken real ownership of health, education and social protection. On this view, remaining frictions are the ordinary politics of any federation, not a structural crisis.

*(b) "The 18th Amendment went too far."* Critics in federal circles argue that it weakened the centre's fiscal capacity just as debt and defence costs rose; that provinces lacked capacity for devolved subjects; that national standards in education and health fragmented; and that the NFC floor locks the federation into deficits, contributing to repeated IMF programmes. Some propose returning education or population planning to the centre, revising the NFC formula, or creating new provinces.

My judgement is that both views capture part of the truth but miss the central point. The 18th Amendment did not go "too far" in autonomy; it went **not far enough in accountability and fiscal responsibility**. It gave provinces money and powers without matching duties to raise their own revenue or devolve to local governments, and it left the federal government without a mechanism to share debt. Reversing it would reopen old wounds; completing it — through a new NFC, a stronger CCI and constitutional protection for local government — is the way to reduce tension.

**6. Evidence from 2024–26**
The federal government imposed **governor's rule and an emergency in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa on 11 October 2026** after the chief minister announced a march on Islamabad; reported drafts of a 28th Amendment touch NFC shares, education and local government; AJK and Balochistan saw unrest. These show that the centre's intervention tools — and provinces' suspicion — remain.

**7. Way forward**
1. A new NFC award rewarding provincial tax effort and sharing debt-servicing fairly.
2. Regular CCI meetings with a strong secretariat and published decisions.
3. Amend Art. 140A to guarantee local government terms, elections and funds.
4. Political dialogue in Balochistan; end enforced disappearances; local share in resource projects.
5. National standards with provincial delivery in education and health.
6. Restraint in the use of Art. 232–234 emergency powers.
7. A transparent, agreed census.

**8. Conclusion**
The 18th Amendment settled the legal question of provincial autonomy; it did not settle the political economy of federalism. Federal tension will continue as long as money is scarce, institutions are weak and power is centralised outside Parliament. The Amendment is a foundation, not a final settlement: its promise will be realised only through fiscal reform, local democracy and political trust.

## Answer 10 — The constitutions of 1956, 1962 and 1973 compared
*Question (CSS 2017, 2021): Discuss the major issues of the constitution-making process in Pakistan in 1956, 1962 and 1973. (Also prepares the 2020 comparison question.)*

**Outline**
1. Introduction
2. 1947–56: the long road to the first constitution
3. The 1956 Constitution: issues and features
4. 1958–62: Ayub and the 1962 Constitution
5. 1970–73: the making of the 1973 Constitution
6. Comparative table
7. Lessons
8. Conclusion

**1. Introduction**
Pakistan framed three constitutions in seventeen years. Each was shaped by the same unresolved issues — the balance between centre and provinces, the representation of the two wings, the role of Islam, the form of government and the power of unelected institutions — and each answered them differently. Only the 1973 Constitution, made by elected representatives through consensus, has endured.

**2. 1947–56**
Pakistan began with the adapted Government of India Act 1935. The First Constituent Assembly passed the Objectives Resolution (12 March 1949) and set up the Basic Principles Committee, but its interim report (1950) was rejected in East Bengal over Urdu as the sole national language. Liaquat's assassination (1951), Ghulam Muhammad's dismissal of PM Nazimuddin (1953) and the dissolution of the Assembly (24 October 1954), endorsed by the Federal Court in the *Tamizuddin Khan* case (1955), delayed the process. The Bogra Formula (1953) and the Murree Pact (1955) offered compromises; One Unit merged West Pakistan's provinces in 1955.

**3. 1956: issues and features**
*Issues*: representation of East Pakistan's majority (resolved by **parity**); language (Urdu and Bengali both national languages); the Islamic character (Islamic Republic; directive principles; no law against Islam); electorates (left for later); the President's powers.
*Features*: federal, parliamentary; unicameral National Assembly of 300 split equally; President elected by national and provincial assemblies; fundamental rights; Supreme Court.
*Outcome*: no election was ever held under it; Iskander Mirza dismissed governments at will; it was abrogated on **7 October 1958**.

**4. 1962: issues and features**
Ayub Khan diagnosed parliamentary politics as the problem. The Shahabuddin Commission recommended a presidential system with direct elections and safeguards; Ayub kept the presidential form and dropped the safeguards.
*Issues*: concentration of power; indirect election through 80,000 Basic Democrats; non-justiciable rights (until 1963); unicameral legislature with weak powers; centre able to legislate on anything in the national interest; East Pakistan's alienation.
*Outcome*: stability and growth for a time, but mass protest in 1968–69; abrogated on **25 March 1969** by Yahya Khan.

**5. 1973: issues and features**
After the 1970 election and the separation of East Pakistan, the National Assembly drafted a constitution through an all-party committee and the constitutional accord of 20 October 1972.
*Issues resolved*: form of government (parliamentary, with a ceremonial President); federalism (bicameralism with a Senate of equal provincial representation; CCI, NEC, NFC); Islam (state religion, Council of Islamic Ideology, Art. 227); rights (justiciable); language (Urdu); amendment (two-thirds of each house); Art. 6 against subversion.
*Outcome*: passed without dissent (10 April 1973), in force 14 August 1973; suspended in 1977–85 and 1999–2002 but restored each time; distorted by the 8th and 17th Amendments; restored by the 13th and 18th; debated again after the 26th (2024) and 27th (2025) Amendments.

**6. Comparison**

| Point | 1956 | 1962 | 1973 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Framer | Second Constituent Assembly | Ayub Khan | Elected National Assembly by consensus |
| Form | Federal, parliamentary | Presidential, centralised | Federal, parliamentary |
| Legislature | Unicameral (parity) | Unicameral (indirect) | Bicameral (Senate) |
| Head of state | President with wide discretion | All-powerful President | Ceremonial President (after 18th) |
| Islam | Islamic Republic; directive principles | "Republic of Pakistan" until 1963 | Extensive provisions; FSC (1980) |
| Rights | Justiciable | Non-justiciable at first | Justiciable, expanded |
| Fate | 31 months | 7 years | In force for over five decades |

**6a. Critical assessment of each constitution**
*1956*: Its central flaw was **parity**, which treated East Pakistan's 55% majority as equal to the West's 45% and was combined with **One Unit**, which offended Sindhis, Pashtuns and Baloch. The President retained wide discretion over appointing and dismissing prime ministers; Iskander Mirza changed four prime ministers between 1956 and 1958. Elections were repeatedly postponed, so the constitution was never legitimised by a popular vote. Its supporters credit it with Islamic and democratic features and with recognising Bengali as a national language; its critics see it as a compromise among elites that excluded the masses.

*1962*: It was described as a constitution "of one man, by one man, for one man". The President could appoint ministers outside the legislature, issue ordinances, veto bills, and was elected by Basic Democrats who depended on bureaucratic patronage. Fundamental rights were initially non-justiciable. The centre could legislate on any subject in the national interest. Economic growth under Ayub concentrated wealth (the "22 families"), and East Pakistan's share of power and resources remained low, fuelling the Six Points (1966). When Ayub fell, the system fell.

*1973*: Its strength is consensus and durability, but it has been heavily amended. Early amendments under Bhutto (3rd to 6th) curbed courts and liberties; Zia's 8th Amendment created a semi-presidential system through Article 58(2)(b) and inserted Islamic provisions; Musharraf's 17th repeated the pattern. The 13th (1997) and 18th (2010) Amendments restored parliamentary and federal character. Since 2024 the 26th and 27th Amendments have restructured the judiciary and the military command, which supporters call parliamentary supremacy and critics call executive dominance. The 1973 text therefore shows both the resilience of a consensual constitution and its vulnerability to whoever controls a two-thirds majority or holds power outside it.

**7. Lessons**
1. Constitutions made by consensus last; those imposed by individuals fall with their makers.
2. Federal fairness is existential: denial of East Pakistan's majority and autonomy was a root cause of 1971.
3. Unelected institutions — governors-general, presidents, the army and accommodating courts — were the main obstacles to constitutionalism.
4. Pakistan has oscillated between parliamentary and presidential tendencies; experience has pushed it back to parliamentary federalism each time.

**8. Conclusion**
The issues of 1956, 1962 and 1973 were the same; the answers differed in legitimacy. The 1973 Constitution survived because it was the product of elected consensus, and its future depends on preserving that consensus rather than amending it by narrow majorities.

## Answer 11 — Leadership structures of Iran and Turkey and policy formulation
*Question (CSS 2025): Compare the leadership structures of Iran and Turkey and their impact on policy formulation.*

**Outline**
1. Introduction
2. Iran: structure of leadership
3. Turkey: structure of leadership
4. Comparison table
5. Impact on policy formulation: Iran
6. Impact on policy formulation: Turkey
7. Similarities and conclusions for theory
8. Conclusion

**1. Introduction**
Iran and Turkey are Muslim-majority regional powers with strong executives, but their leadership is built on different principles. Iran's Islamic Republic places an unelected jurist above elected institutions; Turkey, since 2018, concentrates power in an elected executive president. The result is two different policy styles: layered continuity in Tehran and personalised flexibility in Ankara.

**2. Iran**
- **Supreme Leader**: chosen by the 88-member Assembly of Experts; commander-in-chief; sets general policies; appoints the judiciary chief, half the Guardian Council, IRGC and military commanders, and state media chiefs; can dismiss the President (Art. 110). Ali Khamenei held the office from 1989 until he was killed in US–Israeli strikes on 28 February 2026; **Mojtaba Khamenei** was named on 8 March 2026.
- **President**: elected for four years (Masoud Pezeshkian since 2024); runs the government but not the armed forces or strategic policy.
- **Majlis**: 290 members, unicameral; legislation subject to the **Guardian Council**, which also vets all candidates.
- **Expediency Council**, **Supreme National Security Council**, **judiciary**, and the **IRGC** as a political and economic power.

**3. Turkey**
- **President**: directly elected for five years; since the 2017 referendum and 2018 elections, head of state and government; appoints vice presidents and ministers without parliamentary approval; issues decrees; prepares the budget; appoints many senior judges; can dissolve parliament (triggering joint elections). Recep Tayyip Erdoğan has held the office since 2014.
- **Grand National Assembly**: 600 members elected by PR with a 7% threshold; lost confidence votes over the executive.
- **Judiciary**: Constitutional Court, Council of Judges and Prosecutors (partly presidential appointees).
- **Military**: brought fully under civilian (presidential) control after the failed coup of 15 July 2016.

**4. Comparison**

| Point | Iran | Turkey |
|---|---|---|
| Basis of legitimacy | Religious (*velayat-e faqih*) + elections | Elections + nationalism |
| Top authority | Unelected Supreme Leader (life tenure) | Elected President (five years) |
| Structure | Dual: elected and appointed institutions | Single, centralised executive |
| Electoral filter | Guardian Council vets candidates | Open contests on an uneven field |
| Military | IRGC loyal to the Leader | Under presidential control |
| Legislature | Checked by Guardian Council | Weakened since 2018 |
| Accountability | Leader not answerable to voters | President answerable at elections |

**5. Policy in Iran**
Policy emerges from **multiple veto players** — the Leader's office, the IRGC, the Guardian Council, factions of principlists and reformists, and the elected government. Strategic choices (the nuclear programme, the regional "axis of resistance", relations with the United States) are reserved for the Leader and the Supreme National Security Council. Presidents can change tone and tactics — Rouhani negotiated the JCPOA (2015), Pezeshkian sought outreach — but cannot override the Leader. The result is **ideological continuity** and slow, contested change; elected officials can be overruled. In 2026, war and succession concentrated power further in security institutions, and the new Leader's absence from public view complicated communication with the elected government.

**6. Policy in Turkey**
Policy is **centralised and personalised**: Erdoğan and a small circle decide quickly, which allows sharp turns — reconciliation with Gulf states, Egypt and Israel in 2021–22, then rupture with Israel over Gaza; mediation between Russia and Ukraine (the Black Sea Grain Initiative); a decisive role in post-Assad Syria from December 2024; a Kurdish peace process after the PKK's dissolution announcement (2025). The weakness is **unpredictability** and weak institutional checks: monetary policy swung with the President's views (inflation reached about 85% in 2022), and opposition voices are constrained (the jailing of Istanbul's mayor in 2025).

**6a. Illustrations of the two policy styles**
*Nuclear and regional policy in Iran.* The JCPOA (2015) was negotiated by President Rouhani's team but required the Supreme Leader's approval and was constrained by his "red lines"; after the US withdrawal (2018), hardliners in the Majlis passed a law (2020) expanding enrichment, overriding the government's preference for diplomacy. Regional policy — support for Hezbollah, the Houthis and Iraqi militias — has been directed by the IRGC's Quds Force under the Leader, largely regardless of who is president. This shows how Iran's dual structure produces continuity in strategic goals even when elected leaders change.

*Foreign and economic policy in Turkey.* Erdoğan's personal control allowed Turkey to buy Russian S-400 missiles (2019) against NATO allies' objections, mediate the Black Sea Grain Initiative (2022), and then shift towards rapprochement with Gulf states and Egypt. In economics, his unorthodox view that high interest rates cause inflation led to repeated central-bank governor dismissals and rate cuts in 2021–22, contributing to a currency collapse, before a reversal in 2023. The same centralisation that permits bold diplomacy also permits costly policy errors with few institutional brakes.

*Implications for Pakistan.* Iran's structure means that agreements with Tehran (on border security, the gas pipeline, or mediation in the 2026 war) need the Leader's office and the IRGC on board, not just the president. Turkey's structure means that relations with Ankara — defence industry cooperation, the 2026 Makkah defence agreement with Saudi Arabia and Türkiye — depend heavily on personal rapport at the top. Understanding leadership structures is therefore practical diplomacy, not only comparative politics.

**7. Similarities and theory**
Both systems concentrate authority, mix religion and nationalism in legitimacy, and limit pluralism (Iran far more). In Juan Linz's terms Iran is a hybrid theocratic regime with limited pluralism; Turkey is what Levitsky and Way call competitive authoritarianism — elections are real (the opposition won the 2019 and 2024 local elections) but unfair.

**8. Conclusion**
Iran's leadership structure produces policy through an unelected apex and many veto players, giving continuity at the cost of flexibility and accountability. Turkey's produces policy through one elected leader, giving speed at the cost of predictability and checks. For Pakistan, which deals with both, the lesson is to engage Tehran's institutions patiently and Ankara's leader directly.

## Answer 12 — National interest and foreign policy: Pakistan as a case study
*Question (CSS 2026): Discuss how national interest shapes a country's foreign policy, using Pakistan as a case study.*

**Outline**
1. Introduction: national interest as the compass
2. Theories of national interest
3. Pakistan's core national interests
4. Case study: how interests shaped policy (security, economy, great powers, ideology)
5. When interests clash
6. Constraints: capacity and process
7. Assessment
8. Conclusion

**1. Introduction**
National interest — a state's goals of survival, security, prosperity, identity and prestige — is the compass of foreign policy. Hans Morgenthau defined it as "interest defined in terms of power". Pakistan's foreign policy since 1947 is a clear example: alliances, rivalries and partnerships have tracked its perception of security against India and its economic needs, filtered through ideology and domestic politics.

**2. Theories**
- **Realism** (Morgenthau, Waltz): interest is defined by power and survival in an anarchic system.
- **Liberalism**: interests include trade, institutions and democracy.
- **Constructivism** (Wendt): interests are shaped by identity and ideas.
Pakistan's behaviour is mostly realist, with constructivist elements (Islamic identity).

**3. Pakistan's core interests**
Security and territorial integrity; Kashmir; nuclear deterrence; economic survival (finance, trade, energy); Muslim solidarity; strategic partnerships; regional connectivity; counter-terrorism; international standing.

**4. Case study**
*(a) Security against India.* Within seven years of independence Pakistan joined the US-led **SEATO** (1954) and **CENTO** (1955) and signed a Mutual Defence Agreement (1954) to balance India. When the US embargoed arms in 1965, Pakistan turned to **China** (boundary agreement 1963), building an "all-weather" partnership. India's 1974 test drove Pakistan's nuclear programme, tested in May 1998. In May 2025 deterrence and Chinese-supplied systems shaped Pakistan's response to India.
*(b) The western frontier.* Fear of an India–Afghanistan pincer drove support for the Afghan mujahideen (1980s) and the Taliban (1990s) — the "strategic depth" doctrine. After 2021, the TTP's sanctuaries reversed this: Pakistan's interest now demands pressure on Kabul, including the strikes of October 2025 and February 2026, alongside ceasefire diplomacy in Doha and Istanbul.
*(c) Economic survival.* Pakistan's dependence on external finance explains its 24–25 IMF programmes (the current $7bn EFF since 2024), reliance on Saudi and Emirati deposits, the Saudi–Pakistan Strategic Mutual Defence Agreement (September 2025) and the 2025 trade deal with the US (19% tariff). CPEC (from 2015) serves both economic and strategic interests.
*(d) Great-power balancing.* Pakistan facilitated the US opening to China (1971), served as a frontline state for the US in 1979–89 and after 2001, and now balances: deep ties with China and a warming relationship with Washington (2025–26), while engaging Russia and the Gulf.
*(e) Ideology and prestige.* Non-recognition of Israel, support for Palestine, the Lahore Islamic Summit (1974), OIC activism, and mediation between the US and Iran in 2026 reflect identity and the interest in status.

**4a. A constructivist reading**
A purely realist account misses how identity shapes what Pakistan counts as its interest. Because the state was founded on the Two-Nation Theory, Kashmir is seen not only as territory but as an unfinished part of national identity; because Pakistan sees itself as a leading Muslim state, solidarity with Palestine and the OIC is treated as an interest, not a luxury; and the long rivalry with India has made deterrence part of national self-image. Identity also explains continuity: governments of very different ideologies — Bhutto's socialism, Zia's Islamism, Musharraf's "enlightened moderation", Imran Khan's populism — kept the same core positions on India, China and Kashmir. Interests, in short, are partly material and partly constructed by history and ideas.

**5. When interests clash**
Interests often override ideology: Pakistan stays silent on Xinjiang to protect the China partnership; it declined to join the Saudi-led war in Yemen (2015) to avoid sectarian spillover and protect relations with Iran; it allied with the US despite public anti-Americanism. But ideology sets red lines (no recognition of Israel). Short-term interests have also produced long-term costs: the Afghan jihad's legacy of militancy and weapons.

**6. Constraints**
- **National capacity**: a weak economy (debt about 68% of GDP; interest over 42% of federal spending) and low human development limit independence.
- **Process**: decisions are concentrated in a civil–military elite; parliament's oversight is weak; changes of government interrupt continuity.
- **External structure**: US–China rivalry, India's ties with the US, IMF conditionality.

**6a. How interests rank: goals towards India, China and the United States**
Pakistan's current goals show its hierarchy of interests in practice.
- **India**: security first — deterrence and crisis stability after May 2025; then Kashmir (keeping it on the international agenda through the UN, OIC and SCO); then water (using the August 2026 Court of Arbitration award against India's holding of the Indus Waters Treaty "in abeyance"); trade and normalisation come last, conditional on progress on core issues.
- **China**: strategic and economic interests coincide — CPEC Phase II (from 2025), defence modernisation, diplomatic backing at the UN, and security for Chinese nationals, whose protection has become a test of Pakistan's reliability.
- **United States**: economic and security interests — market access (the US is Pakistan's largest export destination), investment in minerals and energy, counter-terrorism cooperation, influence at the IMF — balanced against the China partnership so that Pakistan is not forced into a bloc.

**6b. Recommendations for an interest-based policy**
1. Put **economic security** at the centre: export growth, regional trade, energy diversification.
2. **Stabilise the western border** through a mix of pressure on the TTP and engagement with Kabul.
3. **Manage India** through deterrence plus back-channel dialogue to reduce war risk.
4. **Balance great powers**: deepen China ties while widening the US relationship beyond security.
5. **Institutionalise** foreign policy-making through the National Security Committee and parliamentary oversight, so that interests are defined consistently across governments.

**7. Assessment**
Pakistan's foreign policy has secured survival and deterrence and built durable partnerships with China and the Gulf. It has been less successful in turning geography into prosperity, resolving disputes with India and Afghanistan, or avoiding over-dependence on patrons. Defining interest mainly in security terms has sometimes undermined economic interest.

**8. Conclusion**
Pakistan confirms the realist proposition that national interest shapes foreign policy: alliances followed threats, and partnerships followed needs. The task now is to redefine that interest to give economic security equal weight with military security, as the National Security Policy (2022) proposed — because in the long run, prosperity is the foundation of security.
