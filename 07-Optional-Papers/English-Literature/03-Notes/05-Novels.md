# 5. Novels (syllabus component V, 20 marks)

About 1.2 questions per paper. *Sons and Lovers* has been asked six times in ten years; it is the single most important long text. Hawthorne and Faulkner are an either/or option: past papers have only ever asked Hawthorne, so choose *The Scarlet Letter*.

Reading time: novels take most of your reading hours. Read *Sons and Lovers* and *The Scarlet Letter* in full. For the others, read in full if time allows; otherwise use the summaries below plus key chapters (listed for each).

Copyright note: Hardy, Lawrence (1913), Joyce (1916), Hawthorne and Faulkner (1929, public domain in the US since 2025) are public domain; short quotations given. Orwell's *1984* is public domain in Pakistan but not the US; Murdoch is in copyright: paraphrase only.

## 5.1 Thomas Hardy, *Far from the Madding Crowd* (1874)

Asked: 2022 (tragic aspects, with *Sons and Lovers*). MCQ 2026.

### Context
- Hardy (1840–1928): Dorset stonemason's son, trained as an architect; novelist (1871–95), then poet. First great success; serialised anonymously in the *Cornhill Magazine* (editor Leslie Stephen, Virginia Woolf's father). First use of the name **Wessex**.
- Title from Thomas Gray's "Elegy Written in a Country Churchyard" (1751): country people living "far from the madding crowd's ignoble strife". The title is ironic: passion and violence come to the country too.
- Rural Dorset in the 1860s–70s: farming, sheep-shearing, harvest suppers, the workhouse, the coming of change.

### Plot (own words)
1. **Gabriel Oak**, a young, steady shepherd-farmer, proposes to the proud, beautiful **Bathsheba Everdene**, who refuses him. His inexperienced dog drives his whole flock over a cliff; ruined, he seeks work.
2. Bathsheba inherits her uncle's farm at **Weatherbury** and decides to run it herself, unusual for a woman. Gabriel saves her ricks from fire and becomes her shepherd.
3. As a joke, Bathsheba sends a valentine sealed "Marry Me" to her neighbour **William Boldwood**, a serious, reserved gentleman-farmer. He falls into an obsessive passion and presses her to marry him.
4. **Fanny Robin**, a servant girl, has been seduced by **Sergeant Francis Troy**; their wedding fails when she goes to the wrong church (All Saints vs All Souls) and Troy, humiliated, abandons her.
5. Troy dazzles Bathsheba with flattery and a famous **sword exercise** in a hollow among the ferns; she marries him secretly in Bath. Troy gambles, drinks, neglects the farm (Gabriel saves the ricks in a storm while Troy and the workers lie drunk).
6. Fanny dies in childbirth in the Casterbridge workhouse. Her coffin is brought to Bathsheba's house; Bathsheba opens it and finds Fanny with Troy's baby. Troy kisses the dead Fanny and tells his wife that Fanny was more to him than she will ever be. He plants flowers on Fanny's grave; rain from a gargoyle washes them away. He goes swimming, is swept out to sea and is presumed drowned.
7. Boldwood renews his suit; Bathsheba half-promises to marry him after a set period. At Boldwood's Christmas party Troy returns to claim his wife; Boldwood shoots him dead. Boldwood is found insane; his death sentence is commuted to imprisonment.
8. Gabriel, about to emigrate to California, is persuaded to stay; Bathsheba and Gabriel marry quietly, in a love based on long friendship and shared work ("camaraderie").

### Characters
- **Bathsheba**: independent, vain, impulsive, brave; learns through suffering. One of Hardy's first strong heroines.
- **Gabriel Oak**: patient, skilled, loyal, close to nature: the moral centre (his name: angel; oak).
- **Boldwood**: repressed, then obsessive: love as madness.
- **Troy**: charming, reckless, selfish, but capable of real feeling (for Fanny).
- **Fanny Robin**: the victim of class and sexual double standards.
- **The rustics** (Joseph Poorgrass, Jan Coggan, etc.): a comic chorus.

### Themes
1. **Kinds of love**: steady (Gabriel), obsessive (Boldwood), sensual and selfish (Troy), vain and impulsive (early Bathsheba). Mature love grows from shared work.
2. **Women's independence**: a woman farmer in a man's world; her mistakes come from inexperience and social pressure.
3. **Nature and rural life**: the cycle of the farming year; man in harmony with nature (Gabriel reading the stars and weather).
4. **Chance and fate**: the wrong church; the valentine; the storm; Troy's return.
5. **Class and the double standard**: Fanny's fate vs Troy's freedom.

### Tragic aspects (2022)
- Though the novel ends happily, it contains tragedy: Fanny's death in the workhouse; Boldwood's madness and imprisonment; Troy's death; the near-ruin of Bathsheba. Tragedy here comes from chance, passion and social injustice. It anticipates Hardy's later tragic novels (*Tess*, *Jude*).
- **Comparison with *Sons and Lovers***: both show love destroyed by forces inside and outside the self; Hardy stresses chance and nature, Lawrence the psyche and family; both have a heroine or hero who survives (Bathsheba, Paul).

### Critical views
- **Henry James** (review, 1874): harsh; the book is long and artificial; the only things he believed in were the sheep and the dogs [paraphrase].
- **D.H. Lawrence** (*Study of Thomas Hardy*, 1914): Hardy's characters break out of convention and are destroyed by the community; a good link for comparison questions.
- **Raymond Williams** (*The English Novel from Dickens to Lawrence*, 1970): Hardy is not a writer of timeless peasants; he shows a changing rural society.
- **Penny Boumelha** (*Thomas Hardy and Women*, 1982): feminist reading of Hardy's women.

## 5.2 D.H. Lawrence, *Sons and Lovers* (1913)

Asked: 2017 (autobiography; Paul's self-realisation), 2018 (libidinal patterns; reception), 2022 (tragic aspects), 2023 (Paul's conflicts and his mother), 2025 (desire vs duty), 2026 ("Two-in-One"). **Learn this text best of all.**

### Context
- Lawrence (1885–1930): son of a coal miner (Arthur Lawrence) and a former teacher (Lydia Beardsall) in Eastwood, Nottinghamshire. Scholarship boy; teacher; TB. Eloped with Frieda Weekley (1912). Censorship (*The Rainbow* banned 1915; *Lady Chatterley's Lover*, 1928, tried in 1960).
- **Autobiographical**: Paul Morel = Lawrence; Mrs Morel = his mother (died of cancer, December 1910); Miriam = Jessie Chambers; Clara = partly Alice Dax and Louie Burrows; Bestwood = Eastwood; William = his brother Ernest (died 1901).
- **Lawrence's letter to Edward Garnett** (14 November 1912) gives the plan: a woman of character and refinement marries beneath her; she has no satisfaction in her husband and turns her passion to her sons; when they grow up and choose lovers, her hold on them prevents them from loving fully; William dies; Paul is torn; when the mother dies, he is left with "the drift towards death". He called it "a great tragedy" and the tragedy of thousands of young men in England.
- Garnett cut about a tenth of the manuscript; the full text (Cambridge edition) was published in 1992.

### Plot (own words)
**Part I**
1. **Gertrude Coppard**, refined, puritan, educated, marries **Walter Morel**, a handsome, warm, dancing miner. She soon finds he drinks, lies about money, and is uneducated; the marriage becomes bitter. In one scene he throws her out of the house at night while pregnant with Paul; she stands in the moonlit garden among the lilies.
2. Children: **William**, **Annie**, **Paul**, **Arthur**. Mrs Morel turns her love and ambition to her sons, and the children side with her against their father, who is pushed out of his own family.
3. William, clever and successful, goes to London, gets engaged to a shallow girl, **Lily ("Gipsy") Western**, and dies of pneumonia and erysipelas. Mrs Morel is numb with grief until Paul falls dangerously ill with pneumonia; nursing him, she turns her life to him.

**Part II**
4. Paul works as a clerk at Jordan's surgical-appliance factory in Nottingham and paints. He meets **Miriam Leivers** at Willey Farm: intense, religious, poetic, shy. Their love is spiritual and intellectual; physically she is afraid, and he resents what he feels as her wish to possess his soul. Mrs Morel hates Miriam, who, she thinks, wants to absorb him and leave no room for her.
5. Paul becomes involved with **Clara Dawes**, a sensual, independent suffragette separated from her husband **Baxter Dawes**, a smith at Jordan's. Their passion is physical; Paul feels he cannot give her his whole self. Baxter beats Paul up in a fight. Paul sleeps with Miriam once, out of a sense of obligation; it is a failure.
6. Mrs Morel develops cancer. Paul and Annie, unable to bear her suffering, give her an overdose of morphia. She dies.
7. Paul helps Baxter (ill in hospital) and Clara reunite. He breaks finally with Miriam, who offers to marry him; he refuses.
8. **"Derelict"** (final chapter): Paul, alone at night, feels the immensity of darkness and wants to follow his mother into death. Then he turns away, towards the "faintly humming, glowing town", walking quickly. The ending is open: survival or the start of a new life.

### Characters
- **Paul Morel**: sensitive, artistic, divided between body and spirit, mother and lovers; at once victim and selfish.
- **Mrs Morel**: strong, proud, moral, ambitious, possessive; her love is both sustaining and crippling.
- **Walter Morel**: vital, warm, physical, violent when drunk; increasingly isolated; Lawrence later regretted portraying him so harshly.
- **Miriam**: soul, intensity, religious feeling; Paul's "spiritual" love. (Jessie Chambers protested that the portrait was unfair.)
- **Clara**: body, independence, passion; Paul's "sensual" love; returns to her husband.
- **William**: the first son destroyed by the split between mother and lover.

### Themes
1. **The mother–son bond and its cost**: love that becomes possession.
2. **The divided self**: spirit (Miriam) vs body (Clara); Paul cannot unite them.
3. **Class and industrialism**: mining life, the pit, the mother's wish to lift her sons above the father's class.
4. **Nature and vitality**: flowers, moonlight, the countryside as the place of feeling.
5. **Marriage and gender conflict**: the Morels' marriage; women's lives (Mrs Morel's thwarted mind; Clara's feminism).
6. **Death and rebirth**: William's death, the mother's death, Paul's drift towards death and his turn to life.

### Style
- Realist in setting (dialect, the mining village) but psychological in focus; poetic, symbolic nature scenes (lilies, moon, the sunset); free indirect discourse; repeated scenes of tension.

### Key critical lines
- **Alfred Booth Kuttner**, "Sons and Lovers: A Freudian Appreciation" (*Psychoanalytic Review*, 1916): the first psychoanalytic reading of a novel; Paul's failure in love is a textbook **Oedipus complex**: his libido is fixed on his mother, so he can love Miriam only spiritually and Clara only physically. Lawrence disliked the reading (he attacked Freud in *Psychoanalysis and the Unconscious*, 1921), though Frieda had told him of Freud's ideas.
- **F.R. Leavis** (*D.H. Lawrence: Novelist*, 1955): Lawrence is the great novelist of life; *Sons and Lovers* is a fine but early work, the mother's view too dominant.
- **Kate Millett** (*Sexual Politics*, 1970): Lawrence uses women (Miriam, Clara) as instruments of Paul's growth and discards them; the novel is male-centred.
- **Jessie Chambers** (*D.H. Lawrence: A Personal Record*, 1935, as "E.T."): the real Miriam felt betrayed; Lawrence had given his mother's view of her.
- **Raymond Williams**: the novel shows the working-class community and the cost of the mother's class ambition.

### "Two-in-One" (2026)
- In his *Study of Thomas Hardy* (1914) and later writings, Lawrence describes life as a union of opposites: male and female, Law and Love, flesh and spirit. True union does not merge the two into one but keeps each fully itself, held in balance: the consummation is a "two-in-one", like a new third thing (he calls it the Holy Spirit).
- **In the novel**: Paul's relationships fail because neither achieves this balance. With Miriam there is spirit without flesh (she wants to absorb his soul); with Clara flesh without spirit (she cannot reach his inner self); and his mother holds the centre of his being, so he cannot give it to any woman. The Morels' marriage fails too: physical attraction without shared mind.
- **Argument**: yes, the quest for an impossible complete union, combined with the mother's possession, makes the romances fail. But it is also Paul's immaturity and egoism (Millett's point). The ending suggests the quest may succeed later (*The Rainbow*, *Women in Love*).

### Exam angles
- **Paul's self-realisation after his mother's death** (2017): he faces his isolation, recognises he must live his own life, turns towards the town and life.
- **Libidinal and psychic patterns** (2018): Oedipal triangle; the split between "sacred" and "profane" love (Freud's 1912 essay on the "universal tendency to debasement in the sphere of love" describes exactly this split); reception: praised as a frank study of family psychology; attacked as indecent; Freudians claimed it; feminists criticised it.
- **Desire vs duty; sensuality vs spirituality** (2025): Paul's duty to his mother vs desire for Miriam/Clara; Miriam (spirit) vs Clara (sense); engagement vs detachment (Paul's impersonal attitude to Clara).
- **Tragic aspects** (2022): William's death, the mother's suffering and death, Walter's isolation, Paul's near-despair.
- **Pakistani link**: the possessive mother and the son's marriage are central themes in South Asian family life and fiction (and in many Pakistani TV dramas).

### Key lines (public domain)
- The last line: Paul walks towards the "faintly humming, glowing town, quickly."
- Mrs Morel on Miriam: she is one of those who will want to suck a man's soul out till he has none of his own left [paraphrase].

## 5.3 George Orwell, *Nineteen Eighty-Four* (1949)

Asked: 2024 (relevance today). MCQs 2026 (Newspeak).

### Context
- Written 1946–48 on the island of Jura while Orwell was dying of TB. Background: Stalin's purges and show trials; Nazi propaganda; wartime BBC and Ministry of Information (the model for the Ministry of Truth's building); the division of the world at Tehran (1943). Influences: Zamyatin's *We* (1921), James Burnham's *The Managerial Revolution* (1941).
- Orwell's statement (1949): the novel is not an attack on socialism or the British Labour Party but a show-up of the perversions to which a centralised economy is liable, already partly realised in Communism and Fascism; something like it could happen anywhere.

### Plot (own words)
- **Part One**: London, chief city of Airstrip One, province of **Oceania**, permanently at war with Eurasia or Eastasia. **Winston Smith** (39) works at the **Ministry of Truth**, rewriting old newspapers so the Party's past statements always match the present. Telescreens watch everyone; the **Thought Police** arrest dissenters; posters say BIG BROTHER IS WATCHING YOU. Winston starts a secret diary ("Down with Big Brother"), visits the proles' district, and buys a glass paperweight from **Mr Charrington**'s junk shop. He suspects **O'Brien**, an Inner Party member, of being a rebel.
- **Part Two**: **Julia**, a young woman from the Fiction Department (and the Junior Anti-Sex League), slips him a note: "I love you." They begin an affair, first in the countryside, then in the room above Mr Charrington's shop. O'Brien invites them to his flat and enrols them in the **Brotherhood**, the supposed resistance led by **Emmanuel Goldstein**, and gives Winston "the book", *The Theory and Practice of Oligarchical Collectivism*, which explains how the Party keeps power through permanent war and doublethink. The Thought Police burst in; Mr Charrington is one of them.
- **Part Three**: in the **Ministry of Love**, O'Brien tortures and "re-educates" Winston: Winston must see that two and two make five if the Party says so; power is an end in itself, "a boot stamping on a human face—forever" [O'Brien's image, paraphrased]. In **Room 101**, facing his worst fear (rats), Winston cries "Do it to Julia!" and betrays her. Released, broken, he sits in the Chestnut Tree Café. He and Julia meet coldly. News of a victory: Winston weeps with love. "He loved Big Brother."
- **Appendix: "The Principles of Newspeak"**: written in the past tense, in standard English, which some readers (e.g. Margaret Atwood) take as a hint that the regime eventually fell.

### Key concepts (MCQ gold)
| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Big Brother | The face of the Party; may not exist |
| Doublethink | Holding two contradictory beliefs and accepting both |
| Newspeak | Official language designed to shrink vocabulary so that unorthodox thought becomes impossible ("thoughtcrime" literally unthinkable); Syme works on the dictionary |
| Thoughtcrime / crimestop | Thinking against the Party / instinctive stopping of dangerous thoughts |
| Memory hole | Chute for destroying documents |
| Two Minutes Hate | Daily ritual of rage against Goldstein |
| Proles | 85% of the population, kept poor and distracted; Winston: "If there is hope, it lies in the proles" |
| Party slogans | WAR IS PEACE; FREEDOM IS SLAVERY; IGNORANCE IS STRENGTH |
| The four ministries | Truth (propaganda), Peace (war), Love (torture), Plenty (rationing) |
| Ingsoc | English Socialism, the Party's ideology |
| Unperson | Someone erased from all records |

### Themes
1. **Totalitarian power** for its own sake.
2. **Control of truth and history**: "Who controls the past controls the future: who controls the present controls the past" (Party slogan).
3. **Language and thought** (links to "Politics and the English Language").
4. **Surveillance and privacy**: the telescreen.
5. **Love and sex as rebellion**, and their defeat.
6. **The individual mind**: can it resist? Winston's defeat suggests not.

### Critical views
- **Isaac Deutscher** ("1984 — The Mysticism of Cruelty", 1955): the book's despair served Cold War anti-communism; Orwell's vision is irrational, a mysticism of cruelty.
- **Raymond Williams** (*Orwell*, 1971): admires its power but finds its picture of the proles contemptuous and its pessimism disabling.
- **Margaret Atwood** (2003 essay): reads the Newspeak appendix as hopeful; she wrote *The Handmaid's Tale* (1985) partly in dialogue with it.
- **Bernard Crick** (*George Orwell: A Life*, 1980): a satire and warning, not a prophecy.

### Relevance today (2024)
- **Surveillance**: mass state surveillance (revealed by Edward Snowden, 2013); facial recognition; "surveillance capitalism" (Shoshana Zuboff, 2019): companies, not just states, watch us.
- **Propaganda and disinformation**: "alternative facts" (2017), deepfakes, troll farms, algorithmic echo chambers.
- **Rewriting history**: deleted posts, textbook revisions, state narratives.
- **Language**: euphemism in politics and war.
- **Pakistan**: internet and platform shutdowns (e.g., X/Twitter blocked from February 2024), media censorship, enforced disappearances (the "unperson"), and also the opposite danger of unregulated disinformation. Use carefully and factually.
- **Limits**: our societies are not totalitarian in Orwell's sense; Aldous Huxley's *Brave New World* (control by pleasure and distraction) may describe the present better (Neil Postman, *Amusing Ourselves to Death*, 1985).

## 5.4 James Joyce, *A Portrait of the Artist as a Young Man* (1916)

Asked: 2019 (Stephen's maturity at the end), 2026 (masculinity and the artist-genius). MCQ 2026.

### Context
- Joyce (1882–1941): Dublin; Jesuit-educated (Clongowes Wood, Belvedere, University College Dublin); left Ireland in 1904 with Nora Barnacle; lived in Trieste, Zurich, Paris. *Dubliners* (1914), *Portrait* (serialised in *The Egoist*, 1914–15; book 1916), *Ulysses* (1922), *Finnegans Wake* (1939).
- Rewritten from an earlier draft, *Stephen Hero* (published 1944).
- Ireland under British rule; the Catholic Church's power; the fall of Charles Stewart Parnell (1890–91), the nationalist leader destroyed by scandal and condemned by the Church.
- Epigraph from Ovid's *Metamorphoses* (Book 8): Daedalus "turns his mind to unknown arts". Stephen Dedalus is named after the mythical craftsman who made wings to escape the labyrinth; his son Icarus flew too near the sun and fell.

### Plot by chapter (own words)
1. **Childhood**: baby talk ("Once upon a time… a moocow coming down along the road"). Stephen at **Clongowes Wood College**: pushed into a cold ditch by Wells, falls ill; the **Christmas dinner** quarrel about Parnell between Dante Riordan (pious) and Mr Casey and Stephen's father (Parnellites); unjustly beaten (pandied) by Father Dolan, he complains to the rector and is cheered by the boys.
2. **Adolescence**: family's financial decline; move to Dublin; **Belvedere College**; Stephen's literary ambitions; growing sexual desire; at sixteen, his first encounter with a prostitute.
3. **Sin and terror**: a school retreat; Father Arnall's long **hellfire sermons** terrify Stephen; he confesses and returns to the Church.
4. **Piety and vocation**: rigid devotion; the director offers him the chance to join the Jesuits; he refuses, sensing that his calling is not the priesthood but art. On the beach he sees a girl wading, like a beautiful seabird: an **epiphany** of mortal beauty and his vocation ("to live, to err, to fall, to triumph, to recreate life out of life").
5. **University**: talks with friends Davin (nationalist), Cranly (confidant), Lynch; his **aesthetic theory** (from Aquinas: wholeness, harmony, radiance; art should produce static, not kinetic emotion); the conversation with the English dean of studies about the word "tundish", where Stephen feels the English language is the dean's before it is his; writes a villanelle. Refuses to serve family, nation or Church ("I will not serve"): he will use "silence, exile, and cunning". **Diary entries** end the book: he will go "to encounter for the millionth time the reality of experience and to forge in the smithy of my soul the uncreated conscience of my race", and calls on "Old father, old artificer" (Daedalus).

### Themes
1. **The growth of the artist** (Künstlerroman).
2. **Rebellion against the "nets"** of nationality, language and religion (Stephen: when the soul is born, nets are flung at it to hold it back; he will try to fly by those nets).
3. **Religion**: guilt, terror, rejection, but Catholic imagery remains (the artist as "priest of the eternal imagination").
4. **Colonial identity and language**: English as an acquired, foreign tongue (the "tundish" scene).
5. **Sexuality and the body**.
6. **Beauty and art**: Stephen's aesthetics.

### Style and technique
- **Style matches age**: childish language, then adolescent romantic prose, then scholastic argument, then a diary.
- **Free indirect discourse**: the narrator's voice blends with Stephen's.
- **Epiphanies**: moments of sudden revelation.
- **Leitmotifs**: birds and flight, water, the colours, Daedalus/Icarus.
- **Episodic structure**: five chapters each ending in a climax (triumph over Dolan; first sex; confession; the bird-girl; the departure).

### Critical views
- **Hugh Kenner** (*Dublin's Joyce*, 1956): Stephen is treated ironically; he is a prig and a self-dramatising aesthete, an Icarus who will fall; Joyce stands at a distance.
- **Wayne C. Booth** (*The Rhetoric of Fiction*, 1961): the problem of distance: it is impossible to tell how far Joyce shares Stephen's views; the novel's irony is unstable.
- **Richard Ellmann** (*James Joyce*, 1959): the novel is a fictionalised autobiography of Joyce's escape.
- **Postcolonial readers** (e.g. Declan Kiberd, *Inventing Ireland*, 1995) read Stephen's struggle with English as a colonial subject's.

### Stephen's maturity at the end (2019)
- **For**: emotionally independent of family and Church; intellectually formed (aesthetic theory); artistically committed (villanelle, diary voice, clear plan of exile).
- **Against**: his theory is borrowed; his poem is weak; his pride (Icarus); the diary shows uncertainty; *Ulysses* opens with Stephen back in Dublin, unhappy. So he has reached the beginning of maturity, not its end.

### Masculinity and the artist-genius (2026)
- **Hegemonic masculinity** (R.W. Connell, *Masculinities*, 1995): the dominant, culturally honoured way of being a man (strength, sport, authority, heterosexual success). **Homosociality** (Eve Kosofsky Sedgwick, *Between Men*, 1985): male bonds that structure society, often through women.
- **Stephen's early roles**: the schoolboy code (not telling tales, physical courage before Father Dolan), his father's model of the convivial, drinking Irishman, the nationalist hero (Davin), the Catholic patriarchal order (priests as "fathers"), the brothel as rite of manhood.
- **His rejection**: he refuses priesthood, nationalism and his father's world.
- **The new role**: the solitary, superior, self-mythologising **artist-genius** (the priest of imagination, Daedalus the maker), which keeps the old masculine claims (authority, mastery, exclusion of women: the bird-girl and E.C. are images, not people). So he trades one masculine performance for another. Kenner's ironic reading supports this.

## 5.5 Iris Murdoch, *Under the Net* (1954)

Asked: 2021 (politics, philosophy and self-image), 2022 (love for others vs self-love in Maugham). In copyright: paraphrase only.

### Context
- Murdoch (1919–1999): Irish-born; Oxford philosophy tutor; wrote the first English book on Sartre (*Sartre: Romantic Rationalist*, 1953); member of the Communist Party (1938–42), later anti-communist; 26 novels; Booker Prize 1978 (*The Sea, the Sea*). Her first novel, dedicated to the French writer Raymond Queneau.
- **Title**: from Wittgenstein's *Tractatus* (1921): theories and language are like a net laid over the world; we cannot get "under the net" of concepts to reality itself. Hugo's philosophy dramatises this.

### Plot (own words)
- **Jake Donaghue**, the first-person narrator, is a talented but lazy Irish-born writer and translator in London who lives off friends, with his loyal companion **Finn**. Thrown out of his lodging by **Madge** (who is marrying a bookmaker, Sammy Starfield), he looks for a place to stay.
- He seeks his old love **Anna Quentin**, a singer now running a small mime theatre, and then her sister **Sadie**, a film star, who asks him to mind her flat. He learns that his old friend **Hugo Belfounder**, a fireworks manufacturer turned film-company owner, is involved.
- **Backstory**: years earlier Jake and Hugo were volunteers in a cold-cure experiment; they talked philosophy for weeks. Jake turned the conversations into a book, ***The Silencer*** (a dialogue between Tamarus and Annandine), which distorted Hugo's ideas; ashamed, Jake avoided Hugo ever since.
- **Comic episodes**: Jake and Finn steal **Mister Mars**, a famous film-star dog, from Sammy to sell; Jake joins a riot on a film set (a Roman set) when a political meeting of **Lefty Todd**'s New Independent Socialist Party is broken up; he swims in the Thames at night with friends; he chases a woman he thinks is Anna through Paris on Bastille Day, and loses her.
- Jake, broke, takes a job as a hospital orderly. Hugo, injured, is a patient; Jake helps him escape. Hugo explains the truth: Jake loves Anna, but Anna loves Hugo, and Hugo loves **Sadie**, who does not return his love [verify the last link]. Jake had misread everyone, casting them as players in his own story. Hugo has given away his money and is going to Nottingham to become a watchmaker.
- **Ending**: Finn goes back to Ireland; Jake decides to write seriously. His landlady Mrs Tinckham's cat gives birth to kittens of different kinds; Jake laughs that it is just one of the wonders of the world.

### Characters
- **Jake**: clever, self-centred, unreliable narrator who constantly misreads others; comic and lovable; grows towards seeing other people as real.
- **Hugo**: a holy fool of philosophy: silent, honest, distrusts theory; believes every situation is unique and that language and theory falsify it.
- **Anna and Sadie**: images Jake builds, who turn out to be independent people with their own lives.
- **Finn**: the silent, loyal shadow.

### Themes
1. **Theory vs reality** (philosophy): Hugo's view that theory is a "net" that hides particular truth; language falsifies experience.
2. **Seeing others truly**: Jake's education is learning that other people are not characters in his story. Murdoch: love is the hard realisation that something other than oneself is real.
3. **Art and truth**: Jake must stop writing clever, distorting books and write truthfully.
4. **Freedom and contingency**: existentialist themes (Sartre, Beckett, Queneau).
5. **Politics**: Lefty's socialism, the film-set riot: Murdoch's ambivalent treatment of the Left.

### Style
- **Picaresque** comic novel: episodic adventures, a roguish narrator; slapstick; first-person retrospective narration; philosophy in dialogue.

### Critical views
- **A.S. Byatt** (*Degrees of Freedom*, 1965): Murdoch's novels explore freedom and the danger of imposing patterns on others.
- **Peter Conradi** (*Iris Murdoch: The Saint and the Artist*, 1986): Jake's journey from egotism to attention.
- **Murdoch's essay "Against Dryness"** (1961): the novel should show the opacity and reality of persons rather than neat myths.
- Often grouped (to her annoyance) with the Angry Young Men and Amis's *Lucky Jim* (1954).

### Politics, philosophy and self-image (2021)
- **Politics**: Murdoch's recent Communist past and her disillusion; Lefty Todd's party is treated comically but sympathetically; Jake is drawn to and drifts away from politics.
- **Philosophy**: Wittgenstein (the net), Sartre and existentialism (contingency, freedom), her early moral philosophy (attention to the real).
- **Self-image**: Jake as Murdoch's alter ego: a writer who fears that theorising and clever books betray reality; the male narrator lets her explore her own temptations.

## 5.6 Nathaniel Hawthorne, *The Scarlet Letter* (1850) — option A (choose this)

Asked: 2016 (symbols), 2020 (Hester's wisdom through suffering), 2024 (guilt, redemption, atonement). MCQ 2026.

### Context
- Hawthorne (1804–1864): Salem, Massachusetts; ancestor John Hathorne was a judge at the Salem witch trials (1692), which haunted him. Worked at the Salem Custom House (1846–49), dismissed after a change of government. Friend of Melville; a sceptic of Transcendentalist optimism. Called his work "romances" (freedom to mix the real and the imaginary).
- **"The Custom-House"**: long introductory sketch: the narrator finds in the Custom House attic a faded red cloth letter "A" and old papers about Hester Prynne; the frame makes the story a recovered history.
- Setting: Puritan Boston, about 1642–1649.

### Plot (own words)
1. **The scaffold**: Hester Prynne stands on the town scaffold holding her baby, **Pearl**, with a scarlet "A" (adultery) embroidered on her dress. She refuses to name the father. In the crowd is her long-absent husband, an old scholar who has been a captive of Native Americans; he takes the name **Roger Chillingworth**, makes Hester swear to keep his identity secret, and vows to find her lover.
2. **Life apart**: Hester lives on the edge of town, earns her living by fine needlework and charity; Pearl grows wild and elfin. The magistrates try to take Pearl; the young minister **Arthur Dimmesdale** pleads for Hester to keep her.
3. **Chillingworth and Dimmesdale**: Dimmesdale, admired for his eloquence, is wasting away; Chillingworth becomes his physician and lodger, probes his soul and, seeing something on his chest, is sure he is Pearl's father. Dimmesdale whips himself and fasts.
4. **The midnight scaffold**: Dimmesdale stands on the scaffold at night; Hester and Pearl join him; a meteor seems to trace an "A" in the sky; Chillingworth watches.
5. Over seven years Hester's charity changes how townspeople see her; some say the A means "Able".
6. **The forest**: Hester tells Dimmesdale who Chillingworth is; they plan to flee to Europe. She takes off the letter and lets down her hair; sunlight floods the forest. Pearl refuses to come to her until the letter is put back.
7. **Election Day**: Dimmesdale gives a brilliant sermon, then mounts the scaffold with Hester and Pearl, confesses, reveals his breast (the text hints at a mark), and dies. Pearl kisses him.
8. **Conclusion**: Chillingworth withers and dies within a year, leaving his wealth to Pearl, who goes to Europe and marries well. Hester returns to Boston later, wears the letter by choice, and counsels troubled women, foreseeing a future of better relations between men and women. She is buried near Dimmesdale under one tombstone with a red "A" on a black field.

### Characters
- **Hester**: strong, proud, passionate, independent-minded; her suffering makes her thoughtful and compassionate; she speculates boldly about women's position.
- **Dimmesdale**: sensitive, eloquent, cowardly, tormented by hidden guilt.
- **Chillingworth**: the wronged husband who becomes a fiend; revenge as the worst sin ("violated, in cold blood, the sanctity of a human heart", as Dimmesdale says).
- **Pearl**: the living scarlet letter; wild, truthful, symbolic; becomes human when her father acknowledges her.

### Symbols (2016)
| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| The scarlet letter A | Adultery and shame, then Able, Angel (the night of Winthrop's death), art (her embroidery), finally a badge Hester chooses; meaning is made by society and changes |
| The scaffold | Public judgement and confession; frames the novel (beginning, middle, end) |
| Pearl | The "pearl of great price" (Matthew 13): Hester's treasure and her sin made living; truth and conscience |
| The rosebush by the prison door | Nature's mercy and beauty beside Puritan severity |
| The forest | Freedom, nature, passion, also the devil's realm ("the Black Man") |
| The town | Law, order, Puritan judgement |
| The meteor | Divine sign? or Dimmesdale's guilty imagination |
| Sunlight | Innocence and joy; it shuns Hester, plays with Pearl |
| Dimmesdale's hand over his heart | Hidden guilt |
| Chillingworth's deformity | Moral corruption made visible |

### Themes
1. **Sin, guilt and redemption**: open sin (Hester) vs hidden sin (Dimmesdale) vs the unforgivable sin of cold revenge (Chillingworth).
2. **Public vs private identity; hypocrisy**.
3. **Puritan society and law**: harsh judgement; a community that punishes but also, slowly, accepts.
4. **Women and independence**: Hester as a proto-feminist thinker.
5. **Nature vs civilisation**: forest vs town.

### Hester's wisdom through suffering (2020)
- **Yes**: suffering turns her from a passionate young woman into a thoughtful, compassionate "Sister of Mercy"; isolation lets her think freely ("The scarlet letter was her passport into regions where other women dared not tread"); she becomes a counsellor.
- **But**: her thinking also leads her to rebellion (the forest plan); her wisdom includes the recognition that she cannot herself be the prophetess of a new truth because she is stained. Hawthorne gives her wisdom with sadness.

### Guilt, redemption and atonement in a judgemental society (2024)
- Hester atones through work, charity and endurance; Dimmesdale only through public confession at death; Chillingworth never.
- Hawthorne criticises Puritan judgement (the crowd of harsh women in chapter 2) but also suggests that truth and confession are necessary: "Be true! Be true! Be true!" (the narrator's moral in the conclusion).

### Critical views
- **Henry James** (*Hawthorne*, 1879): admired the novel's moral imagination but found it too symbolic and allegorical.
- **D.H. Lawrence** (*Studies in Classic American Literature*, 1923): reads the novel against its surface: Hester as a destructive, vengeful force and Dimmesdale as the spiritual man brought down; he mocks the American cult of purity.
- **Nina Baym** (*The Shape of Hawthorne's Career*, 1976; *The Scarlet Letter: A Reading*, 1986): Hester as the heroine of the self against society.
- **Sacvan Bercovitch** (*The Office of The Scarlet Letter*, 1991): Hester's return and voluntary wearing of the A shows liberal America's ideology of consensus: dissent absorbed into acceptance.

## 5.7 William Faulkner, *The Sound and the Fury* (1929) — option B

Never asked in Part II. Know the outline in case of an MCQ or comparison.

### Context
- Faulkner (1897–1962): Oxford, Mississippi; created **Yoknapatawpha County** (capital Jefferson). Nobel Prize for 1949. His favourite novel, "the most splendid failure".
- **Title**: *Macbeth* V.v: life is "a tale / Told by an idiot, full of sound and fury, / Signifying nothing." The first section is literally told by an "idiot".

### Structure and plot (own words)
The decline of the **Compson** family, once Southern aristocrats, told in four sections:
1. **7 April 1928 — Benjy**: Benjamin Compson, 33, intellectually disabled, unable to speak. His section jumps between memories from many years without warning (italics mark some shifts). Central memory: his sister **Caddy**, who loved and cared for him and "smelled like trees"; her muddy drawers as a child climbing a tree to see her grandmother's funeral.
2. **2 June 1910 — Quentin**: the eldest son, a Harvard student, on the day he drowns himself in the Charles River. Obsessed with Caddy's lost virginity, family honour and time (he breaks his grandfather's watch). His stream of consciousness grows more chaotic. He had falsely told his father he committed incest with Caddy.
3. **6 April 1928 — Jason**: the bitter, cruel, money-minded brother who supports the household and steals the money Caddy sends for her illegitimate daughter, **Miss Quentin**, whom he bullies. Comic and vicious.
4. **8 April 1928 (Easter Sunday) — third person, centred on Dilsey**: the family's Black servant, who holds the household together. Miss Quentin runs away with Jason's hoarded money and a man from a travelling show; Jason chases them in vain. Dilsey takes Benjy to the Black church, where the Rev. Shegog's Easter sermon moves her to tears: she has seen the beginning and the end. The novel ends with Benjy howling when Luster drives the carriage round the town square the wrong way; Jason turns it, and Benjy is quiet as things pass in their proper order.
- **Caddy** is the absent centre: she never has a section; her pregnancy, hasty marriage to Herbert Head and divorce lead to her exile.
- An **Appendix** (1945) gives the family history.

### Themes
- Decline of the Old South; time and memory; loss and the absent beloved; family dysfunction; honour and sexuality; race (Dilsey's endurance: "They endured", the Appendix says of the Black servants); religion (Easter).

### Critical views
- **Jean-Paul Sartre** ("On *The Sound and the Fury*: Time in the Work of Faulkner", 1939): Faulkner's characters are trapped in the past; time is "decapitated", with no future.
- **Cleanth Brooks** (*William Faulkner: The Yoknapatawpha Country*, 1963): the novel is about the loss of love and community in a family without a real mother.
- **Olga Vickery** and others on its four-part structure as four ways of knowing.
