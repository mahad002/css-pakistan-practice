# 0. Periods and Movements behind the Set Texts

Not a syllabus heading. The syllabus lists authors and texts, not periods. But every good answer places a text in its period, and MCQs ask "which movement / which period / who coined the term". Read this file first, then come back to it before the exam.

## 2. The movements, briefly

### Elizabethan and Jacobean drama (1580s–1625)
- Public theatres (the Globe, 1599). Mixed audiences. Blank verse plus prose for low or mad characters.
- Revenge tragedy tradition: Seneca, Thomas Kyd's *The Spanish Tragedy* (c.1587). *Hamlet* is the greatest revenge tragedy and also questions revenge.
- Festive comedy (*Twelfth Night*): disguise, mistaken identity, the "green world", marriage endings.

### Restoration comedy of manners (1660–1700)
- Theatres reopened in 1660 after the Puritan closure (1642). Actresses appear for the first time.
- Witty, urban, upper-class characters; intrigue over money and marriage; fops, rakes, widows; epigrammatic dialogue.
- Jeremy Collier's attack, *A Short View of the Immorality and Profaneness of the English Stage* (1698), helped end the mode. *The Way of the World* (1700) is its last and most refined example.

### Romanticism (c.1798–1832)
- **Start:** *Lyrical Ballads* (Wordsworth and Coleridge, 1798). **End (conventional):** the Reform Act and Scott's death, 1832.
- **Ideas:** imagination over reason; nature as moral teacher and spiritual presence; the common man and rustic life; childhood; memory; the sublime; individual feeling; reaction against industrialism and Enlightenment rationalism; early sympathy with the French Revolution.
- **Key statements:** Wordsworth's Preface to *Lyrical Ballads* (1800, enlarged 1802): poetry is "the spontaneous overflow of powerful feelings" that takes its origin from "emotion recollected in tranquillity", in "a selection of language really used by men". Keats's letters: "negative capability" (Dec 1817), the "chameleon poet" (Oct 1818).
- **First generation:** Blake, Wordsworth, Coleridge. **Second:** Byron, Shelley, Keats.

### Victorian period (1837–1901)
- Industrial and imperial confidence, plus religious doubt (Darwin, *On the Origin of Species*, 1859).
- Poetry: Tennyson (Poet Laureate 1850–92), Browning (dramatic monologue), Arnold.
- The dramatic monologue: one speaker, a silent listener, the speaker reveals more than he means. "Ulysses" and "Prufrock" both use it.

### American Transcendentalism (1830s–1850s)
- Centre: Concord, Massachusetts. Members: Emerson, Thoreau (*Walden*, 1854), Margaret Fuller, Bronson Alcott. The Transcendental Club (1836); *The Dial* magazine (1840–44).
- Ideas: divinity in each person and in nature; intuition over reason and over church authority; self-reliance; nonconformity; reform. Roots in Kant (the term "transcendental"), Coleridge, German idealism, Neoplatonism, and Hindu and Persian texts (Emerson read the Bhagavad Gita, Hafiz and Saadi).
- "American Renaissance" (F.O. Matthiessen's term, 1941) for the years 1850–55: Emerson, Thoreau, Hawthorne, Melville, Whitman. Hawthorne and Melville are the sceptics, "dark Romantics" who doubted the Transcendentalists' optimism about human nature.

### Victorian realism and the regional novel
- Hardy's Wessex: a fictional south-west England. Chance, nature, rural custom, the coming of modernity. Hardy moves from pastoral comedy (*Far from the Madding Crowd*, 1874) to dark tragedy (*Tess*, 1891; *Jude*, 1895).

### Modernism (c.1910–1940)
- **Causes:** First World War; Freud; Einstein; Nietzsche ("God is dead"); Frazer's *The Golden Bough* (comparative myth); urban mass society; loss of religious and imperial certainty.
- **Features:** fragmentation; stream of consciousness and interior monologue; free indirect discourse; myth as structure (Eliot's "mythical method"); allusion and quotation; impersonality (Eliot, "Tradition and the Individual Talent", 1919); the "objective correlative" (Eliot, "Hamlet and His Problems", 1919); epiphany (Joyce); difficulty; the alienated artist.
- **Key year:** 1922: *The Waste Land*, Joyce's *Ulysses*, Woolf's *Jacob's Room*.
- Lawrence is modernist in subject (sexuality, the unconscious) though more conventional in form.

### Irish Literary Revival (1890s–1920s)
- Yeats, Lady Gregory, Synge; the Abbey Theatre (1904). Irish myth, peasant life, national identity. Joyce stands apart from and against it, but shares the question of Irish identity under British rule.

### Shavian "drama of ideas"
- Shaw, influenced by Ibsen (*The Quintessence of Ibsenism*, 1891). Plays as debates on class, money, marriage, war. Long prefaces. Shaw was a Fabian socialist.

### American drama: O'Neill
- O'Neill (Nobel 1936; four Pulitzers) is the founder of serious American drama. Moved through expressionism (*The Hairy Ape*, 1922) to late realism (*Long Day's Journey*, *The Iceman Cometh*). Sought a modern form of tragedy in which fate works through psychology and family.

### Theatre of the Absurd (1950s–60s)
- Term from Martin Esslin's book *The Theatre of the Absurd* (1961). Writers: Beckett, Ionesco, Genet, Adamov, early Pinter, Albee.
- Philosophy: Camus, *The Myth of Sisyphus* (1942): the absurd is the clash between the human need for meaning and a silent universe. Sartre's existentialism.
- Features: circular or static plots; little action; illogical or broken dialogue; pauses and silences; tramps and clowns; music-hall comedy; no resolution.
- **Comedy of menace:** critic Irving Wardle's label (1958) for Pinter's early plays, borrowed from the subtitle of David Campton's *The Lunatic View: A Comedy of Menace*. Ordinary rooms invaded by an unexplained threat.

### The Movement (1950s)
- Named in an unsigned *Spectator* article (1954, by J.D. Scott). Anthology: Robert Conquest's *New Lines* (1956). Poets: Larkin, Kingsley Amis, Donald Davie, D.J. Enright, Thom Gunn, Elizabeth Jennings, John Wain.
- Plain language, regular forms, irony, empiricism, suspicion of modernist difficulty and of Dylan Thomas's rhetoric. Larkin's main collections: *The Less Deceived* (1955), *The Whitsun Weddings* (1964), *High Windows* (1974).

### Post-war British novel
- Philosophical and comic: Murdoch (*Under the Net*, 1954), Amis (*Lucky Jim*, 1954), the "Angry Young Men" (John Osborne's *Look Back in Anger*, 1956; John Wain).

### American Southern Renaissance (1920s–1950s)
- Faulkner, Robert Penn Warren, Eudora Welty, Flannery O'Connor. The decline of the old Southern families, the burden of history and slavery, experimental narration. Faulkner won the Nobel Prize for 1949.

### The modern short story
- O. Henry: the "twist" or surprise ending, urban New York, sentiment and irony. The O. Henry Prize (since 1919).
- Maugham: the well-made, plotted, ironic story told by a worldly narrator, close to Maupassant.
- Chesterton: paradox, whimsy, Christian moral purpose; also creator of Father Brown.

## 3. Terms that MCQs keep asking

| Term | Meaning | Set-text example |
|---|---|---|
| Blank verse | Unrhymed iambic pentameter | "Tintern Abbey", "Ulysses", *Hamlet* |
| Dramatic monologue | Single speaker, implied listener, self-revelation | "Ulysses", "Prufrock" |
| Rhyme royal | Seven-line stanza, ababbcc | "Resolution and Independence" (with a final alexandrine) |
| Alexandrine | Twelve-syllable (iambic hexameter) line | Last line of each "Resolution and Independence" stanza |
| Ode | Lyric of praise or meditation; Pindaric (triadic, public) vs Horatian (regular stanzas, calm) | Keats's odes are homostrophic, closer to Horace |
| Negative capability | Keats: staying in doubt without forcing an answer | "Ode to a Nightingale" |
| Objective correlative | Eliot: a set of objects or events that evokes a specific emotion | Eliot says *Hamlet* lacks one |
| Dissociation of sensibility | Eliot: the split of thought from feeling after the 17th century | Eliot's essay on the Metaphysical poets (1921) |
| Epiphany | Joyce: a sudden spiritual showing in an ordinary moment | The bird-girl in *Portrait*, ch. 4 |
| Stream of consciousness | Recording the flow of thought (term from William James) | Benjy and Quentin in *The Sound and the Fury* |
| Free indirect discourse | Third-person narration coloured by a character's mind | *Portrait*, *Sons and Lovers* |
| Bildungsroman / Künstlerroman | Novel of growing up / of an artist's growth | *Sons and Lovers*; *Portrait* (Künstlerroman) |
| Comedy of manners | Witty satire of upper-class social codes | *The Way of the World* |
| Proviso scene | Lovers bargain over the terms of marriage | Act IV, *The Way of the World* |
| Tragicomedy | Mixes tragic and comic | *Godot* (subtitle: "a tragicomedy in two acts") |
| Pathetic fallacy | Ruskin's term: giving human feeling to nature | Storm scenes in *King Lear* and *Far from the Madding Crowd* |
| Hamartia | Aristotle: tragic error or flaw | Lear's rash judgement |
| Anagnorisis / peripeteia | Recognition / reversal | Lear recognising Cordelia |
| Catharsis | Purgation of pity and fear | Aristotle, *Poetics* |
| Gyre | Yeats's spiral image of historical cycles | "The Second Coming" |
| Mythical method | Eliot: using myth to order modern chaos | *The Waste Land* |
| Defamiliarisation | Shklovsky (1917): art makes the familiar strange | Formalism, precursor of structuralism |
