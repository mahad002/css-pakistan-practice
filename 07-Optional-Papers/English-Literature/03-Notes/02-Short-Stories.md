# 2. Short Stories (syllabus component II, 10 marks)

One question every year since 2018. "The Lotus-Eater" alone has been asked six times. Three short texts: read each one at least three times.

Copyright note: "The Gift of the Magi" (1905) and Chesterton's story (1908–09) are public domain. Maugham (died 1965) is public domain in Pakistan (life + 50) but not in the US or UK, so it is paraphrased only.

## 2.1 W. Somerset Maugham, "The Lotus-Eater" (1935)

Asked: 2017 (Wilson's character; title), 2021 (circumstances shape character), 2022 (self-love vs Murdoch's love for others), 2023 (title), 2025 (title; suffering and fragility), 2026 (interwar context). MCQ 2026.

### Context
- Maugham (1874–1965): trained as a doctor; playwright, novelist (*Of Human Bondage*, 1915), master of the plotted short story; British agent in both world wars; lived on the French Riviera (Villa Mauresque).
- First published 1935 (in *Argosy*, per Wikipedia's category data); collected in *The Mixture as Before* (1940). Maugham's biographer Selina Hastings (*The Secret Lives of Somerset Maugham*, 2009) says Wilson is loosely modelled on John Ellingham Brooks, an Englishman who idled away his life on Capri.
- Narrated by a worldly first-person narrator close to Maugham himself, who claims only to report what he saw.
- **Interwar context** (2026 question): after 1918 the Victorian faith in duty, work, thrift and progress was badly shaken. The Depression (1929–) made security look fragile. Writers asked whether ordinary men should simply escape to pleasure. Wilson's flight from the bank to Capri expresses that mood; the ending shows the cost.

### Title
- In Homer's *Odyssey* (Book 9), Odysseus's men eat the lotus fruit, forget home and lose all wish to return. Tennyson's "The Lotos-Eaters" (1832) turns them into spokesmen for rest and drowsy forgetfulness.
- Wilson "eats the lotus": he gives up striving for a life of pure ease on a beautiful island, and loses the will that might have saved him.
- **Contrast with Tennyson's "Ulysses"** (also on the syllabus): Ulysses refuses rest and wants "to strive, to seek, to find, and not to yield". Wilson does the opposite. This link earns marks.

### Plot summary (own words)
1. **Opening reflection.** The narrator says most people do not choose their lives; they drift into the shape circumstance gives them. Wilson is an exception: he deliberately shaped his life.
2. **Meeting Wilson (1913).** On a visit to a friend on Capri, the narrator is introduced to Thomas Wilson, a quiet, contented Englishman who has lived on the island for about fifteen years.
3. **Wilson's story.** He had been the manager of a bank branch in London. On a holiday he saw Capri and fell in love with its beauty. He had no family ties to hold him. A year later, rather than work another twelve or thirteen years for a pension, he made a cold calculation: he used his savings to buy an **annuity for 25 years**. He would live in perfect freedom until about 60, and if nature had not ended his life by then, he would end it himself. He thought 25 happy years worth the price. The narrator admits he himself would have taken the safe road and worked for the pension.
4. **His contented life.** He rents a small cottage with a view, is looked after by a local peasant woman, swims, walks, reads, and watches the sea and the moonlight. He is happy, harms no one, and has no ambitions. The narrator is half-admiring, half-doubtful.
5. **The end of the annuity.** About thirteen years later, after the war, the narrator returns and learns what happened. When the annuity ran out, Wilson did not kill himself as planned. He sold what he had and borrowed from the islanders for about a year. When credit ran out, he shut himself in his cottage with burning charcoal to suffocate. He was found and saved, but the fumes damaged his brain: he was left simple-minded, though not enough to be put in an asylum.
6. **Decline.** Penniless, he was allowed to live in the woodshed of his former peasant landlord, carrying water and feeding the animals, treated as little more than a servant. He became timid and frightened, like a hunted creature, and wandered the hills.
7. **Death.** About six years after the suicide attempt he was found dead on a hillside overlooking the Faraglioni, the sea rocks whose beauty had first drawn him to Capri. The narrator suggests that beauty itself may have killed him. He reflects that Wilson probably had the resolve to die when he made his plan, but twenty-five years of ease, without struggle or challenge, had drained his will, as muscles used only on level ground lose the strength to climb a mountain.

### Characters
- **Thomas Wilson**: rational planner turned hedonist. Mild, polite, self-sufficient, unambitious. His flaw is not vice but **lack of will**, and a failure to imagine the future self who would have to keep the bargain. He loves beauty, not people: he has no deep relationships.
- **The narrator**: urbane, observant, ironic, slightly detached. He neither condemns nor praises; his final comment supplies the moral.
- **The landlord's family and the islanders**: practical; they lend while there is hope of repayment and treat him as a drudge when there is none.

### Themes
1. **Free choice vs circumstance.** Wilson thinks he has escaped circumstance by planning; in the end circumstance (and his own nature) defeat him.
2. **Idleness and the erosion of will.** Twenty-five years of ease weaken the resolve that created them.
3. **Self-love and isolation.** His happiness is solitary; when it fails no one loves him enough to help.
4. **The price of escapism.** A dream of pure freedom carries a bill.
5. **Human fragility and suffering** (2025 question): the story shows that no plan secures a person against age, weakness and fear. The reasonable man who calculated everything is reduced to a frightened creature.
6. **Beauty and death**: the island's beauty (the Faraglioni, moonlight, the sea) frames both his happiness and his death.

### Style and technique
- Frame narration by a reliable but ironic narrator; reported speech; long retrospective summary.
- Plain, polished prose; understatement; little authorial judgement until the end.
- Irony: the most rational plan ends in the least rational death; the man who chose his life could not choose his death.
- A Maupassant-like twist, but quieter: the reversal is psychological, not a trick.

### Critical views
- Maugham described himself as "in the very first row of the second-raters" (*The Summing Up*, 1938, paraphrased in substance); critics such as **Edmund Wilson** dismissed his work as competent but shallow. Others, such as **Anthony Burgess** and **Ted Morgan** (biographer), defend his narrative clarity. [verify Burgess and Morgan's exact judgements]
- The 2026 paper quoted a critic (de Onís, 2016) placing the story in the interwar shift from Victorian structure to existential doubt. Use this framing: Wilson is a small existential hero who creates his own meaning, but lacks Sartrean responsibility for his choice to the end.

### Exam angles
- **Significance of the title** (asked three times): Homeric origin, Tennyson's poem, Wilson as lotus-eater, the irony that the lotus was not oblivion but slow decay; contrast with "Ulysses".
- **Character sketch of Wilson**: show mixed traits; was the title fit? Yes, with the added twist that he forgot not home but his own resolve.
- **Circumstances shape character** (2021): Wilson tried to defy circumstance; the story shows both choice and circumstance at work: his choice created the conditions (ease) that later shaped his weakness.
- **Self-love vs love for others** (2022, with *Under the Net*): Wilson lives for his own pleasure; Jake Donaghue in Murdoch's novel learns, painfully, to see other people as real and separate. Murdoch's ethics ("love is the extremely difficult realisation that something other than oneself is real") is the perfect counterpoint [quotation from Murdoch's essay "The Sublime and the Good", 1959; verify wording].
- **Russell's verdict** on Wilson: no zest for wider interests, no affection, no work, so his happiness had no foundation.
- **Pakistani angle**: the dream of early retirement or of "settling abroad" as escape; the need for purpose (Iqbal's praise of constant struggle: life is motion and effort).

## 2.2 G.K. Chesterton, "A Somewhat Improbable Story" (1908–09)

Asked: 2020 (story-within-a-story). MCQ 2026.

### Context
- Gilbert Keith Chesterton (1874–1936): essayist, journalist, poet, novelist (*The Man Who Was Thursday*, 1908), creator of Father Brown (from 1910), Christian apologist (*Orthodoxy*, 1908; converted to Catholicism 1922). Known as "the prince of paradox".
- The piece is chapter XXXVI of *Tremendous Trifles* (1909), a collection of short newspaper pieces first written for the *Daily News*. The collection's argument is that small, ordinary things are full of wonder if we look at them freshly. (I confirmed the chapter number in the 1909 Dodd, Mead edition; the British Methuen edition's contents differ.)

### Plot summary (own words)
1. **Frame.** The narrator is eating in a crowded, hurried City quick-lunch restaurant among clerks who rush their food. Opposite him sits a large, prosperous-looking man who handles his hat, chair and table with odd, solemn care.
2. The narrator makes a casual remark that people treat furniture too carelessly. The man is startled and asks whether he meant anything by it. When told no, he tells his story.
3. **Inner story.** For more than forty years he had left his office in Leadenhall Street at exactly the same time and walked the same route to his station, a walk of a few minutes, without ever noticing it.
4. One day the familiar street felt steeper. Then it rose more and more sharply until he was climbing what seemed a mountain slope, though the shops and signs were unchanged. The street had risen like a wave. When he lifted a coal-hole cover he saw only empty sky below.
5. A strange, silent figure by the railings, plainly not of this world, explained: the street was rising up to heaven to seek justice. It had been used and trampled every day by men who never thought about it, and like an overworked horse it was rebelling and throwing off its rider.
6. The man protested that streets go where they lead. The figure answered by asking whether he was sure he himself was alive, since he had repeated the same journey every day without thought, like a machine.
7. **Return to frame.** Since that day, the man says, he has treated inanimate things with respect. He gets up, bows to the mustard pot, and leaves.

### Themes
- **Against mechanical routine.** Modern commercial life turns people into machines that no longer see the world.
- **Wonder and gratitude.** Chesterton's lifelong theme: ordinary things are miraculous; ingratitude dulls us. (*Orthodoxy* argues that we should be surprised by the sun rising each morning.)
- **Who is alive?** The paradox: the street has more life and will than the clerk.
- **Justice and dignity of things**: a comic version of a serious moral idea, that everything created deserves respect.
- **Satire of the City**: London's business district, the lunch-counter rush, routine.

### Narrative technique: the story within a story (2020)
- **Frame**: realistic, everyday (lunch in the City) and comic.
- **Inner tale**: fantastic, dreamlike, told by an unreliable stranger.
- **Effects:**
  1. **Credibility and distance**: the fantastic is reported second-hand, so the reader can treat it as either a madman's tale or a parable. The title ("somewhat improbable") winks at this.
  2. **Irony**: the narrator's throwaway remark about furniture becomes, by chance, the key to the stranger's life; the frame mirrors the theme (people treat things carelessly).
  3. **Moral without preaching**: the stranger, not Chesterton, draws the lesson.
  4. **Comic closure**: the final bow to the mustard pot brings the parable back to everyday absurdity.
- **Judgement**: the technique works well for so short a piece. A possible weakness: the inner tale is so brief and the frame so slight that some readers find the piece more an essay with a fable than a full story.
- Compare other frame narratives: Conrad's *Heart of Darkness* (Marlow), Chaucer's *Canterbury Tales*, *The Thousand and One Nights*; in the syllabus, Maugham's narrator in "The Lotus-Eater".

### Style
- Paradox and inversion (the living street, the dead man); vivid fantasy; playful tone over a serious core; comic precision of detail (the hat, the mustard pot).

### Critical views
- **T.S. Eliot**, in his obituary note (1936), credited Chesterton with defending the dignity of the ordinary man and doing more than anyone to keep a Christian minority alive in the modern world [verify wording].
- **Jorge Luis Borges** admired Chesterton's fantastic imagination and his use of the detective form for metaphysical ends.
- Critics such as **Garry Wills** (*Chesterton: Man and Mask*, 1961) see paradox as Chesterton's method for restoring wonder.

### Exam angles
- Success of the frame technique (above).
- Satire of modern mechanised life: compare Eliot's crowd flowing over London Bridge in *The Waste Land*, also City clerks.
- Chesterton's philosophy of wonder: relate to Wordsworth (nature restores "the eye made quiet") and to Emerson (seeing the world freshly).
- Pakistani angle: the office routine of a government clerk; Ghalib's and Iqbal's praise of wonder (*hairat*).

## 2.3 O. Henry, "The Gift of the Magi" (1905)

Asked: 2016 (richer or poorer; was the sacrifice wise?), 2018 (reassurance for lovers), 2019 (are they "wise"?), 2024 (intentions, style, devices). MCQ 2026.

### Context
- O. Henry: pen name of William Sydney Porter (1862–1910). Bank teller convicted of embezzlement; wrote stories in prison (1898–1901); then in New York wrote a story a week for the *New York Sunday World*. About 600 stories.
- First published on 10 December 1905 in the *Sunday World*; collected in *The Four Million* (1906). The collection's title answers the claim that only "the Four Hundred" (New York's high society) mattered: O. Henry wrote about the four million ordinary people of the city.

### Plot summary (own words)
1. **Christmas Eve.** Della Dillingham Young has saved one dollar and eighty-seven cents, much of it in pennies bargained from the grocer, butcher and vegetable man, to buy a present for her husband Jim. She cries on the shabby couch.
2. **Setting.** A furnished flat at $8 a week. Jim's income has fallen from $30 to $20 a week; the name "Dillingham" on the mailbox seems to shrink.
3. **Their two treasures.** Jim's gold watch, which had been his father's and grandfather's, and Della's long brown hair. The narrator jokes that her hair would make the Queen of Sheba's jewels look poor, and Jim's watch would make King Solomon envious.
4. **The sacrifice.** Della sells her hair to Madame Sofronie for $20 and spends hours searching shops. She buys a simple platinum fob chain for the watch, for $21. At home she curls her short hair and fears Jim will think she looks like a Coney Island chorus girl.
5. **Jim's return.** He stares at her with a strange expression that is not anger or surprise. He gives her his package: a set of beautiful tortoise-shell combs with jewelled rims that she had long admired in a Broadway window. He sold his watch to buy them.
6. **Resolution.** Della shows him the chain; Jim smiles, tells her to put the presents away for a while because they are too good to use now, and suggests they have dinner.
7. **Narrator's close.** The Magi were wise men who brought gifts to the infant Christ and invented the art of giving Christmas presents. The narrator has told the story of two foolish children who unwisely sacrificed their greatest treasures for each other. But, he says, of all who give gifts these two were the wisest; everywhere they are the wisest. **They are the magi.**

### Characters
- **Della**: loving, emotional, impulsive, practical in saving, brave in sacrifice; also vain of her hair, so her sacrifice is real.
- **Jim**: young, serious, overworked, devoted; his calm on seeing her hair shows his love is not for her looks.

### Themes
1. **Sacrificial love**: each gives up the most valuable thing he or she owns.
2. **The true value of gifts**: the useless presents become precious as signs of love.
3. **Poverty and dignity**: lower-middle-class life in New York; love makes them rich.
4. **Wisdom vs foolishness**: by worldly reason the sacrifice was foolish; by the logic of love it was wise.
5. **Christmas and the spirit of giving** (the Magi, the nativity).

### Devices and style (2024 question)
| Device | Example and effect |
|---|---|
| Situational irony | Each gift is made useless by the other's sacrifice |
| Twist ending | Withheld until the final scene; the story's power depends on it |
| Intrusive, chatty narrator | Comments, jokes, addresses the reader; turns pathos into warmth |
| Allusion | The Magi, the Queen of Sheba, King Solomon: biblical grandeur set against a poor flat |
| Hyperbole and humour | Hair that would devalue Sheba's jewels; the shrinking "Dillingham" |
| Symbolism | Hair = beauty and youth; watch = family heritage and time; combs and chain = love's gifts |
| Sentiment balanced by wit | Della's tears are described with comic precision (the couch, the "sniffles") |
| Paradox | "Foolish children" who are "the wisest" |

### Answering "richer or poorer?" and "wise or foolish?" (2016, 2019)
- **Materially poorer**: they have lost $40 of value and their two treasures, and gained objects they cannot use.
- **Emotionally and morally richer**: each has proved the other's love beyond doubt.
- **Wise?** The narrator deliberately reverses the judgement. Their act was impulsive and unpractical, but wisdom in the story is the knowledge of what really matters. The Magi's gifts were also symbolic, not useful.
- A balanced answer can admit the sentimental view and still defend it: O. Henry knows the act is foolish and says so, then makes the reader agree it is wise.

### Reassurance for lovers (2018)
- Against the many stories of betrayal and infidelity in literature (Troilus and Cressida, Madame Bovary, the faithless lovers of modern fiction), this story shows two people each putting the other first. Its reassurance is that love can survive poverty and that mutual sacrifice cancels out loss. Compare *Under the Net* and *Sons and Lovers*, where love is selfish or divided.

### Critical views
- **Fred Lewis Pattee** (*The Development of the American Short Story*, 1923) criticised O. Henry for tricks, sentimentality and surface cleverness without depth [verify]. Many later critics repeated this view.
- Defenders point to his sympathy for ordinary city people and his skill in compression; the O. Henry Prize (founded 1919) honours the short story form in his name.
- **Formalist reading**: a perfect example of the "well-made" story with a single effect, as Edgar Allan Poe prescribed in his review of Hawthorne (1842).

### Exam angles
- Intentions: to celebrate the dignity and love of the poor; to redefine wisdom; to entertain with irony.
- Devices table above.
- Comparison with Maugham: both have a reflective narrator; O. Henry's twist is sentimental and uplifting, Maugham's is bleak.
- Pakistani angle: dowry and wedding spending versus the value of sacrifice and sincerity; the Urdu short-story tradition (Premchand's "Eidgah": the boy Hamid buys tongs for his grandmother instead of toys, a similar story of loving sacrifice).
