# 3. Poetry (syllabus component III, 20 marks)

About 1.5 questions per paper. Wordsworth, Keats and Eliot carry almost all of them. Larkin, Stevens and Whitman have never had a full question; learn them at MCQ-plus depth.

Copyright note: Wordsworth, Keats, Tennyson, Whitman, "The Second Coming" (1920) and Eliot's two poems (1917, 1922) are public domain in the US and Pakistan; short quotations are given. Yeats's "A Dialogue of Self and Soul" (1933), Stevens and Larkin are still in copyright in the US/UK (Larkin everywhere), so they are paraphrased only. Read the full poems from the sources in `../08-Resources.md`.

**How to use quotations:** learn 4–6 short lines per major poem. One quotation per main paragraph, followed by your comment, is enough.

## 3.1 William Wordsworth (1770–1850): background

- Born Cockermouth, Lake District. Orphaned young. Cambridge. In France 1791–92: supported the Revolution, loved Annette Vallon (daughter Caroline). Disillusioned by the Terror and by England's war with France (1793). Crisis, recovery through nature and his sister Dorothy.
- *Lyrical Ballads* (1798) with Coleridge. Preface (1800, 1802): poetry of "humble and rustic life" in "a selection of language really used by men"; poetry as "the spontaneous overflow of powerful feelings" that "takes its origin from emotion recollected in tranquillity".
- *The Prelude* (finished 1805, published 1850): autobiographical epic, "the growth of a poet's mind". Poet Laureate 1843.
- **Key ideas**: nature as teacher and moral guardian; memory ("spots of time"); childhood; the dignity of humble people; the imagination as half-creating what it perceives; a sense of a spirit running through all things (pantheism, later toned down into orthodox Anglicanism).

## 3.2 "Lines Composed a Few Miles above Tintern Abbey" (1798)

Asked: 2016 (change in attitude to nature), 2018 (recovering a fading past), 2019 (refusal to mourn), 2024 (ecocriticism), 2025 ("still, sad music of humanity").

### Facts
- Full title adds "on Revisiting the Banks of the Wye during a Tour, July 13, 1798". Last poem in *Lyrical Ballads*. 159 lines of **blank verse**. Wordsworth said he composed it in his head on a four-day walk with Dorothy and did not alter a line [his own later note].
- The abbey itself is never described. The poem is set above it, in the Wye valley on the Welsh border.
- Five years after a first visit (August 1793), when Wordsworth was 23 and in crisis over France.

### Structure and summary
1. **Lines 1–22: the return.** Five years have passed. He sees again the cliffs, the orchards, the hedgerows, the smoke rising among the trees (perhaps from vagrants or a hermit's cave). The scene connects landscape with "the quiet of the sky".
2. **Lines 22–49: what memory of the scene has given him.** In lonely rooms in towns and cities, the memory brought "sensations sweet", calm, and probably unremembered acts of kindness and love. Above all it gave a "blessed mood" in which "We see into the life of things": the body almost asleep, the soul alive, harmony and joy.
3. **Lines 49–57: doubt.** Even if this is a vain belief, he has often turned to the Wye in spirit.
4. **Lines 58–111: three stages of his relation to nature.**
   - **Boyhood**: "glad animal movements", purely physical.
   - **1793, youth**: nature was "an appetite; a feeling and a love", needing no thought; he bounded like a roe, more like one fleeing something he dreaded than seeking something he loved; "aching joys" and "dizzy raptures".
   - **Now, maturity**: that time is past, but he does not mourn: "other gifts / Have followed; for such loss, I would believe, / Abundant recompence." He has learned to hear "The still, sad music of humanity" and feels "A presence that disturbs me with the joy / Of elevated thoughts; a sense sublime / Of something far more deeply interfused", dwelling in sunsets, ocean, air, sky and the mind of man: "A motion and a spirit". Nature is "The anchor of my purest thoughts, the nurse, / The guide, the guardian of my heart, and soul / Of all my moral being."
5. **Lines 111–159: address to Dorothy.** Even without these beliefs, he would have his sister. In her "wild eyes" he reads his former self. He prays that nature will do for her what it did for him, because "Nature never did betray / The heart that loved her". Whatever pain comes, memory of this day will heal her, and she will remember that he loved this place, more dear for itself and for her sake.

### Themes
1. **Memory and time**: the past survives inside the present; the mind half-creates and half-perceives.
2. **Loss and recompense**: the poem refuses to mourn (2019). It is an argument for maturity: passion is replaced by wisdom.
3. **Nature as moral and spiritual teacher**: pantheistic intuition of one spirit in all things.
4. **Humanity**: the "still, sad music" links nature-feeling with sympathy for human suffering.
5. **Sibling love and continuity**: Dorothy is his past self and his future hope.
6. **Doubt**: "If this / Be but a vain belief…", "I would believe": the confidence is hard-won and qualified.

### Form and style
- Blank verse that sounds like thinking aloud: long, winding sentences; enjambment; repetition ("Five years… five summers… five long winters").
- Close to Milton's blank verse and to the conversation poem (Coleridge's "Frost at Midnight").
- **M.H. Abrams** ("Structure and Style in the Greater Romantic Lyric", 1965) took this poem as the model of a Romantic form: a speaker in a specific landscape; a meditation that the scene starts; a return to the scene with new insight.

### Critical views (paraphrased)
- **M.H. Abrams**: the greater Romantic lyric (above); Romanticism as "natural supernaturalism", religious feeling moved into nature (*Natural Supernaturalism*, 1971).
- **Geoffrey Hartman** (*Wordsworth's Poetry 1787–1814*, 1964): the poem shows the poet's imagination struggling with, and growing out of, nature.
- **Marjorie Levinson** (New Historicist, *Wordsworth's Great Period Poems*, 1986): the poem's real meaning lies in what it leaves out: the ruined abbey, the beggars who lived in it, the iron works and river pollution of the Wye, the French Revolution, the date (one day before the anniversary of the fall of the Bastille). The poem turns history into private landscape.
- **Jonathan Bate** (*Romantic Ecology*, 1991), answering Levinson: Wordsworth is a pioneer of ecological thinking; his "politics" is a politics of the earth; nature as a whole system with the human mind inside it.

### Ecocritical reading (2024 question)
- **Ecocriticism**: the study of literature and the physical environment (Cheryll Glotfelty's definition, *The Ecocriticism Reader*, 1996). Key ideas: nature as more than a backdrop; anthropocentrism vs ecocentrism; "deep ecology" (Arne Naess); the "pastoral" (Terry Gifford's "post-pastoral").
- **In the poem**: hedgerows "hardly hedge-rows, little lines / Of sportive wood run wild" (human order softened back into wildness); smoke among trees (humans living within nature); "the life of things"; a spirit "Rolls through all things" (interconnection, a holistic view); "Nature never did betray" (trust and reciprocity); the moral claim that loving nature makes us better.
- **Environmental ethics**: reverence, care, the idea that nature has value in itself and heals the mind (modern research on green space and mental health supports this).
- **Limits**: the poem is still centred on the human mind (what nature does *for me*); Levinson's point that it hides the industrial damage on the Wye. A good answer gives both Bate and Levinson.
- **Pakistan link**: deforestation, the Margalla Hills, smog in Lahore, the 2022 floods; Wordsworth's call to treat nature as "guardian" rather than resource.

### Exam angles
- Change in attitude to nature (2016): the three stages.
- Recovering a fading experience (2018): memory as both loss and restoration; the "blessed mood"; Dorothy as living memory.
- Refusal to mourn (2019): "Abundant recompence"; "other gifts"; compare Keats's "Nightingale", which does end in loss.
- "Still, sad music of humanity" as Romantic value (2025): link to the Preface, to "Resolution and Independence", to Romantic sympathy for the poor.

## 3.3 "Resolution and Independence" (1802, published 1807)

Asked: 2017 (Wordsworth democratised poetry).

### Facts
- Written May–July 1802; in *Poems, in Two Volumes* (1807). Twenty stanzas of seven lines: a variant of **rhyme royal** (ababbcc) with the last line an **alexandrine**.
- Based on a real meeting: Dorothy's journal (3 October 1800) records an old leech-gatherer they met near Grasmere.
- Also called "The Leech-Gatherer".

### Summary
1. **Stanzas 1–2**: after a night of storm, a bright morning; birds sing, a hare races over the moor raising a mist of glittering spray. All things rejoice.
2. **Stanzas 3–7**: the poet, a traveller, is happy too, then suddenly falls into dejection: "fears and fancies"; fear of future "Solitude, pain of heart, distress, and poverty". He has lived as a happy child of the earth, as if life were a summer mood; who will provide for one who takes no care? He thinks of poets who began in joy and ended in misery: **Chatterton**, "the marvellous Boy", who killed himself at 17, and **Burns**, who walked "in glory and in joy / Following his plough". "We Poets in our youth begin in gladness; / But thereof come in the end despondency and madness."
3. **Stanzas 8–12**: by a pool on the bare moor he sees a very old man, bent double, like a huge stone on a hilltop that seems alive, or a sea-beast crawled out to sun itself. He leans on a staff and stares into the muddy water.
4. **Stanzas 13–17**: the poet asks what he does. The old man answers in lofty, measured speech, "Choice word and measured phrase, above the reach / Of ordinary men": he is poor, gathers leeches (for doctors) from pond to pond, a hard and dangerous trade; leeches are now scarce, but he perseveres.
5. **Stanzas 17–19**: the poet's mind drifts; the old man seems like a figure in a dream sent to give him "human strength". He repeats his question; the man patiently repeats his answer, cheerfully.
6. **Stanza 20**: the poet laughs at himself: in such a decrepit man such a firm mind. "God," he says, "be my help and stay secure; / I'll think of the Leech-gatherer on the lonely moor!"

### Themes
- **Resolution and independence** (the title): firmness of mind and self-reliance in poverty.
- **The poet's anxiety**: fear that imaginative joy leads to ruin (Chatterton, Burns, and Coleridge's own crisis in "Dejection: An Ode", April 1802, to which this poem replies).
- **Dignity of the humble**: a poor, old labourer becomes a moral teacher and almost a visionary figure.
- **Nature and the human figure**: the man seems part of the landscape (stone, sea-beast, cloud).

### "Democratising the literary tradition" (2017)
- Subject: an aged, poor, rural labourer, not a hero, king or god, is the source of wisdom.
- Language: the Preface's programme of "language really used by men".
- The common man's endurance is presented with the seriousness once reserved for epic.
- **Complication** (earns marks): the leech-gatherer actually speaks in a stately, almost biblical style ("Choice word and measured phrase"), and **Coleridge** (*Biographia Literaria*, 1817, ch. 17–22) criticised Wordsworth's theory, arguing that his best poetry did *not* use real rustic speech, and pointed to faults such as "matter-of-factness" and "mental bombast". So Wordsworth democratised the *subject* more than the *diction*.
- **Lewis Carroll** parodied the poem as the White Knight's song in *Through the Looking-Glass* (1871): a sign of how famous the poem's repeated questioning was.

## 3.4 John Keats (1795–1821): background

- Son of a London stable-keeper; father died 1804, mother of TB 1810. Trained as an apothecary-surgeon at Guy's Hospital; gave it up for poetry (1816).
- Attacked by reviewers (*Blackwood's* "Cockney School" articles; the *Quarterly* on *Endymion*, 1818). Nursed his brother **Tom**, who died of TB in December 1818. Loved Fanny Brawne. Died of TB in Rome, February 1821, aged 25. Epitaph: "Here lies one whose name was writ in water."
- **1819**, the "great year": "The Eve of St Agnes", "La Belle Dame sans Merci", the five spring odes (Psyche, Nightingale, Grecian Urn, Melancholy, Indolence) and "To Autumn" (September).
- **Letters (key ideas)**: "negative capability", being "capable of being in uncertainties, Mysteries, doubts, without any irritable reaching after fact and reason" (to his brothers, Dec 1817); the "chameleon poet" with no fixed identity (to Woodhouse, Oct 1818); the world as "the vale of Soul-making" (April 1819); "I am certain of nothing but of the holiness of the Heart's affections and the truth of Imagination" (Nov 1817).

### The ode and Keats's form (2022 question)
- **Pindaric ode** (Pindar, 5th century BC): public, ceremonial praise of victors and gods; triadic structure (strophe, antistrophe, epode); elevated, irregular in English imitations (Cowley, Gray, Wordsworth's "Immortality Ode").
- **Horatian ode** (Horace): private, reflective, calm; regular repeated stanzas (homostrophic).
- **Keats** built his own stanza from the sonnet: a Shakespearean quatrain (abab) plus a Petrarchan sestet (cdecde), ten lines in "Nightingale", eleven in "Autumn". His odes are Horatian in form (regular stanzas) but address and praise an object (bird, urn, season), keeping the ode's root sense of praise and invocation.
- **Answer frame**: the odes keep the classical ode's elements (apostrophe, praise, elevated diction, meditation) but turn praise inward: the object praised becomes a way to think about the self, mortality and art.

## 3.5 "Ode to a Nightingale" (May 1819)

Asked: 2019 (pleasure and pain), 2021 (sensuousness), 2022 (classical ode).

### Facts
- Written in Hampstead, May 1819, according to his friend Charles Brown, under a plum tree where a nightingale nested. Eight stanzas of ten lines, rhyme ababcdecde, iambic pentameter with the eighth line shortened to three feet (trimeter).

### Summary by stanza
1. **Numbness and joy**: his heart aches and a drowsy numbness pains his sense, as if he had drunk hemlock or an opiate. Not from envy of the bird, but from being "too happy in thine happiness" as it sings of summer "in full-throated ease".
2. **Wine**: he longs for a draught of vintage tasting of Flora, dance and "sunburnt mirth", "a beaker full of the warm South", "the blushful Hippocrene", to drink and fade away with the bird into the forest dim.
3. **The human world**: to forget "The weariness, the fever, and the fret" where men groan, "Where youth grows pale, and spectre-thin, and dies" (Tom), where to think is to be full of sorrow and beauty and love cannot last.
4. **Poetry instead of wine**: "Away! away! for I will fly to thee… on the viewless wings of Poesy." He is with the bird in the night; the moon may be on her throne, but here there is no light except what the breezes blow through the leaves.
5. **Embalmed darkness**: he cannot see the flowers but guesses each by its scent: hawthorn, eglantine, violets, the musk-rose, "The murmurous haunt of flies on summer eves".
6. **Death**: "Darkling I listen; and, for many a time / I have been half in love with easeful Death". Now more than ever it seems rich to die at midnight with no pain while the bird pours out its soul in ecstasy. But he would become a sod, deaf to its requiem.
7. **The immortal bird**: "Thou wast not born for death, immortal Bird!" The same song was heard by emperor and clown, by Ruth "amid the alien corn", and has charmed "magic casements, opening on the foam / Of perilous seas, in faery lands forlorn".
8. **Return**: "Forlorn! the very word is like a bell / To toll me back from thee to my sole self!" Fancy cannot cheat as well as she is said to. The song fades past the meadows and over the hill. "Was it a vision, or a waking dream? / Fled is that music:—Do I wake or sleep?"

### Themes
1. **Pleasure and pain intertwined** (2019): the opening ache is caused by happiness; death is "easeful" and "rich"; the joy of the song sharpens awareness of human suffering. Compare "Ode on Melancholy": "in the very temple of Delight / Veil'd Melancholy has her sovran shrine."
2. **Escape and its failure**: three means of escape (wine, poetry, death) are tried; none lasts.
3. **Art and permanence vs human mortality**: the bird's song as an emblem of timeless art.
4. **Imagination and reality**: the closing questions leave both open. This is **negative capability** in practice.
5. **Death**: personal (Tom; his own illness) and philosophical.

### Sensuousness (2021)
- Keats's poetry appeals to all five senses, often at once (**synaesthesia**): "Tasting of Flora and the country green" (taste with colour and sight); "embalmed darkness" (smell and sight); the "murmurous haunt of flies" (sound).
- Thick, slow vowels and consonants; heavy adjectives ("full-throated", "purple-stained mouth").
- But sensuousness serves thought: the senses lead to meditation on mortality. **Matthew Arnold** praised Keats's "natural magic" and argued that he had "flint and iron" in him too, not just luxury.
- Also in "The Eve of St Agnes" (the feast of "candied apple, quince, and plum") and "To Autumn".

### Critical views
- **Helen Vendler** (*The Odes of John Keats*, 1983): the odes form a sequence working out the relation between art, beauty and transience; "Nightingale" tests escape through imagination and finds it inadequate; "To Autumn" is the resolution.
- **Earl Wasserman** (*The Finer Tone*, 1953): the poem moves towards a visionary "fellowship with essence" and is pulled back by mortal limits.
- **Cleanth Brooks** and the New Critics valued the odes' paradox and tension.
- Some critics find the bird's "immortality" illogical (individual birds die); the usual answer: Keats means the species and its song, unchanged across history, as against the individual human.

## 3.6 "To Autumn" (19 September 1819)

Asked: 2021 (with Keats generally), 2022 (classical ode).

### Facts
- Written at Winchester after a Sunday walk; he wrote to Reynolds that the stubble fields looked warm, like a picture. Three stanzas of eleven lines (abab cdecdde pattern in the first stanza; the sestet varies). The last ode he wrote.

### Summary
1. **Stanza 1 — ripeness (morning, the season's fullness).** "Season of mists and mellow fruitfulness, / Close bosom-friend of the maturing sun"; they conspire to load the vines, bend apple trees, fill fruit with ripeness to the core, swell the gourd, plump the hazel shells, and set budding late flowers for the bees, until they think warm days will never cease.
2. **Stanza 2 — Autumn personified (midday, the harvest).** Autumn is seen sitting careless on a granary floor, hair lifted by the winnowing wind; asleep on a half-reaped furrow, drowsed with the fume of poppies; like a gleaner keeping steady her laden head across a brook; by a cider-press watching "the last oozings hours by hours".
3. **Stanza 3 — music (evening, the dying year).** "Where are the songs of Spring? Ay, where are they? / Think not of them, thou hast thy music too." Barred clouds bloom the soft-dying day; gnats mourn in a wailful choir; lambs bleat; hedge-crickets sing; the redbreast whistles; "gathering swallows twitter in the skies" (preparing to migrate).

### Themes
- **Acceptance of time and death**: the poem moves from ripeness to harvest to departure, and from morning to evening, without protest. Unlike "Nightingale", there is no escape attempt and no "I".
- **Fulfilment**: maturity as completion; "ripeness is all" (a phrase from *King Lear*, often linked to this ode).
- **Process and cycle**: the year turning; the hint of winter in the swallows.

### Critical views
- **Geoffrey Hartman** ("Poem and Ideology: A Study of Keats's 'To Autumn'", 1975): an English ode that refuses grand vision; a poem of "enstasis" (staying with what is).
- **Jerome McGann** (1979, New Historicist): the poem was written weeks after the Peterloo Massacre (16 August 1819) and turns away from politics into a timeless pastoral; its "escape" is ideological.
- **Andrew Motion** (biographer) and others answer that the poem's references to harvest, gleaning and enclosure show awareness of rural labour. [verify]
- Often called the most perfect short poem in English (for example by Harold Bloom and Douglas Bush).

## 3.7 Alfred, Lord Tennyson (1809–1892): background

- Rector's son, Lincolnshire. Cambridge "Apostles"; friendship with **Arthur Henry Hallam**, who died suddenly in Vienna in 1833 aged 22. Grief shaped his work for 17 years: *In Memoriam A.H.H.* (1850). Poet Laureate 1850–92. *Idylls of the King* (Arthurian, 1859–85). Baron 1884.
- Representative Victorian: technical mastery of sound; doubt and faith; duty; the past (classical and Arthurian) used to think about the present.

## 3.8 "Ulysses" (written 1833, published 1842)

Asked: 2022 (classical debt).

### Facts
- 70 lines of blank verse. Dramatic monologue. Written in October 1833, weeks after Hallam's death. Tennyson said it expressed "the need of going forward and braving the struggle of life" more simply than *In Memoriam*.

### Summary
1. **Lines 1–32, to himself or the audience**: Ulysses, back in Ithaca, is an "idle king" by a still hearth, "Match'd with an aged wife", handing out laws to a "savage race" that does not know him. He cannot rest from travel: "I will drink / Life to the lees." He has enjoyed and suffered greatly, with friends and alone; he has become a name; he has drunk delight of battle with his peers on the windy plains of Troy. "I am a part of all that I have met". Experience is an arch through which gleams an untravelled world whose margin fades for ever as he moves. "How dull it is to pause, to make an end, / To rust unburnish'd, not to shine in use!" Life piled on life is too little; he wants "To follow knowledge like a sinking star, / Beyond the utmost bound of human thought."
2. **Lines 33–43, about Telemachus**: his son will rule; he is prudent, patient, "decent not to fail / In offices of tenderness", and will civilise the rugged people. "He works his work, I mine."
3. **Lines 44–70, to his mariners**: the port, the vessel, the dark sea. Old age has its honour and toil. "Death closes all: but something ere the end, / Some work of noble note, may yet be done". "Come, my friends, / 'Tis not too late to seek a newer world." He will sail beyond the sunset; perhaps the gulfs will wash them down, perhaps they will reach the Happy Isles and see great Achilles. Though much is taken, much remains: "that which we are, we are; / One equal temper of heroic hearts, / Made weak by time and fate, but strong in will / To strive, to seek, to find, and not to yield."

### Classical and other sources (2022 question)
| Source | What Tennyson takes |
|---|---|
| **Homer, *Odyssey*** (8th c. BC) | Ulysses (Odysseus), Ithaca, Penelope, Telemachus, the Trojan War, Achilles; in Book 11 the prophet **Tiresias** foretells a last journey and a gentle death "from the sea" |
| **Dante, *Inferno*, Canto 26** (c.1314) | The most direct source: Ulysses, in the eighth circle among the false counsellors, tells how he left home in old age and urged his few companions to sail beyond the Pillars of Hercules, "to follow virtue and knowledge", and drowned. Tennyson's speech to the mariners is modelled on this "little oration" |
| **Homeric heroic code** | Glory, fame, comradeship in arms, defiance of death |
| **Shakespeare, *Troilus and Cressida*** | Ulysses's speech on honour and rusting ("perseverance… keeps honour bright") is a likely echo [critics debate this] |
| **Victorian** | The ideal of energy, work and progress; Hallam's death; Tennyson's own grief |

- **Verdict**: the story and setting are Homeric, the spirit of the final voyage is Dantean, and the moral (strive in spite of loss) is Victorian. Homer's Odysseus longs for home; Tennyson's Ulysses longs to leave it.

### Themes
Restless striving; old age and death; the value of experience; duty vs desire; the hero's isolation; grief turned into resolve.

### Critical views
- **Heroic reading** (traditional): the poem is a Victorian anthem of courage. Tennyson's own comment supports it.
- **Ironic reading** (modern critics such as E.J. Chiasson, 1954, and many since): Ulysses is selfish and irresponsible; he scorns his wife and people, abandons his duties, and in Dante he is in Hell for false counsel. The poem may expose the egotism of the heroic ideal.
- **T.S. Eliot** called it a perfect poem [verify]; **Christopher Ricks** (*Tennyson*, 1972) analyses its sound and its link with grief.
- Line 70 is the motto of the 2012 London Olympics village and of many schools; in the 2012 film *Skyfall* it is recited.

### Exam link
- Contrast with Maugham's "Lotus-Eater" (Tennyson also wrote "The Lotos-Eaters"; Wilson is the lotus-eater, Ulysses the anti-lotus-eater); compare with Iqbal's restless *mard-e-momin* and his praise of constant striving.

## 3.9 "The Lady of Shalott" (1832, revised 1842)

Not yet asked in Part II.

### Facts
- Four parts; 19 nine-line stanzas in the 1842 version, rhyme aaaabcccb, mostly iambic tetrameter with refrains "Camelot" and "The Lady of Shalott". Source: an Italian novella (*Donna di Scalotta*), related to Malory's Elaine of Astolat.

### Summary
1. **Part I**: fields of barley and rye by a river that runs down to Camelot. On an island stand four grey walls and four grey towers where the Lady lives. Only reapers hear her singing at dawn and call her a fairy.
2. **Part II**: she weaves a magic web night and day. She has heard a curse will fall on her if she looks down to Camelot; she does not know what it is. She sees the world only as shadows in a mirror: villagers, a troop of damsels, an abbot, shepherds, knights riding two by two, funerals, and lovers newly wed. "'I am half sick of shadows,' said / The Lady of Shalott."
3. **Part III**: Sir Lancelot rides by, dazzling in the sun, singing "Tirra lirra". She leaves the loom, looks down to Camelot; the web flies out, the mirror cracks: "'The curse is come upon me,' cried / The Lady of Shalott."
4. **Part IV**: in a stormy autumn she finds a boat, writes her name on the prow, lies down in a white robe and floats to Camelot singing her last song until her blood freezes. The knights and citizens are afraid; Lancelot muses that she has a lovely face and asks God to lend her grace.

### Themes and readings
- **The artist's dilemma**: art made in isolation, at one remove from life (the mirror), versus direct experience, which destroys the art and the artist. Victorian debate about the poet's social duty.
- **Gender**: a woman confined to an enclosed space and to reflected experience; her move into the public world is punished. Feminist readers see the Victorian ideal of the domestic woman (Coventry Patmore's "Angel in the House") and its cost. **Isobel Armstrong** (*Victorian Poetry*, 1993) reads Tennyson's early poems as exploring the conditions and costs of art [verify].
- **Reality vs illusion**: shadows vs substance (Plato's cave).
- Influence: Pre-Raphaelite painters (J.W. Waterhouse, *The Lady of Shalott*, 1888; Holman Hunt).
- Exam link: compare with Keats's "Nightingale" (imagination vs reality) and with Hester Prynne (isolation; needlework as art).

## 3.10 W.B. Yeats (1865–1939): background

- Dublin-born, Anglo-Irish Protestant. Irish Literary Revival; co-founded the Abbey Theatre (1904). Unrequited love for Maud Gonne. Senator of the Irish Free State (1922–28). **Nobel Prize 1923**.
- Phases: early Celtic Twilight romanticism; middle period of public poems ("Easter, 1916"); late visionary and bodily poems (*The Tower*, 1928; *The Winding Stair*, 1933).
- Occultism; with his wife George's "automatic writing" built the system of ***A Vision*** (1925): history moves in **gyres** (interlocking cones) of about 2,000 years; each era is born from the opposite of the last; the Christian era was ending.
- Lived in a Norman tower, **Thoor Ballylee**, in County Galway (the "winding stair").

## 3.11 "The Second Coming" (written 1919, published 1920)

Asked: 2016 (opposing forces in history; modern vs ancient). MCQ 2026.

### Facts
- Published in *The Dial* (November 1920); in *Michael Robartes and the Dancer* (1921). 22 lines of loose iambic pentameter in two verse-paragraphs (8 + 14), with little rhyme.

### Summary
1. **Part 1 (8 lines): collapse.** A falcon turns in a widening gyre and cannot hear the falconer. "Things fall apart; the centre cannot hold; / Mere anarchy is loosed upon the world". A blood-dimmed tide drowns the ceremony of innocence everywhere. "The best lack all conviction, while the worst / Are full of passionate intensity."
2. **Part 2 (14 lines): revelation.** Surely some revelation, the Second Coming, is at hand. A vast image out of *Spiritus Mundi* (the world's collective memory) troubles his sight: in desert sands a shape with lion body and the head of a man (a sphinx), with a gaze blank and pitiless as the sun, moving its slow thighs while indignant desert birds wheel around it. Darkness drops again; but now he knows that twenty centuries of stony sleep were "vexed to nightmare by a rocking cradle" (the birth of Christ), "And what rough beast, its hour come round at last, / Slouches towards Bethlehem to be born?"

### Context
- First World War (1914–18); Russian Revolution (1917) and civil war; the Easter Rising (1916) and the Irish War of Independence (1919–21) with Black and Tan violence. An early draft mentioned Burke and Pitt and the Germans in Russia.

### Themes
- **Cyclical history** (gyres): the Christian era (2,000 years, born of the cradle in Bethlehem) is ending; an opposite, violent, "antithetical" era is being born.
- **Opposing forces** (2016): order/anarchy; innocence/blood; conviction/intensity; the Christian (gentle, "primary") vs the pagan or bestial ("antithetical"); the ancient (sphinx, desert) returning to replace the modern.
- **Apocalypse inverted**: the "Second Coming" is not Christ's return but a monster's birth.
- **Moral crisis of modern politics**: the decent are paralysed; fanatics are energetic.

### Critical views
- **Richard Ellmann** (*Yeats: The Man and the Masks*, 1948): Yeats used his private system to give shape to public catastrophe.
- **Helen Vendler** (*Our Secret Discipline*, 2007): the poem's form, a broken sonnet in its second part, enacts its theme of collapsing order. [verify]
- **Harold Bloom** (*Yeats*, 1970) is sceptical of the occult system but admires the poem's power.
- **Chinua Achebe** took the title *Things Fall Apart* (1958) from line 3, and his epigraph from the poem, turning it into a postcolonial statement: the "centre" that cannot hold is Igbo society under colonial invasion. Use this in postcolonial answers.
- The poem is among the most quoted in political journalism, especially "the best lack all conviction".

## 3.12 "A Dialogue of Self and Soul" (written 1927, published 1933)

Not yet asked in Part II. In copyright in the US: paraphrase only.

### Facts
- In *The Winding Stair and Other Poems* (1933). Two parts: Part I a dialogue (eight-line stanzas), Part II the Self's monologue.

### Summary (paraphrase)
- **Part I.** **My Soul** summons the speaker to the ancient winding stair of the tower, to fix his mind on the night sky and the darkness beyond thought, which alone can free him from "the crime of death and birth" (the cycle of reincarnation). **My Self** holds instead **Sato's ancient sword** (a real gift from a Japanese admirer, Junzo Sato, 1920), still razor-sharp and bright after five hundred years, wrapped in embroidered silk torn from a court lady's dress. These are emblems of day, love and war, set against the tower, emblem of night. The Self claims a soldier's right to commit the "crime" of rebirth once more. The Soul answers that in the fullness of heavenly vision a man becomes deaf, dumb and blind, and his tongue turns to stone.
- **Part II.** The Self, alone, declares he is content to live it all again, even if it means the humiliations of youth, the distress of growing up, being misjudged by others, and loving a proud woman who does not love him back: like being thrown into the frog-spawn of a blind man's ditch. He will trace every event to its source and forgive himself the lot. When a man casts out remorse, so great a sweetness flows into the breast that we must laugh and sing; we are blessed by everything, and everything we look upon is blessed.

### Themes
- **Body/action vs spirit/renunciation**: the Self (life, history, love, war, rebirth) vs the Soul (contemplation, escape from the cycle).
- **Affirmation of life**: unusually, the Self wins. Compare "Sailing to Byzantium" (1928), where the speaker wants to leave the body for the "artifice of eternity".
- **Self-forgiveness and joy**: casting out remorse.
- Symbols: tower and winding stair (contemplation; Yeats's home), sword and silk (war and love; male and female; East and West; art that survives time).

### Critical view
- **Richard Ellmann** and **A. Norman Jeffares** read it as Yeats's late choice of passionate life over ascetic transcendence, a "tragic joy". Use with Nietzsche's eternal recurrence (to will one's life again).

## 3.13 T.S. Eliot (1888–1965): background

- Born St Louis, Missouri; Harvard (philosophy; studied Sanskrit and Indian philosophy); settled in England 1914; married Vivienne Haigh-Wood (1915), an unhappy marriage; worked at Lloyds Bank (1917–25), then Faber and Faber. British subject and Anglican (1927). **Nobel Prize 1948**.
- **Criticism**: "Tradition and the Individual Talent" (1919): the poet's personality is less important than tradition; poetry is "an escape from personality"; impersonality; the catalyst analogy. "Hamlet and His Problems" (1919): the **objective correlative**. "The Metaphysical Poets" (1921): Donne felt his thought "as immediately as the odour of a rose"; a **dissociation of sensibility** set in during the 17th century (after Milton and Dryden) and thought and feeling have been split since. "Ulysses, Order, and Myth" (1923): the **mythical method**, a way of controlling and giving shape to the futility and anarchy of contemporary history.
- Influences: French Symbolists (Jules Laforgue's ironic monologues), Dante, Jacobean drama, the Metaphysical poets, Ezra Pound.

## 3.14 "The Love Song of J. Alfred Prufrock" (1915/1917)

Asked: 2020 (unified sensibility, with *Waste Land*), 2021 (passion vs timidity), 2025 (with Eliot's poetry), 2026 (foil to an ideal community).

### Facts
- Written 1910–11; published in *Poetry* (Chicago, June 1915) through Pound; title poem of *Prufrock and Other Observations* (1917). 131 lines; free verse with irregular rhyme and line lengths. **Dramatic monologue.**
- **Epigraph**: Dante, *Inferno* 27: Guido da Montefeltro, wrapped in flame, speaks only because he believes no one will return from Hell to repeat his words. So Prufrock's "song" is a confession not meant for the world.

### Summary
1. **The invitation**: "Let us go then, you and I, / When the evening is spread out against the sky / Like a patient etherised upon a table". Through half-deserted streets, cheap hotels and sawdust restaurants, streets like a tedious argument leading to an "overwhelming question". "In the room the women come and go / Talking of Michelangelo."
2. **The yellow fog**, like a cat, rubs against the windows and curls about the house.
3. **"There will be time"**: time to prepare a face to meet the faces, for a hundred indecisions, visions and revisions, before the taking of toast and tea.
4. **Self-consciousness**: "Do I dare / Disturb the universe?" His bald spot; his thin arms and legs; "I have measured out my life with coffee spoons". He already knows the voices, the eyes that fix him "in a formulated phrase", pinned and wriggling on the wall; the arms, braceleted and white, downed with light brown hair.
5. **Lonely men**: he has watched lonely men in shirt-sleeves leaning out of windows. "I should have been a pair of ragged claws / Scuttling across the floors of silent seas."
6. **Failure of nerve**: he has seen his head brought in upon a platter (John the Baptist), but he is no prophet; he has seen "the eternal Footman hold my coat, and snicker, / And in short, I was afraid." Would it have been worth it to squeeze the universe into a ball, to say "I am Lazarus, come from the dead", if she replied "That is not what I meant at all"?
7. **Not Hamlet**: "No! I am not Prince Hamlet, nor was meant to be; / Am an attendant lord", deferential, cautious, at times almost the Fool.
8. **Ending**: "I grow old… I grow old… / I shall wear the bottoms of my trousers rolled." He will walk on the beach; he has heard the mermaids singing, each to each; "I do not think that they will sing to me." "We have lingered in the chambers of the sea… / Till human voices wake us, and we drown."

### Themes
1. **Paralysis and indecision**: the "overwhelming question" (a proposal of love? a declaration of meaning?) is never asked.
2. **Passion vs timidity** (2021): desire (the women's arms, perfume, mermaids) against fear of ridicule, self-consciousness and ageing.
3. **Alienation in modern urban society**: polite drawing rooms, trivial talk, "coffee spoons"; the individual cut off from others.
4. **Time and ageing**: "There will be time" turns into "I grow old".
5. **The divided self**: "you and I" may be two sides of Prufrock.
6. **Anti-heroism**: Prufrock measures himself against Lazarus, John the Baptist, Hamlet and Michelangelo and shrinks.

### Style
- **Dramatic monologue** (after Browning) but interior, closer to stream of consciousness.
- **Shocking imagery**: the etherised patient (romantic evening turned clinical).
- **Allusion**: Dante, Hesiod's *Works and Days* ("works and days of hands"), Marvell ("To His Coy Mistress": "squeezed the universe into a ball" echoes Marvell's "roll all our strength… into one ball"), the Bible, Shakespeare (*Hamlet*, *Twelfth Night*'s "dying fall").
- **Irony and bathos**: grand questions undercut by trousers and peaches.
- Refrain and repetition; irregular rhyme that comes and goes like Prufrock's courage.

### Critical views
- **Hugh Kenner** (*The Invisible Poet*, 1959): Prufrock is less a character than a voice, a "name plus a voice", a consciousness made of fragments.
- **Christopher Ricks** (*T.S. Eliot and Prejudice*, 1988) analyses the poem's embarrassment and social unease.
- **The 2026 claim** (a foil to an ideal community): one can argue the poem implies, by absence, a fuller human community (true communication, love, spiritual meaning) that the drawing room parodies. Eliot's later turn to Christianity and his ideal of an organic Christian society (*The Idea of a Christian Society*, 1939) supports this. Counter-argument: the poem itself offers no positive image except the mermaids, which exclude him.

### Unified sensibility in Eliot's own poems (2020)
- Eliot praised Donne's fusion of thought and feeling (see 3.13). In "Prufrock", thought and feeling are fused in images (the etherised evening; the coffee spoons; the claws) that are at once intellectual and emotional, so yes, in technique. But the *subject* of the poem is a dissociated man who cannot act on feeling, so the poem dramatises dissociation through a unified method. Same for *The Waste Land*.

## 3.15 *The Waste Land* (1922)

Asked: 2017 (trauma of war), 2018 (collapse of belief in order and the sublime), 2020 (unified sensibility), 2025 ("mystery religion"; Christian, myth and global allusion).

### Facts
- Published October 1922 in *The Criterion* (London) and November in *The Dial* (New York); book with notes, December 1922. 434 lines in five parts. Dedicated to **Ezra Pound**, "il miglior fabbro" ("the better craftsman"), who cut the draft heavily (the drafts were published in facsimile in 1971).
- Written partly while Eliot was recovering from a breakdown at Margate and Lausanne (1921).
- **Epigraph**: from Petronius's *Satyricon*: the Sibyl of Cumae, granted long life but not youth, hangs in a jar and, asked what she wants, says "I want to die".
- **Sources named in Eliot's notes**: Jessie L. Weston's *From Ritual to Romance* (1920) (the Grail legend and the Fisher King, a wounded king whose land is barren until a quester asks the right question) and Frazer's *The Golden Bough* (fertility gods who die and are reborn: Adonis, Attis, Osiris).

### Summary by part
1. **"The Burial of the Dead"**: "April is the cruellest month", because it stirs dead roots with spring rain; memory and desire. Marie, a German aristocrat, remembers sledding as a child. A prophetic voice: "a heap of broken images", the dry stone, fear in a handful of dust. The hyacinth girl and a moment of failed love. Madame Sosostris, a fortune-teller with a wicked pack of cards: the drowned Phoenician sailor, the hanged man missing. The "Unreal City": a crowd flows over London Bridge in brown winter fog; "I had not thought death had undone so many" (Dante). The speaker greets Stetson, a comrade from the ancient battle of Mylae, and asks whether the corpse he planted in his garden has sprouted.
2. **"A Game of Chess"**: a rich woman in a heavy, perfumed room (allusions to Cleopatra and Dido); neurotic dialogue with a silent man: "My nerves are bad to-night." Then a London pub at closing time: a woman gossips about Lil, whose husband Albert is back from the army; Lil, aged by pills taken to end a pregnancy. The barman's call "HURRY UP PLEASE ITS TIME".
3. **"The Fire Sermon"**: the Thames in autumn, the nymphs departed; Spenser's refrain "Sweet Thames, run softly, till I end my song". Rats, bones, the Fisher King fishing in the dull canal. Mr Eugenides, a merchant from Smyrna, invites the speaker to a weekend. **Tiresias**, the blind seer of both sexes, watches a typist and a "young man carbuncular" have joyless, mechanical sex. The Thames daughters' songs of seduction. Ending: "To Carthage then I came" (Augustine's *Confessions*) and "Burning burning burning burning" (the Buddha's Fire Sermon against desire).
4. **"Death by Water"** (10 lines): Phlebas the Phoenician, dead a fortnight, forgot the cry of gulls and the profit and loss; a current picked his bones. A warning to all who turn the wheel.
5. **"What the Thunder Said"**: after the agony in the garden (Christ's arrest), a journey through rock and no water (the road to Emmaus, where a hooded third figure walks beside the travellers). Hooded hordes swarming over plains; falling towers: "Jerusalem Athens Alexandria / Vienna London / Unreal". The empty chapel (the Grail's Chapel Perilous). A cock crows; rain comes. Over the Himalaya, thunder speaks: **DA**, interpreted as **Datta** (give), **Dayadhvam** (sympathise), **Damyata** (control), from the *Brihadaranyaka Upanishad*. The Fisher King sits on the shore, his arid plain behind him: "Shall I at least set my lands in order?" A collage of fragments in several languages: "These fragments I have shored against my ruins". The close: "Shantih shantih shantih", the formal ending of an Upanishad, "the Peace which passeth understanding".

### Themes
1. **Sterility and spiritual drought**: the waste land of the Grail legend as modern Europe; failed sex and failed love throughout.
2. **Death and rebirth**: buried gods, the corpse in the garden, water as both drowning and rain; resurrection is hoped for, not achieved.
3. **War and its aftermath** (2017): the crowd of the dead on London Bridge; Albert demobbed; Mylae (an ancient war standing for the recent one); hooded hordes and falling cities (the Russian Revolution, the collapse of empires). But the poem rarely names the 1914–18 war directly; it shows a civilisation's trauma.
4. **Collapse of order and the sublime** (2018): fragments instead of a whole; myth survives only as broken allusion; the sacred (the chapel) is empty.
5. **Search for meaning across cultures** (2025): Christian (Christ, Emmaus, Augustine), pagan (fertility cults, Tiresias), Hindu (the Upanishad), Buddhist (Fire Sermon).

### Form and technique
- **Collage/fragmentation**; many voices (Eliot's working title was "He Do the Police in Different Voices", from Dickens's *Our Mutual Friend*).
- **Mythical method**: the Grail and fertility myths give a buried structure.
- **Allusion and quotation** in six or more languages (English, German, French, Italian, Latin, Greek, Sanskrit).
- **Tiresias as unifying consciousness**: Eliot's note says what Tiresias sees is the substance of the poem.
- Varied rhythm: blank verse, song, pub dialogue, free verse.

### Critical views
- **I.A. Richards** (*Science and Poetry*, 1926): the poem achieves "a complete separation between his poetry and all beliefs", expressing the plight of a generation; Eliot disagreed.
- **F.R. Leavis** (*New Bearings in English Poetry*, 1932): the poem is the great modern poem; its unity is a unity of mood, not of argument.
- **Cleanth Brooks** ("The Waste Land: Critique of the Myth", 1939): a Christian reading; the poem is about the possibility of spiritual rebirth; its method is irony.
- **Eliot himself** later dismissed it as the relief of a personal and wholly insignificant grouse against life (reported remark), pointing to the private, marital and nervous sources.
- **Feminist and biographical critics** (e.g. Lyndall Gordon, *Eliot's Early Years*, 1977) read its sexual disgust in the light of his marriage.

### "Mystery religion" (2025)
- Mystery religions: Greek and Near Eastern cults with secret rites of death and rebirth (Eleusis; Isis and Osiris; Attis; Mithras). Frazer and Weston argued the Grail legend and even Christian rites descend from such fertility cults.
- The poem layers these over Christian imagery (the agony in the garden, Emmaus, the "third who walks always beside you") and Eastern scripture. The result is not orthodox Christianity but a comparative, syncretic sense of the sacred in which the Christian moral virtues (giving, compassion, self-control: the thunder's three words) are recovered through myth. Five years later Eliot was baptised an Anglican (1927).
- **Counter-view**: in 1922 the poem shows the failure of all these religions to revive the land; the peace at the end is formal and uncertain.

## 3.16 Philip Larkin (1922–1985): three poems

Never asked in Part II; Larkin appeared in the 2026 MCQs ("The Trees", "Continuing to Live"). Larkin is in copyright: paraphrase only. Read the poems in *Collected Poems* (ed. Anthony Thwaite, 1988; or Archie Burnett's *Complete Poems*, 2012).

### Background
- Librarian at the University of Hull (1955–85). Leading poet of **the Movement** (see file 00). Collections: *The North Ship* (1945), *The Less Deceived* (1955), *The Whitsun Weddings* (1964), *High Windows* (1974). Declined the Poet Laureateship (1984).
- Themes: death, time, failed love, ordinary English life, religion's decline; tone of irony, honesty and bleak humour; traditional metre and rhyme in plain speech.
- Critics: **Andrew Motion** (*Philip Larkin: A Writer's Life*, 1993); **Seamus Heaney** ("The Main of Light", 1982) on Larkin's moments of transcendence despite his scepticism; **James Booth** on Larkin's humanism; feminist and postcolonial critics attacked the racism and misogyny revealed in his *Selected Letters* (1992).

### "The Trees" (written June 1967; in *High Windows*, 1974)
- **Form**: three quatrains, rhyme abba, iambic tetrameter.
- **Paraphrase**: the trees coming into leaf in spring are like something almost being said; their new green is itself a kind of grief. Are they born again while we grow old? No, they die too; their yearly trick of looking new is recorded in the rings of their wood. Yet every May the full-grown trees thresh with new thickness, seeming to say: last year is dead, begin again, again.
- **Themes**: renewal vs mortality; nature's illusion of youth; the human wish to begin afresh despite knowing better. The final repetition is half consolation, half self-deception.
- **Angle**: compare with Keats's "To Autumn" (acceptance of the cycle) and Wordsworth (nature as consolation). Larkin's nature consoles only through a "trick".

### "Continuing to Live" (written April 1954; first collected posthumously)
- **Paraphrase**: to go on living is to repeat a habit formed to get what we need; it mostly means losing or doing without. Each of us, once we have explored the length of our own mind, knows what we command as clearly as a cargo list; anything else, for us, might as well not exist. And the gain? Only that in time we half-identify the blind impress that all our actions bear, and may trace it to its source. But to confess, at the moment death begins, what it was is hardly satisfying, since it applied only to one man, once, and he is dying.
- **Themes**: the narrowness of individual life; character as an unchosen pattern ("blind impress"); the uselessness of self-knowledge gained too late; death.
- **Critic**: the philosopher **Richard Rorty** opens chapter 2 of *Contingency, Irony, and Solidarity* (1989) with this poem, using it to discuss the contingency of selfhood and the fear of dying with only a private, unrepeatable pattern to show for one's life. A strong, little-known reference.
- **Link**: compare Prufrock (a life measured out in habit) and Maugham's Wilson (the pattern of a life revealed only at its end).

### "Maturity" (dated 1951; first collected posthumously in *Collected Poems*, 1988)
- A short early poem. **Paraphrase** (check against the text [verify]): the speaker senses that what is called the prime of life is really the start of decline. Maturity is not a stable summit but an awareness of the body and mind beginning to weaken, accepted with a wince rather than celebrated.
- **Themes**: ageing; disillusion with the conventional idea of maturity as fulfilment; Larkin's typical deflation of comforting words.
- **Link**: contrast with Keats's "ripeness" in "To Autumn" and with Tennyson's old Ulysses, who refuses to accept decline.

## 3.17 Wallace Stevens (1879–1955) — option A

Either Stevens or Whitman. Never asked in Part II. Stevens is in copyright in the US: paraphrase only.

### Background
- American; Harvard; lawyer and vice-president of the Hartford Accident and Indemnity Company. First book *Harmonium* (1923) at 44. Pulitzer 1955. A philosophical poet of imagination and reality: the mind orders the world through imagination ("supreme fictions"); after the death of God, poetry must supply meaning.
- Critics: **Harold Bloom** (*Wallace Stevens: The Poems of Our Climate*, 1977) places him in the Emersonian line; **Helen Vendler** (*On Extended Wings*, 1969).

### "A Postcard from the Volcano" (*Ideas of Order*, 1936)
- **Paraphrase**: children picking up our bones in the future will never know that these were once as quick as foxes on the hill. They will not know how we felt about autumn, the grapes and the air. Yet we leave behind more than bones: our way of seeing has shaped how the world looks; the old house we leave will seem to future children to speak of what we felt, shuttered and decaying, "a dirty house in a gutted world", yet still lit by the opulent sun.
- **Themes**: mortality; the legacy of perception (each generation's imagination colours the world it hands on); culture as a ruined mansion; the title's image: a message from a buried civilisation (Pompeii and Vesuvius).
- **Link**: compare Shelley's "Ozymandias"; *Heartbreak House* (a decaying culture).

### "Dry Loaf" (*Parts of a World*, 1942)
- **Paraphrase**: to live in a tragic time is like living in a tragic land. The speaker paints a landscape of rocks and birds flying, a dry loaf and water; then soldiers march over the rocks, and birds keep coming in waves, like water. The painting and the history merge; it was soldiers, it was birds, it was the poor (the dry loaf).
- **Themes**: art in wartime (written during the Second World War); the poet's attempt to paint a landscape that history (marching soldiers, hunger) keeps entering; continuity of nature's cycles beside human violence.

### "Continual Conversation with a Silent Man" (*Transport to Summer*, 1947)
- **Paraphrase**: the speaker describes a continual conversation with a silent companion (perhaps reality, the world, or the self) in a world of everyday things: an old brown hen, the sky, carts in the street. The conversation is not a voice but the sound of things themselves and their motion, a turning of the speaker's world, a song with no fixed words.
- **Themes**: the mind's dialogue with an unspeaking reality; meaning as an ongoing process; poetry as listening to the world's motion. [Paraphrase from memory; check the text before quoting details.]

## 3.18 Walt Whitman (1819–1892) — option B

Never asked in Part II. Public domain; Gutenberg has *Leaves of Grass*.

### Background
- Brooklyn journalist, printer, teacher; Civil War hospital volunteer. *Leaves of Grass* (first edition 1855, self-published; revised and enlarged until the "deathbed" edition of 1891–92). Emerson wrote to him in 1855: "I greet you at the beginning of a great career."
- **Free verse**; long cataloguing lines; parallelism; biblical rhythms; the democratic "I" that contains multitudes; celebration of body and soul, America, comradeship. The "Calamus" poems celebrate "adhesive" love between men.
- Critics: **D.H. Lawrence** (*Studies in Classic American Literature*, 1923) admired and mocked his "merging"; **Harold Bloom** calls him the centre of the American canon; **Betsy Erkkila** (*Whitman the Political Poet*, 1989).

### "As I Ponder'd in Silence" (1871; opening "Inscriptions" of *Leaves of Grass*)
- **Paraphrase**: as the poet broods over his poems, a phantom rises before him: the genius of poets of old. It demands to know what he sings, since the one theme of all great bards is war and the making of perfect soldiers. He answers that he too sings war, a longer and greater one than any: the war of life and death, of the soul, fought for the "field of the world", and that he too makes brave soldiers.
- **Themes**: redefining the epic; poetry of the inner and democratic struggle rather than military glory; poetic vocation. Compare Wordsworth democratising poetry, and Iqbal's idea of *jihad* as inner striving.

> "I too haughty Shade also sing war, and a longer and greater one than any"

### "Are You the New Person Drawn Toward Me?" (1860, "Calamus")
- **Paraphrase**: a warning to a new admirer or lover: he is surely far different from what you suppose. Do you think you will find your ideal in him? Do you think it is so easy to have him become your lover? That his friendship is unalloyed satisfaction? That he is trusty and faithful? That under this calm, tolerant manner there is no other self? Have you not thought he may be an illusion?
- **Themes**: the gap between public persona and private self; the risk of idealising; honesty in love; the reader as lover (Whitman often addresses the reader intimately).

### "This Moment Yearning and Thoughtful" (1860, "Calamus")
- **Paraphrase**: sitting alone, yearning and thoughtful, he feels that there are other men in other lands (Germany, Italy, France, Spain, and farther away in China, Russia, Japan) also yearning and thoughtful, talking other languages. If he could know them he would become attached to them as he is to men in his own land; they are wise, beautiful, benevolent; they would be his brothers and lovers.
- **Themes**: universal brotherhood across nations and languages; the democratic, cosmopolitan imagination; loneliness turned into solidarity. Compare Iqbal's universal *ummah* and Emerson's Over-Soul.
