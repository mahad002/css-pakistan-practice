# 1. Essays (syllabus component I, 10 marks)

Asked every year since 2018, sometimes twice. Five texts, all short. This is the best return on time in the whole paper.

Copyright note: Emerson (died 1882) is public domain everywhere, so short quotations are given. Russell's book (1930) is public domain in the US since 2026 and in Pakistan (life + 50 years; Russell died 1970). Orwell (died 1950) is public domain in Pakistan but not in the US, so his essays are paraphrased here, with only short phrases quoted.

## 1.1 Bertrand Russell, *The Conquest of Happiness* (1930)

Asked: 2016 (b), 2018, 2019, 2022 (with Emerson), 2023. MCQ in 2026.

### Context
- Bertrand Russell (1872–1970): mathematician, logician (*Principia Mathematica* with A.N. Whitehead, 1910–13), philosopher, pacifist (jailed in 1918 for anti-war writing), later anti-nuclear campaigner. Nobel Prize in Literature 1950.
- The book is a work of popular philosophy, written when Russell was running an experimental school (Beacon Hill) with his second wife Dora and writing for money.
- **Its stated aim (Preface):** it is not for scholars, and not for people whose misery has an obvious outside cause such as poverty or illness. It is for ordinary people in civilised countries who suffer an everyday unhappiness with no clear external cause. Russell believes much of this is curable by the right habits of thought.
- Background: interwar pessimism. Russell answers the fashionable gloom of writers such as Joseph Wood Krutch (*The Modern Temper*, 1929), who argued that science had destroyed the values that made life meaningful.

### Structure and content (17 chapters in two parts)

**Part I — Causes of unhappiness**

| Ch. | Title | Russell's argument (paraphrased) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | What makes people unhappy? | Unhappiness comes mostly from mistaken views of the world, mistaken ethics and mistaken habits of life. Its root is **self-absorption**. He names three self-absorbed types: the **sinner** (obsessed with guilt), the **narcissist** (wants admiration), and the **megalomaniac** (wants power). He says he hated life as a youth and became happier by caring less about himself and more about the world |
| 2 | Byronic unhappiness | The pose that life is meaningless and that unhappiness is a sign of wisdom. He tests Ecclesiastes and Krutch and finds their gloom is a mood, not a reasoned conclusion. The cure is activity and interest, not argument |
| 3 | Competition | The modern businessman treats life as a race and success as survival. Money and status replace enjoyment. Remedy: a balanced ideal of sane, quiet enjoyment, and education of taste |
| 4 | Boredom and excitement | A craving for constant excitement is harmful. Some boredom is necessary; a life of "fruitful monotony" is the condition of great work. Children should learn to bear dull periods |
| 5 | Fatigue | Modern fatigue is mostly nervous, caused by worry. Think about a problem fully when it is time to decide, then stop. Face the worst possible outcome calmly; it is rarely as bad as feared |
| 6 | Envy | Envy compares instead of enjoying. Napoleon envied Caesar, Caesar envied Alexander, Alexander envied Hercules, who never existed. Cure: enjoy the pleasures you meet and stop comparing |
| 7 | The sense of sin | Irrational guilt planted by moral training in early childhood. Reason should examine these feelings and replace them with a rational ethic |
| 8 | Persecution mania | Exaggerated belief that others are against you. Four maxims: your motives are less unselfish than you think; your merits are less than you think; others are less interested in you than you think; few people want to persecute you |
| 9 | Fear of public opinion | Conformity crushes. Either find a congenial social circle or become independent of others' opinion. Public opinion is most tyrannical to those who fear it |

**Part II — Causes of happiness**

| Ch. | Title | Russell's argument (paraphrased) |
|---|---|---|
| 10 | Is happiness still possible? | Yes. He separates plain, animal happiness (open to anyone) from the happiness of the educated (from work and ideas). Both depend on interest in things outside oneself |
| 11 | Zest | The most general mark of happy people. Image of eaters at a meal: the invalid who eats from duty, the epicure, the glutton, and the man with a good appetite who enjoys the meal. Zest is the healthy appetite for life |
| 12 | Affection | Being loved gives security, which allows adventure. The best affection is reciprocal and not possessive |
| 13 | The family | Parenthood can be a deep happiness if parents respect the child as a separate person instead of possessing it |
| 14 | Work | For most people, even dull work is better than idleness. Work is satisfying when it uses skill and builds something lasting |
| 15 | Impersonal interests | Hobbies and interests unrelated to one's main work give rest and a sense of proportion |
| 16 | Effort and resignation | The golden mean: effort to change what can be changed, resignation to what cannot. Resignation is not despair but a refusal to waste energy |
| 17 | The happy man | The happy man lives objectively: he has free affections and wide interests, and feels himself part of the stream of life, not a separate billiard ball |

### Themes
1. **Happiness is largely an achievement, not a gift.** Hence "conquest" in the title.
2. **Outward attention.** The core thesis: happiness grows as self-preoccupation shrinks.
3. **Reason applied to feeling.** Bad feelings (guilt, envy, fear) can be examined and reduced by thinking clearly.
4. **Critique of modern civilisation**: competition, speed, excitement, mass opinion.
5. **Balance**: effort and resignation; work and leisure; individual and society.

### Style
- Plain, witty, conversational; short paragraphs; aphorisms; everyday examples. The 2016 question called it "straight to the point".
- Secular and rationalist. Philosophical claims tied to everyday advice (asked in 2018).
- Uses gentle irony and self-revelation (his own adolescent unhappiness).

### Critical views (paraphrased)
- **Russell's own framing**: he writes as a practical moralist, not a system-builder. This is both the book's strength (accessible) and weakness (little rigour).
- **Ray Monk** (biographer, *Bertrand Russell: The Ghost of Madness*, 2000) notes a gap between the serene advice and Russell's turbulent private life in these years. Use this to argue the book is aspiration as much as autobiography. [verify exact wording]
- **Common criticism** (make it yourself): it is written for comfortable Western middle-class readers; it neglects structural causes of misery (poverty, injustice, war, illness), which Russell admits he excludes. A Marxist would say it individualises a social problem.
- **Modern support**: positive psychology (Martin Seligman; Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi's idea of "flow", 1990) confirms much of it: engagement, relationships and meaningful work predict well-being. Csikszentmihalyi's "flow" is close to Russell's "zest".

### Exam angles
- **Is happiness possible in the modern world?** (2019, 2023): yes, conditionally; the modern causes (competition, envy, boredom, public opinion) are worse today with social media, but Russell's remedies still work. Pakistani examples: exam competition (CSS itself), status envy at weddings, "log kya kahenge" (fear of public opinion), screen-driven craving for excitement.
- **Philosophy linked to daily life** (2018): show how each abstract idea becomes a habit.
- **Compare with Emerson** (2022): both trust the individual's own judgement. Difference: Emerson is mystical and inward (trust the divine within); Russell is rational and outward (forget the self, engage with the world). Emerson's self-reliance can look like the self-absorption Russell warns against.
- **Compare with Iqbal**: Iqbal's *khudi* (selfhood) asks for self-development through striving; Russell asks for self-forgetting through interest. Both reject passive gloom.
- **Compare with Maugham's Wilson** ("The Lotus-Eater"): Wilson chooses pleasure without effort or wider interests; by Russell's standard his happiness was bound to collapse.

## 1.2 George Orwell, "Politics and the English Language" (1946)

Asked: 2020 (bad habits and remedies), 2023 (language and political thought; rules). MCQs in 2026.

### Context
- Published in *Horizon* (April 1946). Orwell (Eric Blair, 1903–1950): Burma police officer (1922–27), fought in the Spanish Civil War (1936–37), BBC propagandist (1941–43), democratic socialist and anti-totalitarian.
- Written after the war, when propaganda (Nazi, Soviet and British) had shown how words could hide crimes. It prepares the ground for Newspeak in *Nineteen Eighty-Four* (1949).

### Argument, step by step
1. **Thesis.** English is in a bad way. People assume nothing can be done. Orwell disagrees: the decline has political and economic causes, but language also acts back on thought. Slovenly language makes foolish thoughts easier, and foolish thoughts make language worse. The process is **reversible**.
2. **Five specimens.** He quotes five passages (from Harold Laski, Lancelot Hogben, an American essay on psychology, a Communist pamphlet, and a letter to *Tribune*). Their common faults: **staleness of imagery** and **lack of precision**.
3. **Four tricks of bad writing:**
   - **Dying metaphors**: worn-out figures used without thinking of their meaning (e.g. "toe the line", which writers misspell as "tow the line").
   - **Operators or verbal false limbs**: a long phrase in place of a simple verb ("render inoperative" for "break"; "give rise to"); passives; "not un-" constructions.
   - **Pretentious diction**: grand words, foreign phrases and jargon used to look impressive or to dignify ugly facts.
   - **Meaningless words**: in art criticism ("living", "romantic") and politics ("fascism", "democracy", "freedom"), words used dishonestly with private definitions.
4. **The parody.** He rewrites a famous verse from Ecclesiastes ("the race is not to the swift…") into modern abstract prose. The concrete, vivid original becomes a heap of Latinate abstractions with no images.
5. **Ready-made phrases.** Modern writing is gumming together long strips of words already set in order by someone else. These phrases "think your thoughts for you" and conceal meaning even from the writer.
6. **Political language.** In our age political writing is largely the defence of the indefensible: bombing villages, deportation, purges. Such things can only be defended by euphemism, question-begging and vagueness. Examples: undefended villages bombed becomes "pacification"; peasants driven from farms becomes "transfer of population"; people imprisoned or shot becomes "elimination of unreliable elements". He sums up that political language is designed to make lies sound truthful and murder respectable.
7. **Insincerity** is the great enemy of clear language: when there is a gap between real and declared aims, a writer turns instinctively to long words and exhausted idioms.
8. **Remedies.** The scrupulous writer asks four questions: What am I trying to say? What words will express it? What image or idiom will make it clearer? Is this image fresh enough to have an effect? And two more: Could I put it more shortly? Have I said anything that is avoidably ugly?
9. **Six rules** (learn these exactly, in substance):
   1. Never use a metaphor, simile or figure of speech you are used to seeing in print.
   2. Never use a long word where a short one will do.
   3. If it is possible to cut a word out, cut it out.
   4. Never use the passive where you can use the active.
   5. Never use a foreign phrase, scientific word or jargon word if you can think of an everyday English equivalent.
   6. Break any of these rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous.
10. **Modest close.** He admits he commits the faults himself. He does not want a "standard English" or a ban on new words; he wants language to be an instrument for expressing thought, not concealing it. One can at least stop using worn phrases and laugh them out of existence.

### Themes
- Language and thought shape each other (a soft form of linguistic determinism; compare the Sapir–Whorf hypothesis).
- Clarity as a moral and political duty.
- Euphemism as the servant of power.
- Individual responsibility: each writer can resist.

### Style
- Practises what it preaches: concrete images, short words, active verbs, humour, examples first and theory second. Famous self-mocking example of the "not un-" habit: a not unblack dog chasing a not unsmall rabbit across a not ungreen field.

### Critical views
- **Geoffrey Pullum** (linguist) argues Orwell's rules are naive and that Orwell breaks them himself: the essay uses the passive heavily, and grammar does not cause dishonesty. A good balanced point for "evaluate" questions.
- **Steven Pinker** (*The Sense of Style*, 2014) praises the plain-style ideal but rejects mechanical rules.
- **Raymond Williams** (*Orwell*, 1971) admires Orwell's plain style but notes that "plainness" is itself a style with its own ideology: it can present opinion as common sense.
- Many teachers of writing (and the CSS précis paper) still use the six rules.

### Exam angles
- **Bad habits and remedies** (2020): four tricks + six rules + the four questions. Give one modern example per habit.
- **Language and political thought** (2023): euphemism; "meaningless words"; control of thought; link to Newspeak and to Orwell's line in *1984* that the aim of Newspeak is to narrow the range of thought.
- **Modern examples:** "collateral damage" (civilian deaths), "enhanced interrogation" (torture), "rightsizing" (sacking workers), "alternative facts". Pakistan: bureaucratic English ("it is submitted that the needful may kindly be done", "in pursuance of"), political euphemisms such as calling enforced disappearances "missing persons".
- **Relevance to CSS**: Orwell's rules are exactly what FPSC examiners ask for in the Essay and Précis papers. Say so in the conclusion.

## 1.3 George Orwell, "The Prevention of Literature" (1946)

Not yet asked in Part II. Learn it as a companion to *1984* and "Politics and the English Language".

### Context
- Published in *Polemic* (January 1946). Orwell attended a PEN Club meeting marking the 300th anniversary of Milton's *Areopagitica* (Milton's 1644 defence of a free press). He was struck that the speakers attacked censorship in general but avoided criticising the Soviet Union, which many British intellectuals admired.

### Argument
1. **The new enemies of intellectual liberty** are not only governments. They include: press monopolies; the concentration of radio and film in a few hands; the public's indifference; bureaucracy (wartime ministries); and, most dangerous, **intellectuals themselves** who accept lies for a political cause.
2. **Two threats**: the theoretical defence of totalitarianism, and its practical defenders among the intelligentsia (he means Communist sympathisers who excused the Moscow trials and purges).
3. **Totalitarianism and truth.** A totalitarian state must keep rewriting the past, because its leaders claim infallibility. It demands constant changes of doctrine, and so it attacks the very idea of objective truth. (This becomes the Ministry of Truth in *1984*.)
4. **Why prose dies first.** Prose literature (novels, essays, history) needs intellectual honesty and freedom of thought. A writer who lies about facts or suppresses his own feelings destroys his creative power. **Poetry** might survive better, because a poem's meaning is less tied to statements of fact, and some forms (simple songs, collective verse) can flourish even under tyranny.
5. **Prediction.** Under totalitarianism, literature might be produced mechanically, by committees or machines, for the masses. (The novel-writing machines of *1984*'s Fiction Department.)
6. **Conclusion.** Freedom of the intellect means freedom to report what one has seen, heard and felt, and not to fabricate. To write in plain, vigorous language one has to think fearlessly. Wherever orthodoxy is compulsory, literature dies.

### Themes
- Intellectual honesty as the root of literature.
- The treason of the intellectuals (compare Julien Benda's *La Trahison des clercs*, 1927).
- History as a battlefield; the danger to objective truth.

### Exam angles
- "Freedom of expression is the soil of literature": discuss with Orwell's two essays and *1984*.
- Censorship today: PEMRA bans, social-media blocks, self-censorship; also non-state pressure (mob, party, "cancel culture").
- Prose vs poetry under tyranny: test Orwell's claim against Faiz Ahmad Faiz, who wrote some of his finest poetry in prison (*Zindan Nama*, 1956).

## 1.4 Ralph Waldo Emerson, "The Transcendentalist" (1842)

Asked: 2024 (with transcendentalism generally), 2026 (critique of bourgeois materialism).

### Context
- A lecture read at the Masonic Temple, Boston, in January 1842; later collected with *Nature* and other addresses (Gutenberg: *Miscellanies*).
- Emerson (1803–1882) had resigned as a Unitarian minister (1832), published *Nature* (1836), shocked Harvard with the Divinity School Address (1838), and led the Transcendental Club.
- In 1842 the word "Transcendentalist" was used mockingly for young idealists who withdrew from business and church. Emerson explains and defends them.

### Argument
1. **Two sects of thinkers in every age**: **materialists** (who trust facts, history, the senses, the force of circumstance and animal wants) and **idealists** (who trust consciousness, thought and will, inspiration, miracle, individual culture). The materialist takes his departure from external experience; the idealist from consciousness.
2. **Transcendentalism is "Idealism as it appears in 1842".** It is not new; it is the oldest of thoughts in modern dress.
3. **The name**: borrowed from Kant, who answered Locke's claim that nothing is in the intellect that was not first in the senses. Kant showed a class of ideas that do not come from experience but through which experience is acquired; he called them *transcendental* forms. The popular use now means anything intuitive.
4. **The idealist's faith**: the mind sees for itself; facts are signs of spirit; the individual is the world; miracle, inspiration and ecstasy are real.
5. **Portrait of the young Transcendentalists**: they withdraw from society, refuse the common labours and trades, are lonely, critical, sometimes idle. Society calls them selfish and useless. They answer that they will not do work they cannot believe in; they would rather do nothing than serve a false social order.
6. **The capitalist passage** (2026): Emerson challenges the busy, sturdy capitalist. However prudent he is, his whole business rests on foundations that quake; his security is an illusion, because it rests on a system of selfishness and convention, not on the soul.
7. **Balanced judgement**: Emerson admits their faults (they can be fastidious, inactive, unloving) but argues society should value them as protests, as "collectors of the heavenly spark", who keep alive the demand that life be noble. They are signs of a better future.

### Key line (public domain)
> "What is popularly called Transcendentalism among us, is Idealism; Idealism as it appears in 1842."

### Themes
- Idealism vs materialism.
- Critique of commerce and conformity.
- Defence of withdrawal and protest.
- Intuition as a source of knowledge.

### Critical views and angles
- The 2026 question frames it as anti-bourgeois. Agree with qualification: Emerson criticises commercial society's spiritual emptiness, but he is not a socialist. He wants individual moral regeneration, not collective change. Contrast Marx (1840s): Marx's "materialism" is about economic structures; Emerson's "materialist" is a person who trusts only the senses. Do not confuse the two meanings.
- **Lawrence Buell** (*Emerson*, 2003) presents Emerson as a public intellectual who mixed reform sympathies with suspicion of organised movements. [verify]
- Thoreau put the idea into practice at Walden Pond (1845–47).
- **Pakistani link**: the Sufi critique of worldliness (*dunya*) and Iqbal's attack on Western materialism in *Bal-e-Jibril* and *Zarb-e-Kalim*.

## 1.5 Ralph Waldo Emerson, "Self-Reliance" (1841)

Asked: 2017 (two terrors; transcendentalism in the essay), 2021 (nonconformity), 2022 (with Russell), 2024 and 2025 (transcendentalism; Iqbal and Rumi). MCQ in 2026.

### Context
- In *Essays: First Series* (1841). Built from Emerson's journals and lectures of 1830–40.
- American democracy, Jacksonian individualism, and the reaction against European tradition and church authority. Emerson's "American Scholar" address (1837) had called for intellectual independence from Europe.

### Argument (paraphrase, in the essay's order)
1. **Trust thyself.** To believe that your own thought is true for all people is genius. We dismiss our own thoughts because they are ours, and later hear them from others.
2. **Envy is ignorance; imitation is suicide.** Each person must accept his own portion. The power in each is new in nature.
3. **Society is a conspiracy against the manhood of its members.** It values conformity, not self-reliance. "Whoso would be a man, must be a nonconformist." Nothing is sacred but the integrity of your own mind.
4. **The two terrors that scare us from self-trust** (2017 question): **conformity** (fear of others' opinion, the "sour faces" of the multitude) and **consistency** (reverence for our past words and acts). "A foolish consistency is the hobgoblin of little minds." Speak what you think now, even if it contradicts what you said yesterday.
5. **To be great is to be misunderstood**: Pythagoras, Socrates, Jesus, Luther, Copernicus, Galileo, Newton.
6. **The source of self-trust**: the "aboriginal Self", spontaneity or instinct, which Emerson calls Intuition. All later teachings are tuitions. The soul receives truth directly from God; it needs no mediator, no past authority, no sacred text.
7. **Applications** (the essay's second half):
   - **Religion**: prayer that asks for a private benefit is meanness and theft; true prayer is contemplating life from the highest point. Creeds are diseases of the intellect.
   - **Travel**: the soul is no traveller; travelling to escape oneself is "a fool's paradise". (He means escape, not curiosity.)
   - **Culture and art**: imitation of foreign models; Americans should build their own forms.
   - **Society**: society never advances. It gains on one side and loses on the other. The civilised man has built a coach but lost the use of his feet.
   - **Property**: reliance on property and government is lack of self-reliance.
8. **Close**: nothing can bring you peace but yourself; nothing can bring you peace but the triumph of principles.

### Key lines (public domain; short)
> "Trust thyself: every heart vibrates to that iron string."

> "Whoso would be a man, must be a nonconformist."

> "A foolish consistency is the hobgoblin of little minds."

### Themes
Individualism; intuition vs authority; nonconformity; the divine within (immanence); spontaneity; anti-institutionalism; American cultural independence.

### Style
- Aphoristic, oracular, sermon-like (Emerson was a preacher). Paragraphs of loosely linked sentences, each quotable; in his journal Emerson described each of his sentences as an "infinitely repellent particle" [verify source].
- Paradox, imperative mood, metaphor, examples from history.

### Critical views (paraphrased)
- **Harold Bloom** calls Emerson the central figure of American culture and the founder of its "religion of the self".
- **Stanley Cavell** reads Emerson as a serious philosopher of "moral perfectionism": becoming one's "unattained but attainable self".
- **Nietzsche** admired Emerson and carried copies of his essays; the idea of self-overcoming has Emersonian roots.
- **Critics of individualism**: Melville parodied Emersonian optimism in *The Confidence-Man* (1857); Hawthorne mocked Transcendentalist dreamers (*The Blithedale Romance*). Modern critics argue that self-reliance can be turned into "rugged individualism" that ignores the poor and excuses selfish capitalism, the opposite of what Emerson intended.
- **Feminist and social critique**: self-reliance assumes the leisure and independence of a propertied man; women, slaves and labourers were not free to "trust themselves" in his sense. (Emerson later spoke against slavery, from 1844.)

### Emerson, Iqbal and Rumi (2025 question)

| Point | Emerson | Iqbal | Rumi |
|---|---|---|---|
| Core idea | Self-reliance; the divine Over-Soul in each person | *Khudi* (selfhood) developed through love (*ishq*) and striving; *Asrar-i-Khudi* (1915) | The soul's longing for union with God; the reed cut from the reed-bed (opening of the *Masnavi*) |
| Source of knowledge | Intuition | Intuition plus action; *ishq* above *aql* | Love and inner experience above book-learning |
| Society | Suspicious of society and institutions | Self fulfilled in the community (*millat*); *Rumuz-i-Bekhudi* (1918) | The Sufi order and the master–disciple bond |
| God | Immanent, impersonal Over-Soul (near pantheism) | Personal God; the self grows towards Him without dissolving | Union and annihilation (*fana*) in the Beloved |

- **Real historical links**: Emerson read Persian poetry in German translation; he wrote the essay "Persian Poetry" (1858), translated Hafiz, and wrote the poem "Saadi". Iqbal took Rumi as his spiritual guide (*Pir-e-Rumi*) and admired Nietzsche and Goethe; there is no firm evidence that Iqbal read Emerson closely [verify].
- **Argument for an answer**: real resonance on intuition, inner divinity, dynamism and anti-formalism; real difference on society (Iqbal's self serves the *millat*) and on God (personal for Iqbal and Rumi, more impersonal for Emerson). The resonance shows a shared Neoplatonic and mystical heritage across East and West.

### Exam angles
- Nonconformity (2021): define, explain the two terrors, give historical examples, then evaluate (healthy dissent vs egoism).
- Is transcendentalism still relevant? (2024): yes for authenticity, environmental feeling (Thoreau), civil disobedience (Gandhi, King drew on Thoreau), critique of consumerism; limits: social media's "be yourself" slogans as commercial conformity; need for community.
- Compare with Russell (2022): see 1.1.
