# 4. Drama (syllabus component IV, 20 marks)

About one question per paper. *Hamlet* (four times) and *Long Day's Journey into Night* (three times) lead. *Twelfth Night*, *The Way of the World* and *Heartbreak House* have never been asked: learn them at plot-and-themes depth, because the examiner has begun to rotate in new texts (*Pygmalion* first appeared in 2026).

Copyright note: Shakespeare, Congreve and Shaw's two plays (1913, 1919) are public domain; short quotations are given. Pinter, Beckett and O'Neill are in copyright: paraphrase only.

## 4.1 William Shakespeare, *Hamlet* (c.1600–01)

Asked: 2016 (tragic flaw and determinism), 2017 (Bradley's melancholy; why the delay?), 2023 (Hazlitt: "It is we who are Hamlet"), 2024 (Gertrude and Ophelia). MCQs 2026 (twice).

### Context
- Sources: the Danish legend of Amleth (Saxo Grammaticus, c.1200; French version by Belleforest, 1570); a lost earlier *Hamlet* play (the "Ur-Hamlet", perhaps by Thomas Kyd).
- Genre: **revenge tragedy** (Seneca; Kyd's *The Spanish Tragedy*): a ghost, a duty of revenge, delay, feigned madness, a play-within-the-play, a bloody ending. Shakespeare turns it inward.
- Three early texts: Q1 (1603, "bad quarto"), Q2 (1604–05), First Folio (1623). Longest Shakespeare play.
- Intellectual background: Renaissance humanism and scepticism (Montaigne); Reformation debates on purgatory (the Ghost says he is from purgatory, a Catholic idea, while Hamlet studies at Wittenberg, Luther's university).

### Plot (own words)
- **Act I**: at Elsinore, guards and Horatio see the Ghost of old King Hamlet. Claudius, his brother, has become king and married the widow Gertrude within two months. Prince Hamlet, in black, is disgusted ("frailty, thy name is woman"). Laertes leaves for France; Polonius and Laertes warn Ophelia against Hamlet's love. The Ghost tells Hamlet that Claudius poisoned him in the ear while he slept, and demands revenge, but not against Gertrude: "Leave her to heaven." Hamlet decides to "put an antic disposition on" (feign madness).
- **Act II**: Hamlet frightens Ophelia; Polonius thinks love has maddened him. Claudius sets Rosencrantz and Guildenstern, Hamlet's school friends, to spy. Players arrive; the First Player weeps for Hecuba; Hamlet reproaches himself ("O, what a rogue and peasant slave am I!") and plans to test the Ghost with a play: "The play's the thing / Wherein I'll catch the conscience of the king."
- **Act III**: "To be, or not to be"; Hamlet rejects Ophelia ("Get thee to a nunnery") while Claudius and Polonius spy. The play *The Mousetrap* (The Murder of Gonzago) re-enacts the murder; Claudius rises and leaves. Hamlet finds Claudius praying and spares him lest he go to heaven. In Gertrude's closet he stabs Polonius behind the arras, mistaking him for Claudius, and attacks his mother's marriage; the Ghost appears to "whet" his "almost blunted purpose".
- **Act IV**: Hamlet is sent to England with a secret order for his death; on the way (Act IV sc. 4) he sees Fortinbras's army fighting for a worthless patch of Poland ("How all occasions do inform against me"). Ophelia goes mad and drowns (Gertrude's report). Laertes returns for revenge; Claudius plots with him a fencing match with a poisoned, unbated sword and a poisoned cup.
- **Act V**: gravediggers; Yorick's skull; Ophelia's funeral; Hamlet and Laertes fight in the grave. Hamlet tells Horatio how he sent Rosencrantz and Guildenstern to their deaths instead, and speaks of providence: "There's a divinity that shapes our ends", "the readiness is all". The duel: Gertrude drinks the poisoned cup; Laertes wounds Hamlet; in a scuffle they exchange rapiers and Hamlet wounds Laertes; Laertes confesses; Hamlet kills Claudius. Dying, Hamlet asks Horatio to tell his story and gives his voice to Fortinbras, who enters and takes the throne. "The rest is silence."

### Characters
- **Hamlet**: scholar, prince, soldier; witty, melancholy, reflective, cruel at times, morally serious; plays the madman; doubts the Ghost, himself, women, life; changes in Act V to calm acceptance.
- **Claudius**: able, politic, guilty; his prayer scene ("My offence is rank") shows a conscience he cannot obey.
- **Gertrude**: loving mother, sensual, weak; does she know of the murder? The play never says she did. In the closet scene she sees "black and grained spots" in her soul; she dies drinking the cup she was warned against (perhaps knowingly, to save Hamlet? the text is open).
- **Ophelia**: obedient daughter, used as bait; her madness songs expose sexual and political pressures; her drowning is reported, ambiguous (accident or suicide).
- **Polonius**: meddling, pompous counsellor; comic and dangerous.
- **Laertes and Fortinbras**: foils, sons who avenge fathers without hesitation. **Horatio**: the loyal, balanced friend.

### Themes
1. **Revenge and delay**: why does Hamlet wait? (see critical table).
2. **Appearance vs reality** ("seems, madam? Nay, it is"; acting, spying, the play-within-a-play, feigned madness).
3. **Death and mortality**: "To be, or not to be"; Yorick; the "undiscovered country".
4. **Corruption**: "Something is rotten in the state of Denmark"; disease and poison imagery (the poison in the ear, the "unweeded garden").
5. **Action and thought**: "conscience does make cowards of us all".
6. **Women and sexuality**: Hamlet's disgust at Gertrude's remarriage spreads to all women.
7. **Providence and fate**: the movement from Hamlet's sense that he must set the world right ("The time is out of joint") to "the readiness is all".

### Why does Hamlet delay? The main critical answers

| Critic | Answer (paraphrased) |
|---|---|
| **Goethe** (*Wilhelm Meister*, 1795–96) | A great deed is laid on a soul too sensitive for it, like an oak planted in a costly vase that shatters as the roots grow |
| **Coleridge** (lectures, 1811–18) | An excess of thought over action: Hamlet's mind is overbalanced towards contemplation |
| **Hazlitt** (*Characters of Shakespeare's Plays*, 1817) | Hamlet is the most universal character: "It is we who are Hamlet". He is a man of thought and feeling, not of will; his speeches are our own thoughts |
| **A.C. Bradley** (*Shakespearean Tragedy*, 1904) | Not over-thinking but **melancholy**: a morbid depression caused by the shock of his father's death and above all his mother's hasty remarriage, which poisoned his faith in life. The melancholy paralyses the will |
| **Ernest Jones** (*Hamlet and Oedipus*, 1949; following Freud, *Interpretation of Dreams*, 1900) | **Oedipal**: Claudius did what Hamlet unconsciously wished (killed the father, married the mother). To kill Claudius would be to punish himself |
| **T.S. Eliot** ("Hamlet and His Problems", 1919) | The play is "most certainly an artistic failure": Hamlet's emotion is in excess of the facts; Shakespeare found no **objective correlative** for it |
| **Practical/external view** (e.g. Karl Werder, 1875) | The obstacles are real: Claudius is guarded; the Ghost may be a devil; Hamlet needs public proof |
| **Christian/moral view** | Revenge is forbidden to a Christian; the delay reflects conscience; in Act V Hamlet leaves the timing to providence |
| **Jan Kott** (*Shakespeare Our Contemporary*, 1964) | A political play of surveillance in a police state; Hamlet is a young man trapped by power |

**How to use in an answer**: give Bradley fairly, test him against the text (Hamlet acts rashly at times: he kills Polonius, sends his friends to death, boards the pirate ship), then add one more view and end with your own judgement: the delay has many causes; Shakespeare deliberately leaves it open, which is why the play endures.

### The "tragic flaw" reading (2016)
- Aristotle's *hamartia* means an error of judgement, not a moral flaw. Bradley popularised the "fatal flaw" idea.
- **Against a deterministic reading**: chance and other people's plots shape the ending (the pirates, Polonius behind the arras, the swapped rapiers, Gertrude drinking). Hamlet's character is mixed, not a single flaw.
- **Could it have ended otherwise?** If Hamlet had killed Claudius at prayer in Act III, Polonius, Ophelia, Gertrude and Laertes might have lived; but Hamlet would have become a simple avenger like Laertes, and the play is about the cost of becoming one. The ending is less fated than it looks.

### Women in *Hamlet* (2024)
- **Elaine Showalter** ("Representing Ophelia", 1985): Ophelia has been read only in relation to Hamlet; her madness has been represented by painters and psychiatrists as a model of female insanity; feminist criticism must recover her story.
- **Jacqueline Rose** (1985): Eliot's charge that the play lacks an objective correlative is really about Gertrude: the critics' uneasiness with female sexuality is projected onto the play.
- **Agency**: Gertrude acts only in small ways (asking Hamlet to stay, drinking the cup, perhaps protecting him from Claudius); Ophelia obeys her father and brother, is used as bait, and finds a voice only in madness (her songs about lost virginity and a dead father). Both are defined by men's views (Hamlet's "frailty, thy name is woman").
- **Counter-view**: Gertrude's final act may be a choice; Ophelia's songs are a protest. The play may expose patriarchal control rather than endorse it.
- Pakistani link: honour, obedience and women's voice; the madwoman as protest in South Asian fiction.

### Key lines (public domain; use sparingly)
- "Frailty, thy name is woman!" (I.ii)
- "The time is out of joint. O cursed spite, / That ever I was born to set it right!" (I.v)
- "To be, or not to be, that is the question" (III.i)
- "Thus conscience does make cowards of us all" (III.i)
- "There's a divinity that shapes our ends, / Rough-hew them how we will" (V.ii)

## 4.2 *King Lear* (c.1605–06)

Asked: 2022 (family betrayal and disappointment).

### Context
- Sources: the legend of King Leir (Geoffrey of Monmouth, Holinshed), an older play *King Leir* (1594, happy ending), Sidney's *Arcadia* (the Paphlagonian king, source of the Gloucester subplot). Two texts: Quarto (1608) and Folio (1623).
- Background: James I's wish to unite the kingdoms (division of Britain as disaster); anxieties about old age, inheritance and the new individualism.

### Plot (own words)
- **Act I**: old Lear divides Britain among his daughters according to how much each says she loves him. Goneril and Regan flatter; Cordelia, the youngest, says she loves him according to her bond, "no more nor less", and "Nothing" more to say. Lear disowns her; the King of France marries her without dowry; Kent, who protests, is banished but returns in disguise. **Subplot**: Edmund, Gloucester's illegitimate son, forges a letter to turn Gloucester against his legitimate son Edgar ("Now, gods, stand up for bastards!"). Lear, staying with Goneril, is insulted; his Fool mocks his folly.
- **Act II**: Regan and Cornwall also reject Lear's knights; both daughters strip him of his followers ("O, reason not the need!"). Lear goes out into the storm. Edgar, hunted, disguises himself as Poor Tom, a mad beggar.
- **Act III**: on the heath Lear rages against the storm and his daughters, pities "poor naked wretches", meets Poor Tom ("unaccommodated man is no more but such a poor, bare, forked animal") and goes mad. Gloucester helps Lear and is betrayed by Edmund; Cornwall puts out Gloucester's eyes ("Out, vile jelly!"); a servant kills Cornwall.
- **Act IV**: blind Gloucester, led by the disguised Edgar, tries to throw himself from Dover cliff; Edgar saves him by a trick. "As flies to wanton boys are we to the gods; / They kill us for their sport." Goneril and Regan both desire Edmund. Cordelia returns with a French army; Lear wakes, recognises her, asks forgiveness.
- **Act V**: Edmund's forces win; Lear and Cordelia are captured ("We two alone will sing like birds i' the cage"). Edgar defeats Edmund in single combat; Goneril poisons Regan and kills herself; dying Edmund tries to cancel his order to hang Cordelia, too late. Lear enters with Cordelia dead in his arms ("Howl, howl, howl!") and dies, perhaps believing she breathes. Kent and Edgar remain.

### Themes
1. **Family betrayal and filial ingratitude** (2022): two parallel families; Goneril and Regan betray Lear, Edmund betrays Gloucester and Edgar; Cordelia and Edgar remain loyal. "How sharper than a serpent's tooth it is / To have a thankless child!"
2. **Disappointment and blindness**: Lear's moral blindness and Gloucester's physical blinding; both "see" only after suffering ("I stumbled when I saw").
3. **Nature**: two meanings: natural bonds and order (Lear, Cordelia) vs nature as raw self-interest (Edmund's "Thou, Nature, art my goddess").
4. **Justice and the gods**: is the universe just or indifferent? The play gives no clear answer.
5. **Madness and wisdom**: the Fool, Poor Tom and mad Lear speak truth.
6. **Power and its loss**: what is a man without title and property?
7. **"Nothing"**: the word recurs from Cordelia's "Nothing, my lord" to Lear's "Never, never, never, never, never".

### Critical views
- **Samuel Johnson** (1765): Cordelia's death was so shocking that he could not bear to reread the last scenes until he edited the play; he approved of Nahum Tate's adaptation (1681), which gave a happy ending and held the stage until 1838.
- **Charles Lamb** (1811): the play is too vast to be acted; Lear is "essentially impossible to be represented on a stage".
- **A.C. Bradley** (1904): the play shows Lear's redemption through suffering; it might be called "The Redemption of King Lear".
- **Jan Kott** ("King Lear, or Endgame", 1964): a grotesque, absurd play like Beckett's; the gods are absent.
- **Jonathan Dollimore** (*Radical Tragedy*, 1984), cultural materialist: the play is not about redemption but about power and property; humane values survive only with those who have lost power.
- **Coppélia Kahn** ("The Absent Mother in King Lear", 1986), feminist-psychoanalytic: the missing mother; Lear's rage at his daughters is fear of his own dependence ("hysterica passio").

### Exam angles
- Family betrayal: compare with *Hamlet* (son, mother, uncle), *Long Day's Journey* (family blame), *Sons and Lovers* (mother and son).
- Is the play nihilistic or redemptive? Bradley vs Kott.

## 4.3 *Twelfth Night, or What You Will* (c.1601)

Never asked in Part II.

### Plot (own words)
- In Illyria, **Duke Orsino** loves **Countess Olivia**, who has sworn to mourn her brother for seven years. **Viola**, shipwrecked and believing her twin **Sebastian** drowned, disguises herself as a young man, **Cesario**, and serves Orsino. He sends her to woo Olivia, who falls in love with Cesario, while Viola loves Orsino.
- **Subplot**: Olivia's drunken uncle **Sir Toby Belch**, the foolish suitor **Sir Andrew Aguecheek**, the clever maid **Maria** and the clown **Feste** resent **Malvolio**, Olivia's self-important puritan steward. Maria forges a letter in Olivia's hand which convinces Malvolio that Olivia loves him and wishes him to wear yellow stockings, cross-gartered, and to smile constantly ("some are born great, some achieve greatness, and some have greatness thrust upon 'em"). He obeys, is declared mad and locked in a dark room.
- Sebastian arrives with his devoted friend **Antonio**; Olivia mistakes him for Cesario and marries him. Confusion follows (Antonio arrested, Sir Andrew beaten) until the twins meet. Orsino turns to Viola; Toby has married Maria; Malvolio is released and exits vowing "I'll be revenged on the whole pack of you." Feste ends with the song "The rain it raineth every day".

### Themes
- **Love as madness and self-indulgence**: Orsino loves love ("If music be the food of love, play on"); Olivia's mourning is self-dramatising; Viola's patient love ("Patience on a monument") is the true one.
- **Gender and disguise**: Viola-as-Cesario attracts both Orsino and Olivia; boy actors played women in Shakespeare's theatre, so the audience saw a boy playing a girl playing a boy.
- **Festivity vs puritanism**: the title refers to the last night of Christmas revels; Sir Toby: "Dost thou think, because thou art virtuous, there shall be no more cakes and ale?"
- **Class and ambition**: Malvolio's dream of marrying above his station.
- **Melancholy**: Feste's songs, the cruelty to Malvolio and Antonio's isolation darken the comedy.

### Critical views
- **C.L. Barber** (*Shakespeare's Festive Comedy*, 1959): the festive pattern, "through release to clarification": holiday licence leads to self-knowledge.
- **Jan Kott**: a bitter, ambiguous comedy about the fluidity of sex.
- **Stephen Greenblatt** ("Fiction and Friction", in *Shakespearean Negotiations*, 1988): links the play's gender confusion to Renaissance medical ideas of sex difference.
- Modern productions often play Malvolio's humiliation as near-tragedy.

## 4.4 William Congreve, *The Way of the World* (1700)

Never asked in Part II.

### Context
- Congreve (1670–1729). Comedy of manners at the end of the Restoration period. Jeremy Collier's attack (1698) had named Congreve among immoral dramatists. The play was coolly received at first, and Congreve wrote no more comedies. It is now seen as the finest of its kind.

### Plot (own words)
- **Mirabell**, a reformed rake, loves the witty heiress **Millamant**. Half her fortune is controlled by her aunt, **Lady Wishfort**, a vain, ageing widow who hates Mirabell because he once pretended to court her.
- **Mirabell's scheme**: his servant **Waitwell**, married to Lady Wishfort's maid **Foible**, disguises himself as "Sir Rowland", Mirabell's supposed uncle, to woo Lady Wishfort; Mirabell plans to expose the trick and win her consent in return.
- **Villains**: **Fainall**, married for money to Lady Wishfort's daughter **Mrs Fainall** (Mirabell's former mistress), and his mistress **Mrs Marwood**, who secretly loves Mirabell, discover the plot. Fainall then blackmails Lady Wishfort: threatening to expose his wife's past affair, he demands control of his wife's fortune and of Millamant's money.
- **Resolution**: Mirabell produces a deed by which Mrs Fainall, before her marriage, had put her property in trust to him. Fainall's plan collapses. Lady Wishfort, grateful, consents to Mirabell's marriage to Millamant. Comic fops **Witwoud** and **Petulant** and the country squire **Sir Wilfull Witwoud** add satire.
- **The proviso scene (Act IV)**: Millamant and Mirabell bargain over the terms of their marriage. She wants freedom to lie in bed as long as she likes, to receive visits, to be called by no pet names, to keep her privacy, and to "dwindle into a wife" by degrees; he forbids masks, tight lacing during pregnancy and strong drinks among women. They agree to be well-bred and "as strange as if we had been married a great while". It is a witty contract of mutual respect.

### Themes
- **Love vs money**: marriage as property deal (Fainall) versus marriage as a freely negotiated partnership (Mirabell–Millamant).
- **Wit and manners**: true wit (Mirabell, Millamant) vs false wit (Witwoud, Petulant).
- **Deceit**: everyone plots; the "way of the world" is intrigue. The title phrase recurs.
- **Women's position**: women's fortunes controlled by men and aunts; Millamant's provisos assert autonomy within marriage.

### Critical views
- **Charles Lamb** ("On the Artificial Comedy of the Last Century", 1822): Restoration comedy is a fairyland of manners, outside real morality, so it should not be judged morally.
- **L.C. Knights** ("Restoration Comedy: The Reality and the Myth", 1937): the plays are trivial, repetitive and thin in feeling.
- **Bonamy Dobrée** (*Restoration Comedy*, 1924) and later critics praise Congreve's style and the seriousness of the proviso scene.
- Feminist critics see in Millamant a sharp awareness of what marriage costs a woman.

## 4.5 George Bernard Shaw, *Pygmalion* (1913)

Asked: 2026 (knowledge in the hands of a newly emancipated member of the working class). MCQ 2026.

### Context
- Shaw (1856–1950): Irish-born critic and dramatist in London; Fabian socialist; Nobel Prize 1925. Plays of ideas with long prefaces.
- First performed in Vienna (1913) and London (1914, with Mrs Patrick Campbell as Eliza; the word "bloody" caused a sensation). Title from Ovid's *Metamorphoses* (Book 10): the sculptor Pygmalion falls in love with the statue he made, which Venus brings to life (later named Galatea).
- **Preface**: a play about phonetics; it is impossible for an Englishman to open his mouth without making some other Englishman despise him. Higgins is partly based on the phonetician Henry Sweet.

### Plot (own words)
- **Act I**: under the portico of St Paul's, Covent Garden, in the rain. A note-taker (Professor **Henry Higgins**, phonetician) identifies everyone's origins from their speech. He meets **Colonel Pickering**, an expert on Indian dialects. He claims he could pass the flower girl **Eliza Doolittle** off as a duchess in three months by teaching her to speak properly.
- **Act II**: Eliza comes to Higgins's Wimpole Street laboratory to pay for lessons so she can work in a flower shop. Higgins and Pickering make a bet; the housekeeper **Mrs Pearce** objects to how he treats her. Eliza's father **Alfred Doolittle**, a dustman, arrives to sell his daughter for £5; he defends himself as one of the "undeserving poor" who will not be ruled by "middle class morality".
- **Act III**: Eliza's trial at Mrs Higgins's at-home: perfect vowels, but she talks about her aunt being "done in" and ends with "Not bloody likely." Young **Freddy Eynsford Hill** is enchanted. Months later she passes as a duchess triumphantly at an ambassador's reception. (In the 1913/1916 text this happens offstage between acts; Shaw added an embassy scene to the 1941 printed version, following the 1938 film.)
- **Act IV**: after the triumph, Higgins and Pickering congratulate themselves and ignore her. Eliza throws Higgins's slippers at him: "What's to become of me?" She has the manners of a lady but no money and no place.
- **Act V**: at Mrs Higgins's. Doolittle has become rich and miserable: Higgins had jokingly recommended him to an American millionaire, Ezra D. Wannafeller, who left him an income of £3,000 a year to lecture for his Moral Reform World League; now he must marry and live by middle-class morality. Eliza says she learned to be a lady from Pickering, who treated her as one, not from Higgins. She threatens to marry Freddy and teach phonetics as an assistant to Higgins's rival (Nepommuck). Higgins admires her new independence ("By George, Eliza, I said I'd make a woman of you; and I have"). The ending is open: she leaves; Higgins is sure she will return.
- **Shaw's prose sequel** (1916): Eliza marries Freddy and they run a flower shop, helped by Pickering; she never marries Higgins. Shaw fought every romantic ending (the 1938 film and the musical *My Fair Lady*, 1956, unite Eliza and Higgins).

### Characters
- **Eliza**: proud, sharp, emotionally intelligent; grows from object to independent woman.
- **Higgins**: brilliant, childish, rude, a bully indifferent to feelings; treats Eliza as an experiment.
- **Pickering**: courteous gentleman whose manners make Eliza a lady.
- **Doolittle**: comic philosopher of the poor; his fortune satirises middle-class morality.

### Themes
1. **Class and language**: accent as the marker of class; class as a performance that can be learned.
2. **Education and social mobility**: what is the use of becoming a lady without money or work?
3. **Creator and creation**: the myth reversed: the "statue" rebels.
4. **Gender**: Eliza's independence against Higgins's male arrogance; marriage as a woman's only escape.
5. **Middle-class morality**: Doolittle's speeches attack respectability as a cage.

### The 2026 question: knowledge in the hands of the newly emancipated working class
- The knowledge is phonetics and polished speech, the code of the ruling class. In Higgins's hands it is a hobby and a game. In Eliza's hands it becomes **power and a livelihood**: she can earn her living, choose her husband, and even compete with Higgins by teaching it.
- It also brings **alienation**: she can no longer go back to selling flowers; she belongs nowhere (Act IV).
- Doolittle shows the reverse: sudden money "emancipates" him into bondage to middle-class duties.
- Shaw's Fabian point: knowledge and money, not birth, make a "lady"; class distinctions are artificial and could be abolished through education. Link to Marxist criticism (ideology and class) and to Bourdieu's idea of "cultural capital" (education and manners as forms of capital).
- **Pakistan link**: English-medium education as a class marker; "English is a gatekeeper" in CSS itself; the speaker of "proper" English treated as more able.

### Critical views
- **Eric Bentley** (*Bernard Shaw*, 1947): Shaw's comedies are serious debates; Eliza's revolt is the play's true climax.
- **Shaw's own afterword** insists the play is not a love story.
- Feminist critics debate whether Eliza is liberated or remains defined by men's choices.

### Key lines
- "Not bloody likely." (Act III)
- "The great secret, Eliza, is not having bad manners or good manners or any other particular sort of manners, but having the same manner for all human souls." (Higgins, Act V)

## 4.6 George Bernard Shaw, *Heartbreak House* (published 1919)

Never asked in Part II.

### Context
- Written 1916–17 during the First World War; published 1919; first staged in New York (1920). Subtitle: "A Fantasia in the Russian Manner on English Themes": Shaw's homage to Chekhov (*The Cherry Orchard*).
- **Preface**: "Heartbreak House" is cultured, leisured Europe before the war, drifting while the rulers ("Horseback Hall", the hunting gentry) led it into catastrophe. Neither the cultured nor the powerful governed wisely.

### Plot (own words)
- The setting is the Sussex house of **Captain Shotover**, aged 88, a retired sea captain who has built his house like a ship, drinks rum, invents weapons and seeks the "seventh degree of concentration".
- **Ellie Dunn**, a young woman, arrives at the invitation of Shotover's daughter **Hesione Hushabye**. Ellie is engaged to **Boss Mangan**, a rich industrialist, out of gratitude to her father **Mazzini Dunn**, a gentle idealist whose business Mangan ruined. Ellie confesses she loves a romantic stranger, "Marcus Darnley"; he turns out to be Hesione's husband **Hector Hushabye**, a handsome teller of heroic lies. Ellie's heart is broken; she hardens and decides to marry Mangan for money.
- Shotover's other daughter, **Lady Ariadne Utterword**, returns after 23 years as the wife of a colonial governor; her father pretends not to recognise her. Her brother-in-law **Randall Utterword** is helplessly in love with her.
- **Act II**: Ellie hypnotises Mangan; he overhears the others mocking him and breaks down; he reveals he has no money of his own, only other people's, and that he is to join the government. A **Burglar** is caught; he is Billy Dunn, Shotover's former boatswain and Nurse Guinness's husband, who makes a living by being caught and paid off. Ellie decides not to marry Mangan and declares a "spiritual marriage" with old Shotover.
- **Act III**: in the garden at night the characters talk of the emptiness of their lives. Shotover warns that England is a ship drifting onto the rocks; the only cure is to learn navigation. Bombs fall in an air raid. Mangan and the burglar hide in the gravel pit where Shotover stores his dynamite; they are killed. The rest are exhilarated and Ellie hopes the bombers will come again tomorrow.

### Themes
- **The drifting ship of England**: the leisured, cultured class talked while the war came. Shotover: navigation, "Learn it and live; or leave it and be damned".
- **Heartbreak**: romantic illusion shattered (Ellie); heartbreak as the beginning of wisdom.
- **Capitalism exposed**: Mangan the "captain of industry" is a fraud who manages other people's money.
- **Death wish of a civilisation**: the excitement at the bombs.
- **Empire**: Lady Utterword's colonial governor husband, Hastings, can rule natives with the whip; she thinks England needs the same.

### Critical views
- Shaw himself considered it among his best plays; critics such as **Eric Bentley** praised its Chekhovian depth [verify]. Others find it formless and talky.
- Compare **Stevens's "Postcard from the Volcano"** (a decayed mansion of culture) and Eliot's *Waste Land* (post-war sterility).

## 4.7 Harold Pinter, *The Caretaker* (1960)

Asked: 2019 (tragicomic breakdown of communication), 2020 (how Mick uses language against Davies). In copyright: paraphrase only.

### Context
- Pinter (1930–2008): son of a Jewish tailor in Hackney; actor; playwright (*The Birthday Party*, 1958; *The Homecoming*, 1965); later political activist. **Nobel Prize 2005**.
- *The Caretaker* opened at the Arts Theatre, London (April 1960), and made his name. Labelled **Theatre of the Absurd** (Martin Esslin) and **comedy of menace** (Irving Wardle's term for his early plays).
- "Pinteresque": everyday speech full of pauses, silences, repetitions and evasions, beneath which lie power struggles; menace without explanation.

### Plot (own words)
- **Act I**: a cluttered room in a west London house, full of junk (a gas stove, a statue of the Buddha, a bucket catching drips from the roof). **Aston**, a slow, quiet man in his early thirties, brings in **Davies**, an old tramp he has rescued from a fight in a café. Davies complains, boasts, insults foreigners and Black neighbours, and says his papers are in **Sidcup** and he will go to get them when the weather breaks. He uses a false name, Bernard Jenkins. Aston offers him a bed and plans to build a shed in the garden. Next morning, when Aston goes out, Davies is attacked by **Mick**, Aston's younger brother, who owns the house.
- **Act II**: Mick plays cat and mouse with Davies: rapid questions, long irrelevant stories about relatives, sudden switches of tone, legal and estate-agent jargon. Each brother separately offers Davies the job of caretaker. At the end of the act Aston tells, in a long monologue, how he was taken to a mental hospital and given electric shock treatment against his will, which damaged his mind; he says he wants to find the man who reported him.
- **Act III**: Davies, emboldened, sides with Mick, whom he thinks is the stronger, and turns on Aston, calling him mad and threatening him with a knife. Mick, after describing his dreams of redecorating the house as a luxury flat, turns on Davies, exposes his lies, and smashes the Buddha. Aston returns; the brothers exchange a faint smile. Aston tells Davies to leave. Davies pleads and promises; Aston, his back turned, looks out of the window. Silence.

### Characters
- **Davies**: selfish, prejudiced, cowardly, ungrateful, comic, pitiful; a survivor who cannot help biting the hand that feeds him; his identity is unfixed (two names, no papers).
- **Aston**: gentle, damaged, slow; kind but withdrawn; the shed is his fragile hope of rebuilding.
- **Mick**: energetic, unpredictable, witty, menacing; loves his brother but cannot reach him; uses Davies as a tool, then discards him.

### Themes
1. **Territory and power**: a struggle for possession of a room; each character seeks a place and status.
2. **Failure of communication** (2019): characters talk past each other; language is used to hide, attack or evade rather than to communicate; long silences.
3. **Language as weapon** (2020): see below.
4. **Isolation and the need for connection**: three lonely men; the brothers' bond is real but unspoken.
5. **Identity**: Davies's lost papers; Aston's damaged self.
6. **Tragicomedy**: Davies's shoes and excuses are funny; his expulsion is painful.

### How Mick uses language against Davies (2020)
- **Interrogation**: a barrage of questions ("What's your name?") that leaves Davies no time to answer.
- **Non-sequitur monologues**: long, absurd stories about people Davies does not know (an uncle's brother, a man in Shoreditch), which disorient him and assert Mick's control of the talk.
- **Jargon**: the language of estate agents, lawyers and interior decorators (rent terms, insurance, furnishing fabrics), which Davies cannot follow and which underlines his lack of status.
- **Shifts of tone**: from menace to friendliness to formality, so Davies never knows where he stands.
- **Mimicry and traps**: Mick uses Davies's own words against him (his claim to be an interior decorator) to expose his lies.
- Effect: language as territorial aggression; Mick wins without real violence (until the Buddha).

### Critical views
- **Martin Esslin** (*The Theatre of the Absurd*, 1961; *Pinter: The Playwright*): Pinter's realism is "absurd" because it shows the unverifiable nature of human motive.
- **Pinter himself**: the play is about "a particular human situation, concerning three particular people", not symbols; he also said at some point that it was funny up to a point, and that beyond that point it ceased to be funny [paraphrase].
- **Michael Billington** (*Harold Pinter*, 1996/2007): the play is about power and the hunger for territory, rooted in post-war London and Pinter's own experience.
- **Kenneth Tynan** and others praised its comic realism.

## 4.8 Samuel Beckett, *Waiting for Godot* (1953; English 1955)

Asked: 2018 (Absurd; insignificance of human achievement). MCQ 2026. In copyright: paraphrase only.

### Context
- Beckett (1906–1989): Irish, lived in Paris from 1937; assistant to James Joyce; French Resistance in the Second World War; wrote in French and translated himself. **Nobel Prize 1969**.
- *En attendant Godot* premiered in Paris (January 1953); his own English version, subtitled "a tragicomedy in two acts", in London (1955, directed by Peter Hall).
- A famous production by San Quentin prisoners (1957): inmates understood the waiting at once.

### Plot (own words)
- **Act I**: a country road, a single bare tree, evening. Two tramps, **Vladimir** (Didi) and **Estragon** (Gogo), wait for someone called **Godot**. They talk, quarrel, joke, struggle with boots and hats, consider hanging themselves, and are not sure they are in the right place or day. **Pozzo**, a pompous landowner, arrives driving **Lucky**, his slave, on a rope. Lucky, ordered to "think", pours out a long, breathless, nonsensical speech mixing theology, science and decay. Pozzo and Lucky leave. A **boy** arrives: Mr Godot will not come this evening, but surely tomorrow. The tramps agree to go but do not move.
- **Act II**: next day, same place; the tree has a few leaves. Estragon remembers little. Pozzo returns blind and Lucky dumb; they fall and cannot get up. Pozzo, asked when he went blind, cries out that time is meaningless: one day we are born, one day we die; birth and the grave are almost the same moment. The boy returns with the same message and does not remember them. The tramps try to hang themselves with Estragon's belt; it breaks and his trousers fall. They agree to go and do not move.

### Characters
- **Vladimir**: more thoughtful, remembers, worries about salvation (the two thieves crucified with Christ).
- **Estragon**: more physical, forgetful, sleepy, complains of his boots.
- **Pozzo**: power and property, rhetoric and dependence; in Act II helpless.
- **Lucky**: the servant, the intellect enslaved; his speech is the ruin of human knowledge.
- **Godot**: never appears. Beckett said that if he had known who Godot was he would have said so in the play.

### Themes
1. **Waiting and hope**: life as waiting for a meaning or saviour that does not come.
2. **The Absurd**: humans seek meaning; the universe is silent (Camus).
3. **Time and memory**: repetition, forgetting, the circularity of the two acts.
4. **Habit**: Vladimir: habit is a great deadener [paraphrase].
5. **Friendship and dependence**: the tramps cannot part; Pozzo and Lucky are bound by the rope (master and slave, Hegel).
6. **Religion**: Christian imagery (the thieves, the tree as cross); Godot as God? Beckett denied a simple equation.
7. **The insignificance of human achievement** (2018): no heroism; no action; even suicide fails; Pozzo's power and Lucky's learning collapse.

### Form
- Two nearly identical acts: a circular structure. The critic **Vivian Mercier** (*Irish Times*, 1956) said it is a play in which nothing happens, twice.
- Music-hall and circus comedy: hats, boots, falling trousers, Laurel and Hardy routines.
- Short exchanges, repetition, pauses, silence.
- **Tragicomedy**: laughter at despair.

### Critical views
- **Martin Esslin** (1961): the defining play of the Theatre of the Absurd; it presents the absurdity of the human condition instead of arguing about it.
- **Jean Anouilh** compared the first night to Pascal's *Pensées* performed by music-hall clowns [paraphrase].
- **Kenneth Tynan** (1955) said it forced him to re-examine the rules of drama.
- **Theodor Adorno** wrote on Beckett's *Endgame* as the drama of a world after Auschwitz; some critics read *Godot* through the war and the Resistance (waiting in hiding).
- Marxist and postcolonial readings (the master–slave rope); in Pakistan and South Asia, readings linking it to political waiting for a saviour.

### The 2018 claim, with evidence
- Heroic adventure is replaced by waiting; the only "events" are minor.
- Achievement is mocked: Pozzo's power ends in blindness; Lucky's "thinking" is gibberish; the tramps cannot even hang themselves.
- But: the play shows endurance, friendship and humour as small human achievements. A balanced answer adds that the tramps' persistence has a dignity (compare Camus: "one must imagine Sisyphus happy").

## 4.9 Eugene O'Neill, *Long Day's Journey into Night* (written 1941–42; published 1956)

Asked: 2016 (Edmund's loneliness), 2021 (modern tragedy), 2025 (blasphemy in the Tyrones). In copyright: paraphrase only.

### Context
- O'Neill (1888–1953): son of the actor James O'Neill (famous for playing the Count of Monte Cristo thousands of times); expelled from Princeton; sailor; TB in 1912; Nobel Prize 1936.
- The play is openly autobiographical: the Tyrones are the O'Neills in August 1912 at their summer house (Monte Cristo Cottage, New London, Connecticut). O'Neill dedicated it to his wife Carlotta, saying he wrote it "in tears and blood" and with pity and forgiveness for all four Tyrones. He wanted it published 25 years after his death; Carlotta released it in 1956. Premiere: Stockholm, February 1956; Broadway November 1956; Pulitzer Prize 1957 (his fourth).

### Plot (own words)
- **One day in August 1912**, from morning to midnight, in four acts. As the day goes on, fog rolls in and the foghorn sounds.
- **Act I (8:30 a.m.)**: after breakfast. **James Tyrone** (65), a famous actor, and his wife **Mary** (54) seem affectionate. The men watch Mary anxiously: she has recently returned from a cure for morphine addiction and was heard moving about at night. Tyrone and his elder son **Jamie** (33) quarrel about money, Jamie's drinking and idleness, and the health of the younger son **Edmund** (23), who has a "summer cold" that is really consumption (TB).
- **Act II (lunchtime)**: Mary is plainly back on morphine, dreamy and evasive. Blame flows in all directions: Tyrone's meanness (he hired a cheap hotel doctor who first gave her morphine after Edmund's difficult birth), Jamie's cynicism, Mary's past (she blames Jamie for infecting her second baby, Eugene, with measles, which killed him). The doctor confirms Edmund has consumption.
- **Act III (evening)**: Mary, alone with the maid Cathleen, drinks and reminisces about her convent girlhood: she wanted to be a nun or a concert pianist until she fell in love with James Tyrone. She refuses to accept Edmund's illness. When Edmund tells her he has TB, she says he is exaggerating; he bitterly says it is hard to have a "dope fiend" for a mother, then regrets it.
- **Act IV (midnight)**: Tyrone and Edmund, drunk, play cards and talk. Tyrone confesses he wasted his talent: once praised as a fine Shakespearean actor, he bought a melodrama (the Monte Cristo role) that made him rich and destroyed his art, because poverty in childhood (Irish immigrant, father deserted) made him afraid of the poorhouse. Edmund tells of his moments of ecstatic belonging at sea, and says he would have been better born a seagull or fish; as it is he will always be a stranger who never feels at home, does not really want and is not really wanted, can never belong, and must always be a little in love with death [paraphrase]. Jamie comes home drunk and confesses he has always wanted Edmund to fail, out of jealousy, while loving him. Mary appears, lost in morphine, carrying her old wedding dress, and remembers the spring when she fell in love with James Tyrone and was so happy for a time.

### Characters
- **James Tyrone**: generous in feeling, miserly in money, proud, sentimental, Catholic, guilty.
- **Mary Tyrone**: charming, nervous, lonely, addicted; lives in the past; feels homeless (never had a real home in hotels and touring).
- **Jamie**: witty, cynical, alcoholic, self-destructive; loves and resents Edmund.
- **Edmund**: the young O'Neill; poet, sailor, reader of Nietzsche and decadent poets; ill; the most sympathetic observer.

### Themes
1. **The past controls the present**: Mary says the past is the present and the future too [paraphrase]. Every quarrel returns to old wounds.
2. **Blame and forgiveness**: they accuse, retract, apologise, accuse again. Love and hatred are inseparable.
3. **Addiction and escape**: morphine (Mary), whiskey (all the men), the fog as a hiding place.
4. **Home and homelessness**: the summer house is not a home; Edmund's sense of never belonging.
5. **Loss of faith**: see below.
6. **Wasted talent and money**: Tyrone's sold-out art; his fear of the poorhouse.
7. **Illness and death**: Edmund's TB; the dead baby Eugene.

### Edmund's loneliness (2016)
- **Causes**: a mother who escapes into drugs and who wishes he had never been born (her addiction began with his birth, so he feels he caused it); a father whose meanness may send him to a cheap sanatorium; a brother who wants him to fail; illness; lost religious faith; the poet's temperament (he can only stammer, he says, compared with real poets); the modern sense of being a stranger in the universe. The sea speech shows that only in moments of dissolving into nature does he feel he belongs.

### Blasphemy in all four Tyrones (2025)
- **Tyrone**: a practising Catholic who calls his sons' atheism blasphemous, yet his worship of money and his lies show a faith that is habit, not life.
- **Mary**: has lost the faith she had in the convent; she cannot pray to the Virgin since she is "living a lie"; at the end she is searching for something she lost (her faith) and remembers wanting to be a nun.
- **Jamie**: mocks religion and quotes cynical verse; calls his mother's drug-state with blasphemous irony; Broadway sneer at everything sacred.
- **Edmund**: quotes Nietzsche (God is dead), Baudelaire, Swinburne and Dowson; Tyrone calls these poets "whoremongers and degenerates". Yet his sea-mysticism is a substitute religion.
- **Point**: the blasphemy is a symptom of the family's spiritual homelessness; the "Irish Catholic family" has kept the guilt of Catholicism but lost its consolation.

### Modern tragedy (2021)
- **Aristotelian features**: unity of time (one day), place (one house) and action; recognition (each learns the truth of the others); pity and fear; catharsis through forgiveness.
- **Modern features**: no hero of high rank; no single flaw; fate works through heredity, addiction, money and memory; no death on stage, only the slow death of hope; the family itself is the tragic hero.
- **Arthur Miller** ("Tragedy and the Common Man", 1949): the common man is as fit a subject for tragedy as kings.
- **Critics**: **Travis Bogard** (*Contour in Time*, 1972) sees it as O'Neill's culminating tragedy of forgiveness; **Harold Bloom** calls it the best American play, despite O'Neill's clumsy prose [verify].
