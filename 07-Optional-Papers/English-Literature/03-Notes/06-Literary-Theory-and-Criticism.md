# 6. Literary Theory and Criticism (syllabus component VI, 20 marks)

**At least one theory question every year; two in 2017, 2020 and 2023.** Six schools. For a candidate with no literature degree this is the hardest part, but also the most predictable: learn each school's founders, key terms, method, one set-text application, and limitations.

**For every theory answer use this frame:**
1. Definition and origin (who, when, why).
2. Core assumptions (four to six numbered points).
3. Key thinkers and their key terms (a table helps).
4. Method: the questions this critic asks of a text.
5. Application to one or two set texts (this is what lifts an answer above guidebook level).
6. Criticisms and limitations.
7. Conclusion: what the theory still offers.

Suggested readings in the FPSC syllabus for this part: Bertens, *Literary Theory: The Basics*; Eagleton, *Literary Theory: An Introduction*; Selden, Widdowson and Brooker, *A Reader's Guide to Contemporary Literary Theory*. Peter Barry's *Beginning Theory* (not on FPSC's list) is the easiest start.

## 6.0 Background: liberal humanism (what theory reacted against)

- The "common-sense" criticism before theory: Matthew Arnold, F.R. Leavis, the New Critics (Cleanth Brooks, W.K. Wimsatt), and in Pakistan the traditional university syllabus.
- **Assumptions** (Peter Barry lists ten "tenets of liberal humanism"; in short): good literature is timeless and universal; the text contains its own meaning; the purpose of literature is to enhance life and spread human values; human nature is essentially unchanging; the individual has a unique, stable "self" or essence; form and content are organically fused; sincerity is a value; the critic interprets the text and mediates between text and reader.
- **New Criticism** (1930s–50s): close reading of "the words on the page"; ambiguity, paradox, irony, tension; the "intentional fallacy" (author's intention irrelevant) and the "affective fallacy" (reader's response irrelevant), both from Wimsatt and Beardsley (1946, 1949).
- **Theory's challenge** (2025 question): from the 1960s, structuralism, Marxism, psychoanalysis, deconstruction, feminism and postcolonialism argued that meaning, the self, "human nature" and literary value are produced by language, ideology, the unconscious, gender and power. Michel Foucault (*The Order of Things*, 1966) said "man" is a recent invention that may be erased "like a face drawn in sand at the edge of the sea".

## 6.1 Structuralism

Asked: 2018 (modernist scientific ambition → structuralism), 2019 (understanding parts through structures), 2025 (structuralism and poststructuralism vs humanism).

### Definition
Structuralism studies literature (and culture) as a **system of signs** governed by underlying rules, the way linguistics studies language. Individual works are understood by relating them to the larger structures (codes, conventions, oppositions) that make meaning possible. It looks for the "grammar" of literature, not the meaning of one text.

### Origins
- **Ferdinand de Saussure**, *Course in General Linguistics* (1916, compiled from his lectures by students):
  - The **sign** = **signifier** (sound-image) + **signified** (concept). The link is **arbitrary**: nothing in the sound "tree" connects it to the idea of a tree.
  - **Langue** (the system of language) vs **parole** (individual utterances). Linguistics studies *langue*.
  - **Synchronic** (system at one time) vs **diachronic** (historical change). Saussure favoured synchronic study.
  - Meaning comes from **difference**: in language there are only "differences without positive terms" ("cat" means what it does because it is not "bat", "cut", "dog").
  - **Syntagmatic** (combination along a sentence) and **paradigmatic** (selection from a set of alternatives) relations.
- **Russian Formalism** (1910s–20s): Viktor Shklovsky's **defamiliarisation** ("Art as Technique", 1917): art makes the familiar strange; "literariness" (Roman Jakobson); fabula (story) vs syuzhet (plot).
- **Vladimir Propp**, *Morphology of the Folktale* (1928): 100 Russian fairy tales reduced to **31 functions** (e.g. "the hero leaves home", "the villain is punished") and seven character roles ("spheres of action": hero, villain, donor, helper, princess, dispatcher, false hero).
- **Prague School**; **Roman Jakobson**: six functions of language, including the **poetic function** (focus on the message itself); metaphor (selection) and metonymy (combination) as two poles.

### The structuralist movement (1950s–60s, France)
| Thinker | Contribution |
|---|---|
| **Claude Lévi-Strauss** | *Structural Anthropology* (1958): myths are built from **binary oppositions** (raw/cooked, nature/culture) and **mythemes**; the Oedipus myth analysed as a structure that mediates contradictions |
| **Roland Barthes** (early) | *Mythologies* (1957): everyday culture (wrestling, wine, a magazine cover) as myth that makes ideology look natural; "Introduction to the Structural Analysis of Narratives" (1966) |
| **A.J. Greimas** | The actantial model: six actants in three pairs (subject/object, sender/receiver, helper/opponent) |
| **Tzvetan Todorov** | Narrative grammar; "narratology" (his term, 1969); the pattern equilibrium → disruption → new equilibrium |
| **Gérard Genette** | *Narrative Discourse* (1972): **order** (analepsis/flashback, prolepsis), **duration**, **frequency**, **mood** (focalisation), **voice** (who narrates) |
| **Jonathan Culler** | *Structuralist Poetics* (1975): the goal is "literary competence", the conventions that let readers make sense of texts |

### Core assumptions
1. Meaning is produced by systems of relations, not by individual intention.
2. The text is like a sentence in the "language" of literature; a poetics should describe that language.
3. Binary oppositions organise thought and texts.
4. The author is less important than the codes and conventions.
5. Criticism can be systematic, even scientific.

### The "scientific" ambition (2018)
- Modernism (Eliot's impersonality, the New Critics' objectivity, Formalism's search for "literariness") wanted criticism to be rigorous and objective rather than impressionistic. Linguistics offered a model of a human science with clear methods. Structuralism took this to its limit: a "science of literature" (Todorov's "poetics"; Barthes's early structural analysis). Northrop Frye's *Anatomy of Criticism* (1957), though not structuralist, also aimed at a systematic, "scientific" criticism of genres and archetypes.

### Method: questions to ask
- What binary oppositions organise the text?
- What narrative functions or actants appear?
- How does the text relate to its genre's conventions?
- How is time handled (order, duration, frequency)? Who sees? Who speaks?

### Application to set texts
- ***The Scarlet Letter***: oppositions town/forest, law/nature, public/private, light/dark, Puritan/Indian; Pearl mediates.
- **"The Gift of the Magi"**: opposition of material value/emotional value; the symmetrical structure (two treasures, two sacrifices, two useless gifts) is the story's meaning.
- ***Hamlet***: Propp/Greimas: subject (Hamlet), object (revenge/justice), sender (Ghost), opponent (Claudius), helper (Horatio); the 2026 paper had an MCQ on a structuralist reading of Hamlet's delay.
- ***Waiting for Godot***: repetition and binary pairs (Didi/Gogo, Pozzo/Lucky, Act I/Act II) produce meaning by difference.

### Criticisms
1. **Ahistorical**: ignores history, the author, the reader's situation.
2. **Reductive**: reduces rich texts to a few functions and oppositions; all texts look alike.
3. **Indifferent to value**: cannot tell a great poem from a bad one.
4. **Claims scientific objectivity it cannot have**: the analyst chooses the oppositions.
5. **Poststructuralism** shows that the system itself is unstable (below).

### Poststructuralism (2025)
- **Derrida**, "Structure, Sign and Play in the Discourse of the Human Sciences" (lecture at Johns Hopkins, 1966): every structure assumes a fixed **centre** that is not itself part of the play of the structure; once we see the centre is a construct, there is only free play of signs.
- **Barthes**, "The Death of the Author" (1967): the birth of the reader must be at the cost of the death of the author; the text is "a tissue of quotations". *S/Z* (1970): "readerly" (lisible) vs "writerly" (scriptible) texts.
- **Foucault**: discourse and power produce knowledge and subjects; "What is an Author?" (1969).
- **Lacan**: the subject is split and constituted in language.
- **Against humanism**: the subject is not a stable essence but an effect of language and discourse; "nature" is a cultural construct; culture is not the expression of a universal human spirit but a field of power; language does not express pre-existing meaning but produces it.
- **Debate**: humanist critics (e.g. M.H. Abrams, Raymond Tallis, *Not Saussure*, 1988) answered that theory's anti-humanism is self-defeating (it is argued by human authors appealing to reason) and ignores ordinary experience.

## 6.2 Marxist criticism

Asked: 2022 (is Marx still relevant?), 2023 (literature expresses society; limitations).

### Definition
Marxist criticism reads literature in relation to the economic and social conditions that produce it, especially **class** and **ideology**. It asks whose interests a text serves, what social reality it reflects or hides, and how its form is shaped by history.

### Marx and Engels
- Karl Marx (1818–1883) and Friedrich Engels (1820–1895): *The Communist Manifesto* (1848); *Capital* (1867).
- **Historical materialism**: history is driven by changes in the mode of production and by class struggle ("The history of all hitherto existing society is the history of class struggles").
- **Base and superstructure** (Preface to *A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy*, 1859): the economic **base** (forces and relations of production) shapes the **superstructure** (law, politics, religion, art, philosophy). "It is not the consciousness of men that determines their being, but their social being that determines their consciousness."
- **Ideology**: the ideas of the ruling class are in every age the ruling ideas; ideology makes the existing order look natural (Engels's phrase "false consciousness").
- **Alienation**: workers estranged from their labour, its products, each other and their human nature (1844 manuscripts).
- Marx admired Balzac and Shakespeare; Engels's letter to Margaret Harkness (1888) praised Balzac's realism for showing social truth despite his royalist politics.
- **Marx's puzzle** (*Grundrisse*): why does Greek art still give pleasure though its social base has vanished?

### Main thinkers
| Thinker | Key ideas |
|---|---|
| **Georg Lukács** (Hungary) | *The Historical Novel* (1937), essays on realism: great **realism** (Balzac, Tolstoy, Scott) shows the "totality" of society through **typical** characters; he condemned modernism (Joyce, Kafka) as decadent subjectivism |
| **Antonio Gramsci** (Italy) | *Prison Notebooks* (1929–35): **hegemony**: rule by consent through culture, education and common sense, not only by force; organic intellectuals |
| **Walter Benjamin** | "The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction" (1936): reproduction destroys the "aura" of art and can democratise it |
| **Frankfurt School**: Adorno and Horkheimer | *Dialectic of Enlightenment* (1944): the **culture industry** produces standardised mass culture that pacifies; Adorno defended difficult modernism (Beckett) as resistance |
| **Louis Althusser** (France) | "Ideology and Ideological State Apparatuses" (1970): **ISAs** (school, church, family, media) and RSAs (police, army); ideology **interpellates** (hails) individuals as subjects; ideology is the imaginary relation of individuals to their real conditions; **relative autonomy** of the superstructure |
| **Pierre Macherey** | *A Theory of Literary Production* (1966): read a text's **silences and gaps**: what it cannot say reveals its ideology |
| **Raymond Williams** (Britain) | *Marxism and Literature* (1977): **cultural materialism**; "**structures of feeling**"; dominant, residual and emergent culture |
| **Terry Eagleton** | *Criticism and Ideology* (1976), *Literary Theory* (1983): literature as an ideological category; "literature" itself defined by social value judgements |
| **Fredric Jameson** (US) | *The Political Unconscious* (1981): "Always historicize!"; every text has a political unconscious; *Postmodernism, or, the Cultural Logic of Late Capitalism* (1991) |

### The sociological view: "literature is an expression of society" (2023)
- Before Marx: **Louis de Bonald** (early 19th century) said literature is an expression of society; **Madame de Staël** (*On Literature Considered in its Relation to Social Institutions*, 1800); **Hippolyte Taine** (*History of English Literature*, 1863–64): literature is the product of *race*, *milieu* and *moment*.
- **Marxist refinement**: literature does not simply mirror society; it is shaped by class position and ideology, and can also expose contradictions (Engels on Balzac; Macherey's gaps; Lukács's realism). So the reflection is mediated, active and sometimes critical.
- **Examples**: Hardy's changing rural economy; the mining community in *Sons and Lovers*; New York's poor in "The Gift of the Magi"; Restoration marriage-as-property in *The Way of the World*.

### Method: questions to ask
- What class does each character belong to? Whose interests does the plot serve?
- What does the text treat as natural that is really social?
- What is absent or silenced (workers, servants, the colonised)?
- How does the form (realism, fragmentation) relate to the society that produced it?

### Applications
- ***Pygmalion***: class as performance; accent as cultural capital; Doolittle's "middle class morality".
- ***1984***: the proles and the Party; Goldstein's book is an analysis of oligarchy in Marxist terms; also Orwell's critique of Soviet "socialism".
- ***Heartbreak House***: Mangan's capitalism exposed; the leisured class drifting.
- ***Sons and Lovers***: the mine, class ambition, the split family as product of industrial capitalism.
- ***The Way of the World***: marriage as transfer of property.
- **"The Gift of the Magi"**: love under poverty; a Marxist might say the story consoles the poor rather than questioning poverty.

### Is Marx still relevant? (2022)
- **Yes**: inequality (Thomas Piketty's *Capital in the Twenty-First Century*, 2013); the culture industry and streaming platforms; ideology in media; postcolonial and feminist criticism borrowed Marxist tools (Spivak, Said, materialist feminism); cultural studies (Stuart Hall). In Pakistan: the Progressive Writers' Movement (1936: Faiz, Sajjad Zaheer, Krishan Chander, Ismat Chughtai) was Marxist-influenced.
- **Limits**: see below.

### Limitations (2023)
1. **Economic determinism / reductionism**: reduces art to economics ("vulgar Marxism").
2. **Neglect of form and aesthetic value**: treats literature as a document.
3. **Prescriptive politics**: Soviet **socialist realism** (Zhdanov, 1934) turned criticism into propaganda; Lukács's attack on modernism.
4. **Cannot explain lasting appeal** (Marx's own Greek-art puzzle).
5. **Class-centred blindness**: neglects gender, race, religion, sexuality (feminist and postcolonial critiques).
6. **Historical failure** of communist states weakened its prestige (though not its analytical value).
7. **Crude reflection theory** ignores the individual imagination.

## 6.3 Deconstruction

Asked: 2023 (explain Derrida; is deconstruction "not destruction"?).

### Definition
Deconstruction is a way of reading, developed by **Jacques Derrida** (1930–2004), that shows how a text's meaning depends on hierarchical oppositions it cannot keep stable. By close reading, the critic shows that the text undermines its own claims: its marginal details, contradictions and figures of speech unsettle the meaning it seems to assert.

### Derrida's key works
*Of Grammatology* (1967); *Writing and Difference* (1967); *Speech and Phenomena* (1967); "Structure, Sign and Play" (1966); *Dissemination* (1972); *Limited Inc* (1988, with the debate with John Searle).

### Key terms
| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Logocentrism** | Western thought's desire for a final, fixed meaning or origin (logos: word, reason, God) |
| **Metaphysics of presence** | The belief that meaning is fully present somewhere (in the speaker's mind, in God, in being) |
| **Phonocentrism** | The privileging of speech over writing as closer to presence; Derrida shows writing's features (absence, repetition, difference) are already in speech |
| **Binary oppositions** | speech/writing, presence/absence, male/female, nature/culture, reason/madness: one term is privileged. Deconstruction first **reverses** the hierarchy, then **displaces** it, showing each term depends on the other |
| **Différance** | Derrida's coinage: meaning arises from **difference** (Saussure) and is endlessly **deferred** along a chain of signifiers; the "a" is visible in writing, not audible in French speech |
| **Trace** | Every sign carries traces of the others it is not |
| **Supplement** | Something added to a supposedly complete whole that reveals the whole was lacking (Rousseau calls writing a supplement to speech) |
| **Aporia** | An impasse; a point of undecidable meaning |
| **"Il n'y a pas de hors-texte"** | "There is no outside-text" (*Of Grammatology*): we never reach a meaning outside chains of signification; often misread as "nothing exists but texts" |
| **Dissemination** | Meaning scatters rather than returns to a centre |

### The Yale School (US, 1970s)
- **Paul de Man** (*Blindness and Insight*, 1971; *Allegories of Reading*, 1979): rhetoric and figurative language undo the literal meaning; critics' insights come from their blind spots.
- **J. Hillis Miller** ("The Critic as Host", 1977): answering M.H. Abrams's charge that deconstruction is "parasitical", he argues that every text is already host and parasite to others; deconstruction does not dismantle a text but shows that it has already dismantled itself.
- **Geoffrey Hartman**, **Harold Bloom** (associated, though Bloom went his own way).
- **Barbara Johnson** (*The Critical Difference*, 1980): deconstruction is **not synonymous with destruction**; it is closer to the original meaning of "analysis", which etymologically means "to undo". It teases out the warring forces of signification within the text.

### "Deconstruction is not destruction" (2023): how to argue
- **Agree**: Derrida's own term comes from Heidegger's *Destruktion* (dismantling a tradition to see how it was built) but he chose "deconstruction" to avoid the sense of annihilation. Deconstruction reads closely and respectfully; it does not reject meaning but shows that meaning is plural and unstable. It is a double reading: first the dominant meaning, then the forces that undercut it. Johnson and Miller support this. Derrida also later wrote on ethics, justice ("Force of Law", 1990: justice itself is undeconstructible) and hospitality.
- **Qualify**: in practice it unsettles authority (of the author, of the canon, of fixed meaning), so traditionalists experienced it as destructive. Critics such as **M.H. Abrams** (1977) and **John Searle** accused it of nihilism and obscurity. **Paul de Man**'s wartime collaborationist journalism (revealed 1987) damaged the movement's reputation.

### Method
1. Identify the key binary oppositions in the text.
2. Show which term is privileged.
3. Find moments where the text undermines the hierarchy (a metaphor, a marginal character, a contradiction).
4. Show the "undecidable" point where meaning cannot settle.

### Applications
- ***Hamlet***: being/seeming, madness/sanity, action/thought: Hamlet's "antic disposition" collapses the line between feigned and real madness.
- **"Prufrock"**: "That is not what I meant at all": meaning always deferred.
- ***Waiting for Godot***: Godot as the absent centre (transcendental signified) that never arrives; the play is "free play" around an absence.
- ***The Scarlet Letter***: the letter A has no fixed signified (Adultery, Able, Angel): a near-perfect illustration of the floating signifier.
- **"Self-Reliance"**: the essay that tells us to trust ourselves and not books is itself a book of authoritative pronouncements.

### Criticisms
Nihilism and relativism; jargon and obscurity; ignores history and politics (Marxists' charge, though Derrida later wrote *Specters of Marx*, 1993); makes all readings equally valid; removes the human author.

## 6.4 Psychoanalytic criticism

Asked: 2017 (Freud on children's death-wishes; use for literature), 2020 (is psychoanalysis appropriate for reading literature?). MCQ 2026 (the uncanny).

### Definition
Psychoanalytic criticism uses the theories of Sigmund Freud and his successors to interpret literature: the author's psyche, the characters' unconscious motives, the reader's responses, and the text's own "unconscious" (its symbols, gaps and repetitions).

### Freud (1856–1939): key ideas
- **The unconscious**: repressed wishes, especially sexual and aggressive, that shape behaviour and return in disguised forms (dreams, slips, symptoms, art).
- ***The Interpretation of Dreams*** (1900): dreams are disguised wish-fulfilments; the **dream-work** uses **condensation** (several ideas in one image), **displacement** (feeling moved to a minor object), **symbolism** and secondary revision. "The royal road to the unconscious."
- **The Oedipus complex**: the child's desire for the parent of the opposite sex and rivalry with the same-sex parent; first set out in *The Interpretation of Dreams*, using Sophocles' *Oedipus Rex* and Shakespeare's *Hamlet* as evidence.
- **Id, ego, superego** (*The Ego and the Id*, 1923).
- **Defence mechanisms**: repression, projection, sublimation (desire turned into art or work), denial, regression.
- **"Creative Writers and Day-Dreaming"** (1908): the writer, like the child at play and the day-dreamer, fulfils wishes in fantasy, and disguises them so we can enjoy them without shame.
- **"The Uncanny"** (1919): the *unheimlich* (unhomely) is the frightening return of something familiar that was repressed: doubles, repetition, automata, the fear of being buried alive; analysis of Hoffmann's story "The Sandman".
- ***Beyond the Pleasure Principle*** (1920): **repetition compulsion** and the **death drive** (Thanatos) beside Eros.

### Children's death-wishes: Freud's evidence and reasoning (2017)
- In *The Interpretation of Dreams* (chapter 5, "Typical dreams": dreams of the death of persons of whom the dreamer is fond), Freud argues:
  1. **Evidence from dreams**: many patients dream, with grief, of the death of a parent or sibling; he argues these dreams fulfil a hidden childhood wish.
  2. **Observation of children**: children are frankly egoistic and hostile to siblings who compete for parents' love; a child's idea of "death" means only "being gone".
  3. **Clinical evidence**: neurotic patients' histories show early hostility to the same-sex parent and love for the other.
  4. **Myth and literature**: the universal power of *Oedipus Rex* shows we all recognise the hidden wish; *Hamlet* shows the same wish repressed (Hamlet cannot punish Claudius, who did what Hamlet wished).
- **Use in literature**: explains Hamlet's delay (Ernest Jones, 1949), Paul Morel's attachment (Kuttner, 1916), father–son rivalry (Edmund in *Lear*; Jamie and Edmund in *Long Day's Journey*); family dramas in general.
- **Objections**: unfalsifiable (Karl Popper); based on a small sample of Viennese patients; Freud abandoned his seduction theory controversially; anthropologist Bronisław Malinowski argued the complex takes different forms in other cultures (Trobriand Islanders).

### Later thinkers
| Thinker | Key ideas |
|---|---|
| **Carl Jung** | The **collective unconscious** and **archetypes** (shadow, anima/animus, the wise old man, the great mother); leads to archetypal criticism (Northrop Frye, Maud Bodkin) |
| **Ernest Jones** | *Hamlet and Oedipus* (1949) |
| **Jacques Lacan** | "The unconscious is structured like a language"; the **mirror stage** (the infant forms an ego from an image, a misrecognition); three orders: **Imaginary**, **Symbolic** (language, law, the Name-of-the-Father), **Real**; desire as lack; *objet petit a*; seminar on Poe's "The Purloined Letter" (1956) |
| **Harold Bloom** | *The Anxiety of Influence* (1973): poets struggle with strong precursors in an Oedipal rivalry |
| **Julia Kristeva** | *Powers of Horror* (1980): **abjection**; the semiotic (pre-linguistic, maternal) vs the symbolic |
| **Peter Brooks** | *Reading for the Plot* (1984): narrative desire and the death drive |
| **Norman Holland** | Reader-response psychoanalysis: readers recreate their identity themes |

### Method
- Look for repressed desires, family triangles, recurring symbols, dreams, doubles, slips, silences; treat characters as having unconscious motives; read the text's gaps as symptoms.

### Applications
- ***Sons and Lovers***: the classic Oedipal novel (Kuttner).
- ***Hamlet***: Jones; Lacan's seminar on Hamlet and desire (1959).
- **"Prufrock"**: anxiety, sexual fear, castration imagery (the head on a platter); the divided self.
- ***Long Day's Journey***: guilt, addiction, the family's repetition compulsion.
- ***Portrait***: the Oedipal bond with the mother; the "uncanny" in the hell sermons; sublimation of desire into art.
- ***King Lear***: Coppélia Kahn's absent mother.

### Is psychoanalysis appropriate for literature? (2020)
- **For**: literature is full of desire, dreams, symbols and family conflict; Freud drew his theories partly from literature; it explains ambiguity and the power of texts over readers; Lacan and Kristeva link it to language, which is literature's medium.
- **Against**: the **biographical fallacy** (analysing a dead author is guesswork); characters are not people with unconscious minds; reductionism (everything becomes sex); circularity (the theory finds what it assumes); Freud's sexism (penis envy) and cultural bias (feminist critique: Millett, Irigaray); scientific weakness (Popper; much of Freud's evidence disputed).
- **Judgement**: appropriate as one lens, especially with the text's language and the reader's response, not as a key that unlocks everything.

## 6.5 Feminist criticism

Asked: 2017 (female poetics: why women chose certain genres), 2021 (how feminist theories reshaped literary studies), 2024 (limitations of feminist criticism).

### Definition
Feminist criticism examines how literature represents women and gender, how patriarchy shapes writing, reading and the canon, and how women have written and been excluded. Its aims are both political (exposing and changing male dominance) and literary (recovering women writers and a female tradition).

### Waves and thinkers
| Phase | Thinker | Key ideas |
|---|---|---|
| Forerunners | **Mary Wollstonecraft**, *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman* (1792) | Women seem inferior because they are denied education |
| | **Virginia Woolf**, *A Room of One's Own* (1929) | A woman needs money (£500 a year) and a room of her own to write fiction; **Judith Shakespeare**, Shakespeare's imagined sister, whose genius is crushed; the absence of a female tradition ("we think back through our mothers if we are women"); the "woman's sentence"; the androgynous mind; women as "looking-glasses" that magnify men to twice their natural size |
| | **Simone de Beauvoir**, *The Second Sex* (1949) | "One is not born, but rather becomes, a woman": gender is made, not natural; woman as the **Other** to man's Subject |
| Second wave (Anglo-American) | **Kate Millett**, *Sexual Politics* (1970) | Patriarchy as a political system; attacks the sexual politics of D.H. Lawrence, Henry Miller and Norman Mailer |
| | **Elaine Showalter**, *A Literature of Their Own* (1977); "Towards a Feminist Poetics" (1979) | Three phases of women's writing: **feminine** (imitation, 1840–80), **feminist** (protest, 1880–1920), **female** (self-discovery, 1920–); two kinds of criticism: **feminist critique** (woman as reader of male texts) and **gynocriticism** (woman as writer; a female literary history) |
| | **Sandra Gilbert and Susan Gubar**, *The Madwoman in the Attic* (1979) | Male texts trap women in images of **angel or monster**; women writers suffer an "**anxiety of authorship**" (revising Bloom); the madwoman (Bertha in *Jane Eyre*) as the author's double and rage |
| | **Ellen Moers**, *Literary Women* (1976) | Women writers as a distinct tradition; "female Gothic" |
| French feminism | **Hélène Cixous**, "The Laugh of the Medusa" (1975) | **Écriture féminine**: women must write their bodies; writing that escapes phallocentric binary logic |
| | **Luce Irigaray**, *Speculum of the Other Woman* (1974) | Western philosophy as a mirror for men; female multiplicity |
| | **Julia Kristeva** | The semiotic chora vs the symbolic order |
| Later | **Judith Butler**, *Gender Trouble* (1990) | Gender is **performative**: produced by repeated acts, not an inner essence |
| | **bell hooks**, *Ain't I a Woman* (1981) | Mainstream feminism ignored Black and working-class women |
| | **Chandra Talpade Mohanty**, "Under Western Eyes" (1984) | Western feminism constructs a monolithic, victimised "Third World woman" |
| | **Gayatri Spivak**, "Can the Subaltern Speak?" (1988) | The colonised woman is doubly silenced; "white men saving brown women from brown men" |
| | **Toril Moi**, *Sexual/Textual Politics* (1985) | Critiques Showalter and Gilbert–Gubar for humanist assumptions; supports French theory |

### How feminism reshaped literary studies (2021)
1. **The canon reopened**: recovery of forgotten women writers (Aphra Behn, Kate Chopin's *The Awakening*, Charlotte Perkins Gilman's "The Yellow Wallpaper", Zora Neale Hurston).
2. **New questions about male texts**: images of women (angel, whore, madwoman), silencing (Ophelia, Gertrude).
3. **Gender as a category of analysis**, alongside class and race; led to gender studies, masculinity studies (Connell), queer theory (Sedgwick, Butler).
4. **Reading as a woman**: the "resisting reader" (Judith Fetterley, 1978).
5. **New genres valued**: diaries, letters, domestic novels, life-writing.
6. **Institutional change**: women's studies programmes; in Pakistan, Gender Studies is now an optional CSS subject in its own right.

### Female poetics: why women favoured certain genres (2017)
- **Woolf's argument** (*A Room of One's Own*): when women began to write, the novel was the only form young enough to be soft in their hands; epic and poetic drama were old forms, hardened by men. Women wrote in the common sitting-room with constant interruptions (Jane Austen hid her manuscripts), which suits prose and short forms, not epic.
- **Education**: epic and classical satire require Latin and Greek learning, denied to most women.
- **Public life**: drama needs the theatre (public, commercial, morally suspect for women); satire needs public authority and the right to judge society. Women's lives were private, so private forms (letters, diaries, lyric, the domestic novel) were open to them.
- **Gilbert and Gubar**: "anxiety of authorship": women writers felt illegitimate in male genres.
- **Showalter**: the female tradition developed in the novel.
- **Counter-examples**: Aphra Behn (drama, 1670s–80s), Elizabeth Barrett Browning's verse-novel *Aurora Leigh* (1856), modern women dramatists (Caryl Churchill). In Urdu: Ismat Chughtai, Qurratulain Hyder (novel), Parveen Shakir and Fahmida Riaz (lyric).

### Applications
- ***Hamlet***: Gertrude and Ophelia (Showalter, Rose).
- ***The Scarlet Letter***: Hester as a woman judged by a patriarchal theocracy and as a proto-feminist thinker.
- ***Sons and Lovers***: Millett's attack; Mrs Morel's thwarted intellect; Clara's suffragism.
- ***Pygmalion***: Eliza as an object made by men who claims independence.
- **"The Lady of Shalott"**: confinement and the punished gaze.
- **"Prufrock"** and *The Waste Land*: women as threatening arms and voices; the typist's mechanical seduction.
- ***Far from the Madding Crowd***: Bathsheba's independence and its limits.

### Limitations (2024)
1. **Essentialism**: some feminists speak of a universal female nature or experience, which repeats the patriarchal idea that biology is destiny (Butler, de Beauvoir's own position).
2. **Exclusion**: early Anglo-American feminism centred white, middle-class, Western, heterosexual women (hooks, Mohanty, Spivak).
3. **Reductionism**: reading literature only for its gender politics; neglect of form and aesthetic value; turning criticism into a tribunal.
4. **Anachronism**: judging older texts by modern standards (Shakespeare, Congreve).
5. **Internal divisions and jargon**: Anglo-American empiricism vs French theory; obscure language that excludes general readers.
6. **Separatism**: gynocriticism can create a "female ghetto"; neglect of men and masculinity.
7. **Danger of a new canon of approved texts**.
- **Conclusion**: the limits are real but feminism's core insight, that gender shapes writing and reading, has become part of all criticism.

## 6.6 Postcolonial criticism

Asked: 2016 (Western canonical text and colonial ideology), 2020 (is postcolonial studies the most flourishing branch of cultural studies?), 2026 (the contested "nation"). MCQ 2026 (hybridity).

### Definition
Postcolonial criticism studies literature produced by and about colonialism and its aftermath: how colonial texts represent colonised peoples, how colonised peoples write back, and how identity, language, nation and power are reshaped after empire.

### Key thinkers
| Thinker | Key ideas |
|---|---|
| **Frantz Fanon** (Martinique/Algeria) | *Black Skin, White Masks* (1952): the colonised internalise the coloniser's view of them; *The Wretched of the Earth* (1961): violence of colonialism and decolonisation; national culture; "**the pitfalls of national consciousness**": the national bourgeoisie may simply replace the colonisers |
| **Edward Said** (Palestinian-American) | *Orientalism* (1978): Western scholarship, literature and politics constructed the "Orient" as irrational, sensual, backward and unchanging, an Other that justified domination (drawing on Foucault's discourse and Gramsci's hegemony); *Culture and Imperialism* (1993): **contrapuntal reading**: reading canonical texts with the empire they take for granted (the Antigua sugar plantation behind Sir Thomas Bertram's wealth in Austen's *Mansfield Park*) |
| **Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak** | "Can the Subaltern Speak?" (1988): the **subaltern** (especially the poor colonised woman) cannot be heard within colonial or nationalist discourse; "Three Women's Texts and a Critique of Imperialism" (1985) on Bertha in *Jane Eyre*; "strategic essentialism" |
| **Homi K. Bhabha** | *The Location of Culture* (1994): **hybridity**, **mimicry** (the colonised imitating the coloniser: "almost the same, but not quite"), **ambivalence**, the **Third Space**; *Nation and Narration* (1990): the nation as a narrative, split between the "pedagogical" (official history) and the "performative" (daily life) |
| **Chinua Achebe** | "An Image of Africa: Racism in Conrad's *Heart of Darkness*" (1975/77): Conrad, a "thoroughgoing racist", made Africa a backdrop for European self-discovery; *Things Fall Apart* (1958) writes back |
| **Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o** | *Decolonising the Mind* (1986): African writers should write in African languages; language carries culture |
| **Ashcroft, Griffiths and Tiffin** | *The Empire Writes Back* (1989): "writing back" to the centre; english (local varieties) vs English |
| **Benedict Anderson** | *Imagined Communities* (1983): nations are imagined communities made possible by print capitalism |
| **Partha Chatterjee** | *Nationalist Thought and the Colonial World* (1986), *The Nation and Its Fragments* (1993): Indian nationalism was a "derivative discourse" of European models, yet it carved out an inner, spiritual domain (home, religion, culture) as sovereign; asks "Whose imagined community?" |
| **Aijaz Ahmad** | *In Theory* (1992): a Marxist critique of Said, Jameson's "national allegory" thesis, and postcolonial theory's metropolitan elitism |

### Method: questions to ask
- How does the text represent the colonised? What is silent or absent?
- What binary (civilised/savage, West/East, centre/margin) does it assume?
- Does the text support or undermine imperial ideology?
- How do language, hybridity and identity work in texts from colonised places?

### A canonical text and colonial ideology (2016)
- **Reinforcing**: Shakespeare's *The Tempest* (Caliban; not set, but a classic example); Kipling; Conrad (Achebe's reading); Austen's silence on Antigua (Said).
- **Using set texts**: ***Heartbreak House***: Lady Utterword's husband Hastings governs crown colonies and believes in ruling by force; Shaw satirises this. ***Pygmalion***: Pickering's study of Indian dialects; Higgins's "civilising mission" towards Eliza mirrors the colonial project. ***Portrait***: Ireland as a colony; the "tundish" scene (English as the coloniser's language). **"The Second Coming"**: Achebe's reappropriation. ***The Scarlet Letter***: Native Americans and the "Black Man" in the forest as the Puritan Other. **"Ulysses"**: the "savage race" Ulysses rules and the civilising hero as imperial type (Victorian imperial reading).
- **Undermining**: Joyce and Yeats (writing from a colonised Ireland); Shaw's satire of imperial arrogance.

### The most flourishing branch of cultural studies? (2020)
- **For**: rapid growth since the 1980s; global literatures in English (Rushdie, Achebe, Kamila Shamsie, Mohsin Hamid, Mohammed Hanif); influence on history, anthropology, IR; new fields (diaspora, decolonial studies, Indigenous studies, migration).
- **Against / qualify**: critics say it has become an academic fashion in Western universities (Aijaz Ahmad; Arif Dirlik, "The Postcolonial Aura", 1994, suspicious of its links to global capitalism); "post" is premature in a world of neo-imperialism and economic dependence; ecocriticism, digital humanities and world-literature studies now compete.

### The contested "nation" (2026)
1. **Anti-colonial nationalism as liberation**: Fanon, Gandhi, Jinnah, Sukarno: the nation is the vehicle of freedom.
2. **The nation as a European import**: Chatterjee's "derivative discourse"; Anderson's imagined communities; Tagore's critique of nationalism (*Nationalism*, 1917).
3. **The nation's internal exclusions**: Fanon's "pitfalls of national consciousness"; Spivak's subaltern; feminist critiques of the nation as male; ethnic and linguistic minorities.
4. **The nation as narrative**: Bhabha's split between pedagogical and performative; Jameson's controversial claim (1986) that all Third World texts are "national allegories" and Ahmad's rebuttal.
5. **Beyond the nation**: diaspora, hybridity, transnationalism, globalisation (Arjun Appadurai, *Modernity at Large*, 1996); "postnational" constellations.
- **Pakistan case**: the Two-Nation Theory (a religious-cultural nationalism); partition literature (Manto's "Toba Tek Singh", Intizar Husain, Bapsi Sidhwa's *Ice-Candy-Man*); 1971 and the Bengali language movement as a challenge to a single national narrative; Ayesha Jalal's work on the sole spokesman. Mention briefly and neutrally.

### Criticisms
Jargon and abstraction; Western academic base; tendency to homogenise "the colonised"; neglect of economics (Ahmad); "post" implies colonialism is over; risk of blaming all problems on colonialism and ignoring local power.
