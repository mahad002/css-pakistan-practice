# Model Answers — English Literature (CSS)

Nine full answers, each about 1,000–1,100 words including the outline: roughly what a strong candidate can write in 35 minutes. They are written as a candidate should write them: outline, thesis, headings, short quotations, named critics, a judgement. Quotations are given only from public-domain texts; copyrighted texts are paraphrased. Learn the **structure**, not the wording.

Topics chosen from the most repeated questions (see `02-Past-Paper-Analysis.md`):

## Model Answer 1

**Question (pattern of 2023/2026):** "Paul Morel's tragedy is that he can neither break free from his mother nor love any other woman wholly." Discuss the emotional conflicts of Paul Morel in *Sons and Lovers*.

**Outline**
1. Introduction and thesis
2. The roots: a loveless marriage and a mother's transferred passion
3. Miriam: love of the soul that the mother forbids
4. Clara: love of the body that cannot reach the self
5. The mother's death and the drift towards death
6. Critical perspectives: Lawrence, Kuttner, Millett
7. Conclusion

**1. Introduction**

D.H. Lawrence's *Sons and Lovers* (1913) is a novel about love that cannot find its proper object. Lawrence described his plan in a letter to Edward Garnett in 1912: a refined woman, disappointed in her husband, turns her passion to her sons; when they grow up and choose lovers, her hold on them prevents them from loving fully. Paul Morel is the son in whom this pattern is clearest. My argument is that Paul's emotional life is a series of divisions: between mother and lover, between spirit and body, between duty and desire. His tragedy lies not in any single choice but in a self that has been split before he is old enough to choose.

**2. The roots: a mother's transferred passion**

The conflicts begin before Paul is born. Gertrude Coppard, puritan and educated, marries Walter Morel, a warm, dancing miner, and soon despises his drinking, his lies and his lack of mind. In one of the novel's central scenes Walter locks his pregnant wife out of the house at night, and she stands among the moonlit lilies carrying the unborn Paul. The child is born into a war. The children side with their mother, and the father is pushed to the edge of his own family.

Mrs Morel's love, denied an outlet in marriage, flows into her sons. When William dies, she sinks into grief until Paul falls ill with pneumonia; nursing him, she turns her life to him. From this moment the bond is mutual and intense. Paul paints for her, wins prizes for her, works for her. Lawrence makes the mother admirable: strong, intelligent, morally serious. That is what makes her hold so powerful. Paul cannot simply rebel against a tyrant; he loves and respects the person who confines him.

**3. Miriam: the love of the soul**

Miriam Leivers of Willey Farm is Paul's first love. She shares his feeling for flowers, poetry and painting; she is intense, religious and shy. Their relationship is a communion of minds. Yet Paul comes to resent her. He feels she wants to absorb his soul and that she fears the body; when they finally become lovers, she gives herself as a sacrifice, and he feels the failure.

The mother is a third presence in every scene. Mrs Morel hates Miriam because Miriam claims exactly the part of Paul that she herself holds: his inner, spiritual self. Paul is torn between loyalty to his mother and his need for Miriam. He blames Miriam for what is really his own inability to give his soul to anyone but his mother. This is his first and deepest conflict: duty to the mother against desire for the beloved.

**4. Clara: the love of the body**

Clara Dawes, separated from her husband and active in the women's movement, offers what Miriam could not: frank physical passion. With Clara, Paul experiences sexual fulfilment and a sense of impersonal union with the larger life of nature. Mrs Morel tolerates Clara, because Clara does not threaten her possession of Paul's soul. But the affair cannot last. Paul gives Clara his body but not himself; she senses that she never reaches him. In the end Paul helps to reconcile her with her husband, Baxter Dawes, whom he once fought.

Miriam and Clara thus represent the two halves of a divided love: spirit without flesh and flesh without spirit. Lawrence later called the ideal union a "two-in-one", in which man and woman meet completely while each remains fully themselves. Paul cannot achieve this because the centre of his being is already occupied.

**5. The mother's death and the drift towards death**

The climax of the conflict is Mrs Morel's death from cancer. Paul and his sister, unable to bear her suffering, give her an overdose of morphia: an act of love that is also, symbolically, a release. Yet her death does not free him. In the final chapter, "Derelict", Paul feels the vast darkness of the night and is drawn to follow her into death. He has broken with Miriam, given up Clara, and lost his mother. Then, in the last lines, he turns away from the darkness and walks quickly "towards the faintly humming, glowing town". The ending is open. It suggests that Paul has at last recognised his isolation and chosen life, but it does not tell us whether he will ever love wholly.

**6. Critical perspectives**

In 1916 Alfred Booth Kuttner published the first Freudian reading of the novel, arguing that Paul is a textbook case of the Oedipus complex: his libido is fixed on his mother, so he can love Miriam only spiritually and Clara only sexually. Freud himself had described this split between "sacred" and "profane" love in 1912. The reading fits remarkably well, though Lawrence rejected it and later attacked Freud's theory of the unconscious.

F.R. Leavis praised the novel's psychological truth while noting that the mother's view dominates. Kate Millett, in *Sexual Politics* (1970), argued that Miriam and Clara are used as instruments of Paul's development and then discarded, and that the novel's sympathy lies with the male artist. Jessie Chambers, the original of Miriam, felt betrayed by the portrait. These criticisms remind us that Paul is not simply a victim: he can be selfish and cruel, and his conflicts are partly of his own making.

**7. Conclusion**

Paul Morel's emotional conflicts arise from a love that came too early and too completely from one source. His mother's passion gives him strength and confines him; Miriam offers his soul a home that his mother forbids; Clara offers his body a release that does not reach his self. Lawrence presents this as a tragedy shared by many young men of his class and time. But the final image of Paul walking towards the town keeps hope alive: the conflicts that have destroyed his loves may yet become the experience from which a whole man is made.

*(about 1,080 words)*

## Model Answer 2

**Question (2023/2025):** Discuss the significance of the title of Somerset Maugham's "The Lotus-Eater" in relation to the human condition.

**Outline**
1. Introduction and thesis
2. The myth: Homer and Tennyson
3. Wilson as a modern lotus-eater
4. The double irony of the title
5. The title and human fragility
6. Contrast with Tennyson's "Ulysses"
7. Conclusion

**1. Introduction**

Somerset Maugham's "The Lotus-Eater" (1935) tells the story of Thomas Wilson, a London bank manager who gives up his career to live a life of pure ease on the island of Capri, financed by a twenty-five-year annuity. The title is not decoration. It places a modern, quiet, almost ordinary story inside one of the oldest myths of the West, and it carries the story's moral. I will argue that the title works on two levels: Wilson is a lotus-eater because he chooses forgetfulness and pleasure over striving; but the deeper irony is that the "lotus" of ease does not bring oblivion at all. It slowly destroys the will on which his whole plan depended.

**2. The myth**

In Book 9 of Homer's *Odyssey*, Odysseus's men land among the Lotus-Eaters. Those who taste the honey-sweet fruit lose all desire to return home; they want only to stay and eat. Odysseus has to drag them weeping back to the ships. In 1832 Tennyson turned the episode into "The Lotos-Eaters", in which the mariners argue beautifully for rest: why should man alone toil, when all things else have rest? The myth therefore offers two ideas: the attraction of rest and forgetfulness, and the danger that such rest dissolves purpose and identity.

**3. Wilson as a modern lotus-eater**

Wilson fits the myth closely. He first sees Capri on holiday and is overwhelmed by its beauty. A year later he abandons London, his bank and any future duties. He makes no friends of any depth, has no ambition, and wants nothing except to swim, walk, read and look at the sea and the moonlight. The narrator, meeting him after fifteen years, finds him calm and content. Like the mariners, Wilson has forgotten "home": the world of work, effort and responsibility that had defined him.

The narrator, at the start, almost admires him. Most people, he says, do not choose their lives; circumstance shapes them. Wilson chose. His plan is not the act of a drunkard or a dreamer but of a rational man who has calculated that twenty-five years of perfect happiness are worth an early death.

**4. The double irony**

The title becomes ironic when the annuity ends. Wilson had promised himself that, if nature had not ended his life by sixty, he would end it himself. He does not. He sells his belongings, borrows from the islanders for a year, and only when credit runs out tries to suffocate himself with charcoal fumes. He fails; the fumes damage his brain, and he spends his last years as a half-witted drudge in his former landlord's woodshed, timid as a hunted animal, until he is found dead on a hillside overlooking the rocks whose beauty had first drawn him.

Here the title's second meaning appears. Homer's lotus brings forgetfulness. Wilson's "lotus" brought something worse: it weakened the very will he needed to keep his bargain. The narrator's conclusion is that the long years of ease, without struggle or difficulty, had drained his resolve, as muscles used only on level ground lose the power to climb a mountain. Wilson did not forget home; he forgot how to act. The title thus works as a quiet verdict. Maugham's restraint matters here: the narrator never sneers at Wilson, and the reader is left to feel both the beauty of the dream and the horror of its end.

**5. The title and human fragility**

The 2025 question links the title to suffering and fragility. Wilson's story shows how little control even the most rational person has over his future self. At thirty-five he could imagine dying calmly at sixty; at sixty he was an older, weaker man who wanted to live. The title points to a universal temptation: to believe we can arrange our lives so as to escape pain and effort. Maugham suggests that suffering cannot be bought off. The man who tried to purchase a painless life ends in the most painful and humiliating condition, dependent on the charity of people who had lived off him. The story is not a sermon against pleasure; it is a recognition that human beings are fragile, that will and character need exercise, and that no plan secures us against age and fear.

The interwar context sharpens this. After the First World War the Victorian creed of duty and work had lost its authority, and the Depression showed that security was an illusion. Wilson's escape answers the mood of the time; his fate exposes its cost.

**6. Contrast with "Ulysses"**

The title gains force when set beside Tennyson's "Ulysses", also on the syllabus. Ulysses, old and back in Ithaca, refuses rest: it is dull "to rust unburnish'd, not to shine in use", and his creed is "To strive, to seek, to find, and not to yield". Wilson is Ulysses' opposite: he rusts by choice. Tennyson's poem treats striving, even into death, as the essence of being human; Maugham's story shows what happens when striving is abandoned. Read together, they form a debate across a century about effort and rest.

A Pakistani reader will recall Iqbal, for whom life is perpetual motion and struggle, and stagnation a kind of death. Wilson's fate is an illustration of that idea.

**7. Conclusion**

The title "The Lotus-Eater" gives Maugham's story its moral structure. It identifies Wilson with the mythical sailors who chose sweet forgetfulness over the voyage home, and it then turns the myth against him: the lotus he ate did not bring peace but eroded the will that his peace depended on. Through a plain, ironic narrator and a modest plot, Maugham makes the title speak about the human condition: our dreams of escape are real and understandable, but a life without struggle leaves us defenceless before the suffering that no one can avoid.

*(about 1,020 words)*

## Model Answer 3

**Question (2019/2023):** "Happiness is not, except in very rare cases, something that drops into the mouth… it must be a conquest." Is happiness possible in the modern world? Discuss with reference to Russell's *The Conquest of Happiness*.

*(The quotation in the question is a paraphrase of Russell's idea, not an exact line.)*

**Outline**
1. Introduction and thesis
2. Russell's diagnosis: self-absorption and mistaken habits
3. The modern causes of unhappiness, then and now
4. Russell's remedies: zest, affection, work, impersonal interests
5. Is it possible today? Arguments for and against
6. Conclusion

**1. Introduction**

Bertrand Russell wrote *The Conquest of Happiness* in 1930, at a time when many intellectuals believed that science and modern life had made happiness impossible. Russell disagreed. The very word "conquest" in his title tells us his answer: happiness is not a gift of fortune but an achievement of intelligent effort. My thesis is that Russell's answer remains largely valid: happiness is possible in the modern world, because most modern unhappiness comes from habits of mind that can be changed; but Russell underestimates the outward causes of misery, and his conquest is easier for some people than for others.

**2. Russell's diagnosis**

Russell addresses not those suffering from poverty or illness, but the everyday unhappiness of people in comfortable societies, which has no obvious external cause. He traces it mainly to **self-absorption**. The sinner is obsessed with guilt, the narcissist with admiration, the megalomaniac with power; all three look inward. Russell confesses that as a young man he hated life and thought of suicide, and that he became happier as he cared less about himself and more about the world. This is the core of the book: happiness grows as attention turns outward.

**3. The modern causes of unhappiness**

The causes Russell lists in Part I read like a description of the twenty-first century.

- **Competition.** Russell describes the businessman for whom life is a race and success is survival. Today the race begins in school and continues on social media, where status is measured publicly in followers and likes. In Pakistan the competitive examinations, including CSS itself, can make a young person's whole worth depend on a single result.
- **Boredom and excitement.** Russell warns that a craving for constant excitement destroys the capacity for "fruitful monotony" from which great work comes. Endless scrolling is the modern form of that craving.
- **Envy.** Napoleon envied Caesar, Caesar envied Alexander, and Alexander envied Hercules, who never existed. Social media lets everyone compare themselves to idealised images of others; Russell's Hercules is now on every screen.
- **Fear of public opinion.** Russell advises the unconventional to seek a congenial circle rather than bow to the crowd. In our society, the question "what will people say?" governs weddings, careers and marriages.
- **Fatigue and worry.** Modern fatigue, he says, is nervous rather than physical. Anxiety is now the commonest mental complaint in the young.

If anything, the modern world has intensified every cause Russell names. This makes his question more urgent, not less.

**4. Russell's remedies**

Part II answers that happiness is still possible, and explains how.

- **Zest**: a healthy appetite for life, like that of a hungry man enjoying his dinner, as opposed to the invalid who eats from duty or the glutton who eats too much.
- **Affection**: being loved gives the security that makes adventure possible; the best love is mutual and not possessive.
- **Work**: even dull work usually beats idleness; work that uses skill and builds something lasting is a deep source of satisfaction.
- **Impersonal interests**: hobbies and curiosity that have nothing to do with one's career provide rest and a sense of proportion.
- **Effort and resignation**: strive to change what can be changed, accept what cannot.

His final picture is of the happy man who lives "objectively", with free affections and wide interests, feeling himself part of the stream of life rather than a separate, isolated unit.

Modern psychology largely confirms this. Martin Seligman's positive psychology identifies engagement, relationships and meaning as the pillars of well-being, and Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi's "flow", complete absorption in an activity, is close to what Russell called zest.

**5. Is it possible today?**

**For.** If unhappiness is largely self-made, it is largely curable. Russell's remedies require no wealth: curiosity, affection and work are available to most people. His rational approach to guilt and envy anticipates modern cognitive therapy. Even in a hurried, competitive society, people who cultivate wide interests and close relationships report greater happiness.

**Against.** Russell deliberately excludes poverty, illness and injustice. In a country like Pakistan, where inflation, unemployment and insecurity are daily facts, outward causes cannot be set aside. A Marxist critic would say Russell turns a social problem into an individual one. His advice also assumes the leisure and education of a middle-class reader. Finally, the forces that feed envy and distraction today are not just habits of mind but industries designed to capture attention.

**Russell's style as argument.** Part of the book's persuasiveness lies in its manner. Russell writes plainly, with wit and homely examples (the hungry diner, the envious emperors), and admits his own past misery. The style itself demonstrates his thesis: a mind turned outward, interested in the world and amused by human folly, is a happier mind than one turned inward in solemn gloom.

**Balance.** The objections narrow Russell's claim but do not defeat it. Even where external causes exist, the habits he describes decide how much they hurt. His book is best read as an answer to the private half of unhappiness, which is the half each person can change.

**6. Conclusion**

Russell's answer to the question "Is happiness still possible?" is yes, if it is conquered by turning outward, by cultivating zest, affection, work and impersonal interests, and by reasoning with destructive emotions. The modern world has multiplied the enemies he identified, but it has not changed the nature of the victory. The happiness Russell offers is not perfect or permanent, and it is harder for the poor and the oppressed than for the comfortable. But as a guide to the everyday unhappiness of modern people, *The Conquest of Happiness* remains remarkably sound.

*(about 1,020 words)*

## Model Answer 4

**Question (2021/2025):** "Whoso would be a man, must be a nonconformist." Discuss Emerson's idea of self-reliance, and comment on its resonance with the thought of Iqbal and Rumi.

**Outline**
1. Introduction and thesis
2. The meaning of self-reliance
3. Nonconformity and the two terrors
4. The religious basis: intuition and the Over-Soul
5. Evaluation: strengths and dangers
6. Resonance with Iqbal and Rumi
7. Conclusion

**1. Introduction**

Ralph Waldo Emerson's essay "Self-Reliance" (1841) is the classic American statement of individualism. Its most famous sentence, "Whoso would be a man, must be a nonconformist", is often read as simple advice to be different. In fact it rests on a religious claim: that each person has direct access to divine truth, and that conformity is a betrayal of that truth. My argument is that Emerson's nonconformity is a spiritual discipline rather than a social pose; and that this is exactly why it resonates with Rumi and Iqbal, though it also differs from them in its view of society and God.

**2. The meaning of self-reliance**

The essay begins: "Trust thyself: every heart vibrates to that iron string." To believe that your own thought is true for all people, Emerson says, is genius. We dismiss our own ideas because they are ours, and then hear them, with shame, from someone else. "Envy is ignorance" and "imitation is suicide": each person must accept his own portion and develop the power that is new in him.

**3. Nonconformity and the two terrors**

Society, Emerson argues, is "in conspiracy against the manhood of every one of its members". It values comfort and conformity, not independence. Hence the demand for nonconformity: "Nothing is at last sacred but the integrity of your own mind."

Two terrors, he says, scare us from self-trust. The first is **conformity**, the fear of other people's disapproval, the "sour faces" of the crowd. The second is **consistency**, our reverence for our own past words and acts. "A foolish consistency is the hobgoblin of little minds": a thinking person should say what he thinks today, even if it contradicts yesterday. If this makes him misunderstood, so be it: "To be great is to be misunderstood." Pythagoras, Socrates, Jesus, Luther, Copernicus, Galileo and Newton were all misunderstood.

**4. The religious basis**

Self-reliance is not selfishness because the self Emerson trusts is not the ego. He calls it the "aboriginal Self", the source of instinct and genius, and names its wisdom **Intuition**; all later teachings are mere "tuitions". Through intuition the soul receives truth directly from God, without priests, creeds or sacred books. In his essay "The Over-Soul" he describes the divine unity in which all souls share. To conform to custom, then, is to obey other men's opinions instead of the God within. This is the Transcendentalist idea, derived from Kant, Neoplatonism, and Emerson's reading of Eastern scriptures, that knowledge comes through the mind's intuitions rather than only through the senses.

**5. Evaluation**

The strength of the doctrine is obvious. It defends conscience against crowds, innovation against habit, and integrity against fashion. Thoreau's civil disobedience, which influenced Gandhi and Martin Luther King, grew from it. In any society governed by "what will people say", Emerson's essay is a tonic.

Its dangers are equally real. Herman Melville and Nathaniel Hawthorne mocked Transcendentalist optimism about human nature. Taken without its religious basis, self-reliance can become "rugged individualism", an excuse for selfishness and indifference to the poor. Emerson's freedom also assumed a propertied man's independence; women, slaves and labourers were not free to "trust themselves" in his sense. And intuition is not infallible: fanatics also trust their inner voice. Emerson's answer would be that true intuition is marked by humility and universality, not by hatred; but the essay gives no external test to tell the prophet from the fanatic. Its relevance today is also double-edged: advertising now sells "be yourself" as a slogan, so that nonconformity itself has become a market. Emerson's demand is harder than that: to think one's own thoughts, not to buy a different brand.

**6. Resonance with Iqbal and Rumi**

Emerson himself loved Persian poetry. He read Hafiz and Saadi in German translations, translated some of Hafiz himself, wrote a poem called "Saadi", and an essay on "Persian Poetry" (1858). The resonance with the Muslim mystical tradition is therefore not accidental.

**Rumi** (1207–1273) teaches that true knowledge comes from love and inner experience, not from book-learning; the *Masnavi* opens with the reed cut from its reed-bed, crying out with longing for its origin. Like Emerson, Rumi places the inner light above outward forms and finds the divine within the human heart.

**Iqbal** (1877–1938), who took Rumi as his spiritual guide, developed the philosophy of ***khudi*** (selfhood) in *Asrar-i-Khudi* (1915). The self must be strengthened through love (*ishq*), striving and creative action; weakness, imitation and dependence destroy it. Iqbal's attack on blind imitation (*taqlid*) is close to Emerson's "imitation is suicide", and his dynamic, ever-striving self resembles Emerson's restless genius.

The differences matter as much. Iqbal's self is completed in the community: in *Rumuz-i-Bekhudi* (1918) he insists that the individual finds fulfilment in the *millat*. Emerson is suspicious of all communities. Iqbal's and Rumi's God is personal, the Beloved to whom the self is drawn; Emerson's Over-Soul is closer to an impersonal, pantheistic spirit. And for Iqbal the strengthened self does not dissolve in God but grows towards Him, while Rumi's path leads towards annihilation (*fana*) in the Beloved. There is also no firm evidence that Iqbal studied Emerson closely; the resonance comes from a shared Neoplatonic and mystical inheritance, not direct influence.

**7. Conclusion**

Emerson's call for nonconformity is a call to obey the divine voice within rather than the voice of the crowd or of one's own past. It is a powerful defence of conscience and creativity, though it can be misused as a creed of selfishness. Its affinity with Rumi and Iqbal shows that the idea of a divinely grounded self, which must be defended against imitation and conformity, belongs to both Western and Eastern traditions. Iqbal adds what Emerson lacks: a self that fulfils itself in community. Read together, they offer a fuller picture of what it means to be truly oneself.

## Model Answer 5

**Question (2016/2024):** Trace the development of Wordsworth's attitude towards nature in "Lines Composed a Few Miles above Tintern Abbey". Can the poem be read ecocritically?

**Outline**
1. Introduction and thesis
2. The occasion and the return
3. Three stages: boyhood, youth, maturity
4. Memory and the "blessed mood"
5. Loss and "abundant recompence"
6. An ecocritical reading, and its limits
7. Conclusion

**1. Introduction**

"Tintern Abbey", the last poem in *Lyrical Ballads* (1798), is Wordsworth's most complete account of what nature has meant to him. Written on revisiting the Wye valley after five years, it is at once a description, an autobiography and an argument. My thesis is that the poem traces a growth from sensuous delight to moral and spiritual understanding, and that this growth, though centred on the poet's mind, contains an early ecological vision: a sense of humanity as part of a living whole.

**2. The occasion**

The poem opens with the poet's return: "Five years have past; five summers, with the length / Of five long winters!" He sees again the steep cliffs, the orchards, the hedgerows "hardly hedge-rows, little lines / Of sportive wood run wild", and the smoke rising among the trees. The landscape is human and natural at once, and it connects the scene "with the quiet of the sky". Wordsworth had first come here in 1793, aged twenty-three, in a state of crisis over the French Revolution and England's war with France.

**3. Three stages**

The heart of the poem describes three stages of his relationship with nature.

- **Boyhood**: the "coarser pleasures" and "glad animal movements" of childhood, purely physical.
- **Youth (1793)**: nature was "An appetite; a feeling and a love, / That had no need of a remoter charm, / By thought supplied". He bounded over the mountains like a roe, "more like a man / Flying from something that he dreads, than one / Who sought the thing he loved". This was passionate, sensuous and unreflective: "aching joys" and "dizzy raptures".
- **Maturity (1798)**: that time is past. Now he looks on nature "not as in the hour / Of thoughtless youth; but hearing oftentimes / The still, sad music of humanity". He feels "A presence that disturbs me with the joy / Of elevated thoughts; a sense sublime / Of something far more deeply interfused", a spirit that "rolls through all things", in the setting sun, the ocean, the air, the sky and "the mind of man".

The movement is from body to feeling to thought and spirit; from nature as playground to nature as passion to nature as teacher and divine presence.

**4. Memory and the "blessed mood"**

In the five years away, the memory of the scene had sustained him "in lonely rooms, and 'mid the din / Of towns and cities". It brought calm, perhaps prompted unremembered acts of kindness and love, and above all gave him a "blessed mood" in which, with the body asleep and the soul alive, "We see into the life of things". Nature thus works through memory as well as presence. The mind, he says, half creates what it perceives. This is the Romantic doctrine of the active imagination.

**5. Loss and recompense**

The mature attitude involves loss: the "dizzy raptures" will not return. But the poem refuses to mourn: "other gifts / Have followed; for such loss, I would believe, / Abundant recompence." The careful "I would believe" shows that the faith is hard-won. In the final section he turns to his sister Dorothy, in whose "wild eyes" he reads his former self, and prays that nature will do for her what it did for him, since "Nature never did betray / The heart that loved her". Nature becomes "The anchor of my purest thoughts, the nurse, / The guide, the guardian of my heart, and soul / Of all my moral being."

M.H. Abrams called this pattern of return, meditation and new insight the "greater Romantic lyric", and saw Romanticism as "natural supernaturalism": religious feeling transferred into nature.

**6. An ecocritical reading**

Ecocriticism, defined by Cheryll Glotfelty as the study of the relationship between literature and the physical environment, asks whether a text treats nature as a mere backdrop or as a value in itself.

**Supporting evidence.** The poem presents nature as a living, interconnected whole ("a motion and a spirit, that impels / All thinking things, all objects of all thought, / And rolls through all things"). Human life is woven into the landscape rather than set against it: the hedgerows return to wildness; smoke rises from dwellers in the woods. Nature has moral authority and heals the mind, an idea modern research on green space and mental health supports. Jonathan Bate, in *Romantic Ecology* (1991), argues that Wordsworth is a founder of ecological thinking, whose politics is a politics of the earth.

**Limits.** The poem is still centred on the human mind: nature matters mainly for what it does *for* the poet and his sister. Marjorie Levinson's New Historicist reading (1986) points out what the poem omits: the ruined abbey itself, the beggars who sheltered there, the iron works and charcoal burners that polluted the Wye in 1798, and the political crisis of the time. On this view the poem turns a damaged, social landscape into a private, idealised one.

**Judgement.** Both are right. The poem is not an environmental manifesto, but it gives Western literature one of its first sustained visions of humanity as part of a sacred, living nature, which is the root of modern environmental ethics. For a country facing deforestation, smog and floods, as Pakistan does, Wordsworth's idea of nature as a "guardian" to be honoured rather than a resource to be consumed is not merely poetic.

**7. Conclusion**

"Tintern Abbey" records Wordsworth's growth from animal delight through passionate love of nature to a mature, moral and spiritual relationship with it, mediated by memory and shared with his sister. The poem refuses to mourn the loss of youthful rapture because it has found deeper gifts. Read ecocritically, it anticipates our sense of the earth as a living whole, even if it remains centred on the human mind and silent about the industrial present. That combination of vision and limitation is why it continues to be read and argued over.

## Model Answer 6

**Question (2017):** A.C. Bradley argues that Hamlet's inaction springs from melancholy caused by his father's death and his mother's hasty remarriage. Why, in your view, does Hamlet delay killing Claudius?

**Outline**
1. Introduction and thesis
2. Is there a delay?
3. Bradley's melancholy theory
4. Other explanations: Coleridge, Jones, external obstacles, conscience
5. Testing the theories against the text
6. Act V: from delay to readiness
7. Conclusion

**1. Introduction**

The question of Hamlet's delay has dominated criticism of Shakespeare's tragedy for two centuries. The Ghost demands revenge in Act I; Hamlet kills Claudius only in the last scene, after five other deaths. A.C. Bradley, in *Shakespearean Tragedy* (1904), offered the most influential explanation: melancholy. My argument is that Bradley identifies the most important cause, but that no single theory explains the delay; Shakespeare builds it from psychological, moral and practical causes, and deliberately leaves it open.

**2. Is there a delay?**

Hamlet himself thinks so. After watching an actor weep for Hecuba he calls himself "a rogue and peasant slave" for doing nothing; after seeing Fortinbras's army march to fight for a worthless patch of ground, he reproaches himself again. In the closet scene the Ghost returns to "whet thy almost blunted purpose". So the play itself raises the problem.

**3. Bradley's theory**

Bradley rejects the view that Hamlet simply thinks too much. Before the play begins, he argues, Hamlet was a young man of idealism and energy. The shock of his father's sudden death, and above all his mother's remarriage within two months to a man far inferior, poisoned his faith in life and in women. His first soliloquy shows this: the world seems "an unweeded garden", and he cries "Frailty, thy name is woman!" This is melancholy in the Elizabethan sense: a morbid depression that paralyses the will, produces disgust with life, thoughts of suicide ("To be, or not to be") and sudden swings between apathy and excitement.

The theory explains much. It explains why Hamlet's disgust focuses on Gertrude rather than Claudius; why he can act impulsively (killing Polonius) but not deliberately; and why he keeps finding reasons not to act.

**4. Other explanations**

- **Excess of thought.** Coleridge saw in Hamlet a mind overbalanced towards contemplation; Goethe described a great deed laid on a soul too fine for it, like an oak planted in a costly vase. Hamlet's own line, "conscience does make cowards of us all", supports this.
- **The Oedipus complex.** Freud, in *The Interpretation of Dreams* (1900), and Ernest Jones in *Hamlet and Oedipus* (1949), argued that Claudius did what Hamlet unconsciously wished: killed his father and married his mother. To kill Claudius would be to punish his own repressed desire; hence paralysis.
- **External obstacles.** Some critics argue the problems are practical: Claudius is a guarded king; the Ghost may be a devil sent to tempt him, so Hamlet needs proof, which he seeks through the play-within-the-play.
- **Conscience and religion.** Revenge is forbidden to a Christian. When Hamlet finds Claudius praying, he spares him so as not to send him to heaven; this may be a rationalisation, or genuine moral struggle.
- **Artistic failure.** T.S. Eliot argued that Hamlet's emotion exceeds the facts and that Shakespeare failed to find an "objective correlative" for it.

**5. Testing against the text**

The external-obstacles theory is weak: Hamlet never says he lacks opportunity, and after *The Mousetrap* he is certain of Claudius's guilt. The excess-of-thought theory is too simple: Hamlet acts swiftly at times, stabbing Polonius through the arras, sending Rosencrantz and Guildenstern to their deaths, boarding the pirate ship. Eliot's charge mistakes the play's openness for failure. The Oedipal reading is suggestive, especially in the closet scene, where Hamlet's obsession with his mother's sexuality is overpowering, but it depends on a theory that cannot be proved.

Bradley's account fits best because it unites the evidence: the shock of the remarriage, the disgust with women and the world, the suicidal reflections, the bursts of energy and the paralysis of deliberate will. Yet even Bradley's Hamlet is partly a nineteenth-century character study; the play also gives Hamlet moral reasons (is revenge right? is the Ghost honest?) that melancholy alone cannot explain.

Shakespeare also uses foils to sharpen the question. Laertes, whose father Polonius is killed by Hamlet, returns at once, raises a mob and is ready "to cut his throat i' the church". Fortinbras, whose father was killed by old Hamlet, leads an army to fight for honour over "an eggshell". Both act without hesitation, and both are cruder men than Hamlet. Laertes is easily manipulated by Claudius into treachery with a poisoned sword. The contrast suggests that Shakespeare does not simply condemn delay: the man who acts at once becomes a tool of others, while the man who thinks is paralysed. Hamlet's delay is the price of a conscience that the simple avengers do not possess.

**6. Act V: from delay to readiness**

The final act changes the question. Returning from the sea voyage, Hamlet is calmer. He tells Horatio, "There's a divinity that shapes our ends, / Rough-hew them how we will", and before the duel, "the readiness is all". He no longer tries to plan revenge; he waits for providence. When the moment comes, after Gertrude is poisoned and Laertes confesses, he kills Claudius at once. This suggests that the delay was not only a disease but a moral education: Hamlet moves from the frantic belief that he must "set it right" to an acceptance that he is part of a larger order.

**7. Conclusion**

Hamlet delays for several reasons that reinforce each other. Bradley's melancholy, rooted in his father's death and his mother's remarriage, is the deepest psychological cause; Hamlet's reflective temperament, his moral doubts about revenge and the Ghost, and perhaps unconscious Oedipal conflict add to it. Shakespeare never settles the question, and that is the point. As Hazlitt said, "It is we who are Hamlet": every reader finds in the delay a mirror of his own hesitation before action, which is why the play remains inexhaustible.

## Model Answer 7

**Question (2023):** Explain Jacques Derrida's theory of deconstruction. Do you agree that "deconstruction is not destruction"?

**Outline**
1. Introduction and thesis
2. Background: from structuralism to poststructuralism
3. Derrida's key ideas
4. The deconstructive method, with examples
5. Why deconstruction is not destruction
6. The charge of destruction: critics
7. Conclusion

**1. Introduction**

Deconstruction is the name given to the way of reading developed by the French philosopher Jacques Derrida (1930–2004) in *Of Grammatology*, *Writing and Difference* and *Speech and Phenomena* (all 1967). It became the most controversial movement in literary theory in the 1970s and 1980s, especially through the "Yale School". Opponents accused it of destroying meaning, literature and reason. My argument is that deconstruction, properly understood, is not destruction but a form of rigorous close reading that shows how texts unsettle the oppositions they depend on. It undoes certainty; it does not abolish meaning.

**2. Background**

Structuralism, following Saussure, taught that meaning arises from **difference** within a system of signs: a word means what it does because it is not other words. Structuralists such as Lévi-Strauss looked for the stable binary oppositions beneath myths and texts. In his 1966 lecture "Structure, Sign and Play", Derrida pointed out that every structure assumes a fixed centre that holds it together and is itself outside the play of differences. Once we see that the centre is also a sign, there is no fixed point: only the free play of signification. This marks the move to poststructuralism.

**3. Derrida's key ideas**

- **Logocentrism and the metaphysics of presence.** Western philosophy, from Plato onwards, has sought a final ground of meaning that is fully present: God, reason, the speaker's intention, being. Derrida calls this desire logocentrism.
- **Binary oppositions.** This thought is organised in hierarchical pairs: speech/writing, presence/absence, nature/culture, male/female, reason/madness. The first term is privileged; the second is treated as secondary or derivative.
- **Phonocentrism.** Western thought has privileged speech over writing, as closer to the speaker's living presence. Derrida shows that the features blamed on writing (absence, repetition, distance from origin) belong to all language.
- **Différance.** His coinage combines "to differ" and "to defer": meaning arises from difference and is endlessly postponed along a chain of signifiers. The "a" can be seen but not heard in French, a small proof that writing is not secondary to speech.
- **Trace and supplement.** Each sign carries traces of what it is not. A supplement, something added to a supposedly complete whole, reveals the whole was incomplete.
- **"There is no outside-text."** Often misread as "nothing exists but texts", it means that we never step outside chains of signs to reach a meaning that is simply present.

**4. The method**

Deconstruction works in two steps. First it **reverses** a hierarchy, showing that the "secondary" term is in fact necessary to the "primary" one. Then it **displaces** the opposition itself, showing that neither term can stand alone. It does this through close attention to details, metaphors, footnotes and contradictions that the text tries to keep at the margin.

*Examples.* In *Hamlet*, the opposition between real and feigned madness collapses: Hamlet's "antic disposition" becomes indistinguishable from genuine disturbance. In *The Scarlet Letter*, the letter "A" is meant to fix Hester's identity as Adulteress, but its meaning slides to "Able", "Angel" and art: the signifier cannot be pinned to a single signified. In *Waiting for Godot*, the absent Godot functions as the centre that organises the tramps' lives yet never arrives: the play stages Derrida's absent centre.

**5. Why deconstruction is not destruction**

There are strong reasons to agree with the statement.

- **The word itself.** Derrida took the term from Heidegger's *Destruktion*, the dismantling of a philosophical tradition in order to see how it was built. He chose "deconstruction" precisely to avoid the sense of annihilation. To deconstruct a building is to understand its construction.
- **Barbara Johnson** put it clearly in *The Critical Difference* (1980): deconstruction is not synonymous with destruction; it is closer to the original meaning of "analysis", which etymologically means "to undo". It teases out the warring forces of signification within a text.
- **J. Hillis Miller**, answering M.H. Abrams's charge that deconstruction was "parasitical" on real criticism, argued in "The Critic as Host" (1977) that the deconstructive critic does not tear a text apart but shows that the text has already dismantled itself.
- **Meaning is multiplied, not abolished.** Deconstruction begins by reading the dominant meaning carefully; it then shows other meanings at work. It requires more attention to the text, not less. A careless reader cannot deconstruct anything; the method depends on noticing the smallest metaphor or marginal detail.
- **Ethical turn.** Derrida's later writings on justice ("Force of Law", 1990), friendship and hospitality show a deeply ethical project. He even declared that justice itself is not deconstructible.

**6. The charge of destruction**

The opposite view also has force. Deconstruction does destroy certain things: the authority of the author's intention, the idea of a single correct reading, the stability of the canon. Traditional critics such as M.H. Abrams accused it of making all readings equally valid, which in practice could mean none. The philosopher John Searle, in a famous debate (collected in *Limited Inc*), accused Derrida of obscurity and evasion. Marxist critics complained it ignored history and politics, though Derrida later wrote *Specters of Marx* (1993). The revelation in 1987 that Paul de Man had written collaborationist journalism in wartime Belgium damaged the movement. And the jargon of its followers often obscured more than it revealed.

**7. Conclusion**

Deconstruction is a strategy of reading that exposes the instability of the oppositions on which texts and philosophies are built. It is destructive only of a particular kind of certainty: the belief that meaning is fixed, present and controlled by its author. In every other sense it is constructive: it demands closer reading, reveals richer meanings and makes readers aware of the assumptions they bring. I therefore agree that deconstruction is not destruction, with one qualification: it is an undoing, and those who believed meaning was simple have every reason to feel undone.

## Model Answer 8

**Question (2021/2024):** How have feminist theories reshaped literary studies? Evaluate their limitations.

**Outline**
1. Introduction and thesis
2. Foundations: Woolf and de Beauvoir
3. The second wave: critique and gynocriticism
4. French feminism and gender as performance
5. How literary studies changed
6. Limitations
7. Conclusion

**1. Introduction**

Feminist criticism examines how literature represents women, how patriarchy has shaped the writing, reading and judging of literature, and how women writers have been excluded from or written within the tradition. From the 1970s it transformed university English departments. My argument is that feminism changed literary studies more deeply than almost any other theory, by making gender a basic category of analysis; but that it has faced serious limitations, many of which feminists themselves have exposed.

**2. Foundations**

Virginia Woolf's *A Room of One's Own* (1929) laid the ground. A woman, she argued, must have money and a room of her own if she is to write fiction. Through the imagined figure of Shakespeare's gifted sister, Judith, who is denied education, mocked, seduced and driven to suicide, Woolf showed that genius depends on material conditions. She noted the absence of a female tradition and the difficulty of writing in a sentence shaped by men.

Simone de Beauvoir's *The Second Sex* (1949) added the philosophical claim: "One is not born, but rather becomes, a woman." Femininity is a social construction, and woman has been defined as the Other to man's Subject.

**3. The second wave**

Kate Millett's *Sexual Politics* (1970) treated patriarchy as a political system and analysed the sexual politics of novelists such as D.H. Lawrence, Henry Miller and Norman Mailer. Her reading of Lawrence is directly relevant to *Sons and Lovers*: Miriam and Clara serve Paul's development and are discarded.

Elaine Showalter (*A Literature of Their Own*, 1977) distinguished **feminist critique**, the study of women as readers of male texts, from **gynocriticism**, the study of women as writers, and traced three phases of women's writing: feminine, feminist and female. Sandra Gilbert and Susan Gubar (*The Madwoman in the Attic*, 1979) showed how male texts confined women to the images of angel or monster, and how women writers suffered an "anxiety of authorship"; the madwoman locked in the attic of *Jane Eyre* became the emblem of suppressed female rage.

**4. French feminism and gender as performance**

French feminists approached the problem through language and psychoanalysis. Hélène Cixous ("The Laugh of the Medusa", 1975) called for *écriture féminine*, writing that escapes the binary logic of a phallocentric order. Luce Irigaray and Julia Kristeva explored the feminine as what is repressed by the symbolic order. Later, Judith Butler's *Gender Trouble* (1990) argued that gender is **performative**: produced by repeated acts rather than expressing an inner essence.

**5. How literary studies changed**

1. **The canon reopened.** Forgotten writers were recovered: Aphra Behn, Kate Chopin, Charlotte Perkins Gilman, Zora Neale Hurston. Syllabuses changed worldwide.
2. **New readings of old texts.** Ophelia and Gertrude in *Hamlet* (Elaine Showalter's "Representing Ophelia"; Jacqueline Rose on Eliot's view of Gertrude), Hester Prynne in *The Scarlet Letter*, Eliza in *Pygmalion* and the confined Lady of Shalott were reread as figures shaped by male power.
3. **Gender as a category.** Alongside class and race, gender became a basic question in reading any text; this led to gender studies, masculinity studies (R.W. Connell) and queer theory (Eve Kosofsky Sedgwick).
4. **The reader.** Judith Fetterley's "resisting reader" (1978) taught women not to identify passively with male perspectives.
5. **New genres valued.** Letters, diaries, memoirs and domestic fiction, long dismissed as minor, gained critical respect.
6. **Institutional change.** Women's studies programmes were created; in Pakistan, Gender Studies is now a CSS optional subject in its own right.

**6. Limitations**

- **Essentialism.** Some feminist criticism spoke of a universal female experience or a uniquely female language, risking the very biological determinism that de Beauvoir rejected. Butler and others criticised this.
- **Exclusion.** Early Anglo-American feminism largely reflected white, middle-class, Western, heterosexual women. bell hooks (*Ain't I a Woman*, 1981) showed how Black women were erased; Chandra Talpade Mohanty ("Under Western Eyes", 1984) criticised the image of a single, passive "Third World woman"; Gayatri Spivak asked whether the subaltern woman can speak at all.
- **Reductionism.** Reading literature only for its gender politics can turn criticism into a tribunal and neglect form, style and aesthetic value.
- **Anachronism.** Judging Shakespeare or Congreve by twentieth-century standards can obscure what was progressive in their own time.
- **Internal division and jargon.** Toril Moi (*Sexual/Textual Politics*, 1985) criticised Showalter and Gilbert and Gubar for humanist assumptions, while French theory was accused of abstraction and obscurity.
- **Separatism.** Gynocriticism risked creating a separate female canon rather than transforming the whole field, and early feminism paid little attention to men and masculinity.

These limitations are real, but much of the answer to them came from within feminism itself: intersectional, postcolonial and queer feminisms widened the field.

The limitations can be seen in practice in readings of the syllabus texts. A crude feminist reading of *Hamlet* that simply condemns Hamlet's misogyny misses how the play itself exposes the pressure on Ophelia; Showalter's more careful approach examines how Ophelia has been represented, rather than merely accusing Shakespeare. Millett's attack on Lawrence is powerful, yet it can flatten the novel's genuine sympathy for Mrs Morel's thwarted intelligence and Clara's independence. In Pakistan, feminist criticism also has to reckon with local traditions: the Urdu writers Ismat Chughtai and Rashid Jahan challenged patriarchy decades before Western second-wave theory, which suggests that the theory's Anglo-American history is only one strand of a wider story.

**7. Conclusion**

Feminist theory reshaped literary studies by recovering women writers, rereading the canon, making gender central to interpretation and changing who and what is studied. Its limitations, including essentialism, exclusion and reductionism, have been serious, but they were largely identified and corrected by feminists themselves. Its core insight, that gender shapes how literature is written, read and valued, is now part of every serious critical approach.

## Model Answer 9

**Question (2017/2018):** "*The Waste Land* is not so much about war as about the trauma left by war, and the collapse of modern faith in order." Discuss.

**Outline**
1. Introduction and thesis
2. Context: the war, the poet and the peace
3. War present by allusion
4. The trauma: sterility, neurosis, broken relationships
5. The collapse of order: fragments and the mythical method
6. Is there hope? The thunder
7. Critical views and conclusion

**1. Introduction**

T.S. Eliot's *The Waste Land* (1922) appeared four years after the end of the First World War. It contains no battle scenes and few direct references to the war, yet it is often read as the poem of the post-war generation. My argument is that the statement is right: the poem is about the psychological and spiritual aftermath of the war rather than the war itself, and its form, a heap of fragments, enacts the collapse of the beliefs in order, meaning and the sublime that had sustained European civilisation.

**2. Context**

The war killed millions, destroyed empires and shook faith in progress, religion and reason. Eliot, an American working in a London bank, suffered a nervous breakdown in 1921 and wrote much of the poem while recovering at Margate and Lausanne. His unhappy marriage added private distress. Ezra Pound edited the drafts severely, and the poem is dedicated to him. Eliot drew on Jessie Weston's study of the Grail legend, *From Ritual to Romance*, and on Frazer's *The Golden Bough*: the myth of a wounded Fisher King whose land is barren until it is healed.

**3. War present by allusion**

The war is felt everywhere but rarely named. In "The Burial of the Dead", a crowd flows over London Bridge in the brown fog: "I had not thought death had undone so many", a line from Dante's *Inferno* that turns London's clerks into the dead. The speaker greets an old comrade, Stetson, who was with him "in the ships at Mylae", an ancient battle that stands in for the recent one, and asks whether "That corpse you planted last year in your garden" has begun to sprout. In "A Game of Chess", Lil's husband Albert has been "demobbed". In "What the Thunder Said", hooded hordes swarm over endless plains and cities fall: "Jerusalem Athens Alexandria / Vienna London / Unreal". The war is a buried presence, like the corpse in the garden. Even the section "Death by Water", with Phlebas the Phoenician drowned and forgotten, reads as an epitaph for all the young men whose bodies the war consumed. The poem's indirectness is itself a sign of trauma: what cannot be faced directly returns in disguised images, as in a dream.

**4. The trauma**

Trauma appears as sterility, nervous breakdown and failed human relations. "April is the cruellest month" because spring's renewal is painful to those who would rather remain numb. The rich woman of "A Game of Chess" cries "My nerves are bad to-night", and her silent companion thinks "we are in rats' alley / Where the dead men lost their bones". In the pub, the women's talk of Lil, aged by pills taken to end a pregnancy, shows exhaustion and the failure of fertility. In "The Fire Sermon", Tiresias watches a typist and a "young man carbuncular" have joyless, mechanical sex. Love has become routine, and sex has lost its link with creation. These are the symptoms of a society in shock: not war, but its long shadow.

**5. The collapse of order**

The form of the poem embodies the collapse. Five sections, many voices, six or more languages, quotations from Shakespeare, Dante, Spenser, Augustine, the Buddha and the Upanishads, set side by side without connecting argument. The prophetic voice describes "A heap of broken images", and the poem ends with the line "These fragments I have shored against my ruins". The great structures of the past, Christian, classical and romantic, survive only as fragments.

Eliot's answer is what he later called the "mythical method", in his 1923 review of Joyce's *Ulysses*: a way of controlling and giving shape to the futility and anarchy of contemporary history by setting it against ancient myth. The Grail legend gives the poem a buried structure: the land is waste, the king is wounded, the quester must ask the right question.

**6. Is there hope?**

In the final section the thunder speaks: "DA". Eliot interprets it through the *Brihadaranyaka Upanishad* as *Datta, Dayadhvam, Damyata*: give, sympathise, control. These are moral answers to the sterility of the earlier sections. Rain seems to be coming. The Fisher King asks, "Shall I at least set my lands in order?" The poem ends with "Shantih shantih shantih", the formal close of an Upanishad. But the peace is in a foreign tongue, the rain never actually falls, and the order remains a question. The poem offers the possibility of renewal, not its achievement.

**7. Critical views and conclusion**

I.A. Richards (1926) argued that the poem effects "a complete separation between poetry and all beliefs", expressing the plight of a generation that had lost them. F.R. Leavis (1932) saw it as the great modern poem whose unity is one of mood. Cleanth Brooks (1939) read it as a Christian poem about the possibility of spiritual rebirth. Eliot himself later dismissed it as the relief of a personal grouse against life, a reminder that private suffering lies under the public theme.

These views can be reconciled. *The Waste Land* is not a war poem in the manner of Owen or Sassoon. It is a poem of aftermath, in which a personal breakdown becomes a picture of a civilisation's breakdown. Its trauma is spiritual and sexual sterility; its subject is the collapse of faith in order and the sublime; its method is fragmentation held together by myth. That is why it spoke so powerfully to the generation that survived the war, and why it still speaks to any society living among ruins.
