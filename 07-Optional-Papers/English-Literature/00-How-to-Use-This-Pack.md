**What this means:**

- **2023 looks acceptable** (92% passed the subject, a fifth scored 60%+). **2025 looks bad**: the typical candidate failed the paper. Two years, very different pictures.
- The numbers are tiny (64 and 68 candidates), so one strict examiner can move the mean a lot. That cuts both ways: the subject is **high-variance**. You cannot predict your score from the data.
- Compare Gender Studies, the alternative in Group V: 9% failed and 55% scored 60%+ in 2023; mean 56% in 2025, with 8,245 candidates.
- The research file rates English Literature "Avoid" for the recommended plan. It is in the English-strength combination because the candidate writes well and may enjoy it, **not** because the data favours it.
- **Workload**: six novels, nine plays, about twenty poems, five essays, three stories and six theories. Reading the texts alone takes about 80–100 hours.

**My estimate** (judgement, not data): a well-prepared candidate who reads the core texts, learns the theory and writes argued answers should expect **45–60**. A candidate who writes plot summaries risks **under 33**, which fails the whole exam.

## 3. What examiners want

FPSC's examiner report for CSS 2018 (CE-2018) said of this subject: most candidates could not write correct English; most did not know how to develop an argument; instead of answering the question, they merely summarised the work; they lacked critical thinking. Other examiner comments (2016, reported in the press) say the same: summaries of set texts, poor grammar and vocabulary.

So the marks go to:

| What earns marks | How to show it |
|---|---|
| Answering the question asked | Use the question's key words in your thesis and headings. If it quotes a critic, agree, disagree or qualify |
| Argument | One clear thesis; every section advances it |
| Textual evidence | Short quotations (public-domain texts) or precise references (scene, chapter, image) for every claim |
| Critical awareness | Name two or three critics and state their views accurately; set them against each other |
| Context | One paragraph: period, movement, author's life where relevant |
| Theory | One theory lens where useful (Freud on Lawrence, feminism on *Hamlet*) |
| Balance | A counter-view before your judgement |
| English | Short, correct sentences; no jargon you cannot explain |

## 4. The answer template that scores (20 marks, 35 minutes)

**Plan (5 minutes).** Underline the command word and every part of the question. Decide your thesis. Jot 5–7 headings and, against each, one quotation or reference and one critic.

**1. Outline (5–7 lines).** Numbered headings. Examiners skim; an outline tells them the answer is organised.

**2. Introduction (100–120 words).** Author, text, date and movement in one sentence. Define the key term in the question (tragedy, ecocriticism, deconstruction). **Thesis**: your direct answer.

**3. Body (650–800 words, 4–6 headed sections).** Each heading is a claim, not a label: "Paul's love for Miriam is blocked by his mother", not "Miriam". Under each:
- the point in one sentence;
- evidence (a short quotation or exact reference);
- analysis: how this proves the point;
- a critic, where one fits.

**4. Counter-view (80–120 words).** "However, critics such as … argue …" Then answer it.

**5. Conclusion (60–80 words).** Restate the thesis in fresh words; widen briefly (relevance today, link to another text, Pakistani or Urdu parallel). No new evidence.

**For theory questions**, use this order: definition and origin → core assumptions (numbered) → key thinkers and terms (a small table is fine) → method → application to one or two set texts → limitations → conclusion. The application to a set text is what most candidates leave out.

**Length:** about 1,000–1,100 words per answer, 4–5 sides of the answer book. Quality over length.

## 6. Common mistakes

1. **Retelling the plot.** The single biggest complaint of examiners. Summarise only what your argument needs, in a sentence.
2. **Ignoring the critic in the question.** If the question quotes Bradley or Hazlitt, the answer must engage with that view.
3. **Inventing quotations.** A misquotation looks worse than a paraphrase. Quote only lines you are sure of; otherwise paraphrase precisely.
4. **Theory without texts.** Listing Derrida's terms without applying them to a set text.
5. **Texts without theory.** Recent papers (2024–2026) ask for ecocritical, gender and masculinity readings of set texts.
6. **Guidebook English.** Memorised paragraphs with "In a nutshell" and "To cut a long story short". Write plainly.
7. **Choosing a question you half-know** over one you know well but find less attractive.
8. **Mixing up critics** (Bradley vs Coleridge on Hamlet; Showalter vs Gilbert and Gubar). Use `07-Quick-Revision.md` to drill names.
9. **No counter-view**: one-sided answers rarely pass 12/20.

## 8. Study plan: 4 hours a week, Feb 2027 → Jan 2028

About 50 weeks × 4 hours = 200 hours. Two 2-hour sessions a week. Reading the set texts is the biggest cost, so reading is spread across the year rather than crammed.

**Fitting it into the master schedule.** In `../../01-Plan/Study-Schedule.md` each 100-mark optional gets concentrated blocks (B1 → B4). In the English-strength combination, English Literature is **B2** (order: Public Administration, English Literature, Criminology, Journalism). This subject needs steady reading more than a block, so if you choose it, start reading the texts in the B1 weeks (one novel or play a fortnight in spare time) and use the B2 block (weeks 14–19) for notes and answers. The plan below shows the work in order at an even 4 hours a week; compress it into your actual slots.

**Priority order** (from `02-Past-Paper-Analysis.md`): Essays and Short stories → Theory → *Sons and Lovers* → Wordsworth, Keats, Eliot → *Hamlet* → *Long Day's Journey*, *Scarlet Letter* → the rest.
