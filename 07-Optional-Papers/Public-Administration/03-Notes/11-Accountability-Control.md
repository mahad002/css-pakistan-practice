# XI. Public Administration Accountability and Control

Eight questions 2016–2026: separation of powers (2017, 2026), corruption (2018, 2021, 2024), civil society (2019 note), social audit (2021), citizen engagement (2023). One of the safest sections to prepare.

---

## 1. Bureaucratic responsiveness

- **Meaning:** the degree to which administration acts on the wishes and needs of citizens and their elected representatives.
- **Types:** to political leaders (political responsiveness); to citizens/clients (service responsiveness); to law and professional standards.
- **Tension:** responsiveness vs neutrality and rule of law — responding to every political demand leads to politicisation; responding only to rules leads to rigidity.
- **Tools:** citizen charters, complaint systems (Pakistan Citizen's Portal), service standards, surveys, open data, participatory planning, decentralisation, performance targets based on citizen outcomes.

## 2. Representative bureaucracy

- **J. Donald Kingsley, *Representative Bureaucracy* (1944):** British civil service reflected the dominant class; a bureaucracy should mirror society to be democratic.
- **Frederick Mosher (1968):** **passive** representation (the service resembles society in origin — gender, region, class) vs **active** representation (officials act for the interests of the group they come from).
- **Arguments for:** legitimacy, better understanding of diverse clients, equal opportunity, symbolic value.
- **Arguments against:** may reduce merit; active representation conflicts with neutrality; groups not homogeneous.
- **Pakistan:** regional quotas since 1948/1949 (designed to include East Pakistan and smaller provinces), women's 10% quota, minorities' 5%; debate over extending quotas indefinitely (the Constitution's Article 27 allowed quotas for a limited period, repeatedly extended **[verify current position]**).

## 3. Citizen engagement (asked 2023)

- **Meaning:** involvement of citizens in decisions and in delivering services — informing, consulting, collaborating, co-producing.
- **Sherry Arnstein's ladder of participation (1969), eight rungs:** manipulation, therapy (non-participation); informing, consultation, placation (tokenism); partnership, delegated power, citizen control (citizen power).
- **IAP2 spectrum:** inform, consult, involve, collaborate, empower.
- **Forms:** public hearings, consultations on draft laws, participatory budgeting (Porto Alegre 1989), citizen juries, social audit, community-driven development, co-production (e.g. Orangi Pilot Project's community sanitation in Karachi, 1980), e-participation (online portals), citizen feedback (Punjab's proactive calls to citizens after service).
- **Pakistan examples:** Citizen Community Boards under LGO 2001; Pakistan Citizen's Portal; Punjab Citizen Feedback Monitoring Program; school management councils; police "khidmat" centres; RTI requests.
- **Potential and limits:** can improve services and trust, but often tokenistic; elite capture; low awareness; officials see it as interference; digital tools exclude the poor. Meaningful engagement needs: feedback that changes decisions, information disclosure, legal backing, capacity, and follow-up.

## 4. Accountability: concept and approaches

- **Meaning:** an obligation to explain and justify conduct to a forum that can pose questions, pass judgement and impose consequences (Mark Bovens, 2007). Three elements: information, debate, consequences.
- **Answerability + enforceability** (Schedler, 1999).
- **Friedrich–Finer debate (1940–41):** Friedrich — internal, professional responsibility and conscience; Finer — external control by elected representatives ("subjective" vs "objective" responsibility). Both are needed.
- **Romzek and Dubnick (1987), four types (after the Challenger disaster):**

| Type | Source of control | High/low autonomy | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hierarchical/bureaucratic | Internal, close supervision | Low | Superior–subordinate |
| Legal | External, close | Low | Courts, auditors |
| Professional | Internal, deference to expertise | High | Doctors, engineers |
| Political | External, responsiveness | High | Legislators, public |

- **Other classifications:** vertical (citizens → state: elections, media), horizontal (state institutions checking each other: courts, auditors, ombudsmen — Guillermo O'Donnell, 1998), diagonal/social (civil society working with state bodies); upward/downward; ex ante vs ex post.
- **NPM accountability:** for results, through contracts and performance indicators. **NPS:** multiple — law, community values, political norms, professional standards, citizen interests.
- **"Problem of many hands"** (Dennis Thompson, 1980): in networks and large organisations it is hard to find who is responsible.

## 5. Institutional framework for administrative accountability (Pakistan)

| Institution | Basis | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Parliament: questions, standing committees, PAC | Constitution; Rules of Procedure | Political and financial scrutiny |
| Auditor-General | Arts. 168–171; Audit Ordinance 2001 | Financial, compliance and performance audit |
| Courts: High Courts (Art. 199), Supreme Court, Federal Constitutional Court (since Nov 2025) | Constitution | Judicial review; fundamental rights |
| Service tribunals | Art. 212 | Employee disputes |
| NAB | National Accountability Ordinance 1999 (amended 2022–23) | Corruption of public office holders above thresholds |
| FIA | FIA Act 1974 | Federal crimes, corruption of federal employees |
| Provincial Anti-Corruption Establishments | Provincial laws | Provincial employees |
| Ombudsmen | President's Order 1 of 1983 (Wafaqi Mohtasib); FTO 2000; others | Maladministration complaints |
| Pakistan Information Commission | Right of Access to Information Act 2017 (Art. 19A) | RTI appeals |
| Election Commission | Art. 218 | Elections, asset declarations of legislators |
| Establishment Division / E&D Rules 2020 | Civil Servants Act | Internal discipline |
| Conduct Rules 2026 | Establishment Division | Asset declarations, conflict of interest |
| Media, civil society | Arts. 19, 17 | Social accountability |

**NAB notes:** set up by Musharraf (1999); criticised for selective, political accountability and long pre-trial detention; 2022 amendments raised the threshold (cases under Rs500m excluded), limited powers and remand period; the Supreme Court struck down parts in September 2023, then in **September 2024** a larger bench **restored the amendments** on the federal government's intra-court appeal **[verify]**.

## 6. Legislative, executive and judicial control over administration

**Legislative control:**
- Law-making defines powers; budget approval and supplementary grants; question hour; calling-attention notices; adjournment motions; standing committees (can summon officials); PAC; laying of rules; motions of no confidence against ministers (indirect control over departments).
- **Limits:** weak committees, low attendance, lack of research staff, party discipline, executive dominance, frequent ordinances.

**Executive control:**
- Political executive (PM, ministers): policy direction, appointments, transfers, Rules of Business.
- Budgetary control (Finance Division).
- Establishment Division: personnel.
- Inspections, inquiries, performance agreements, Prime Minister's Delivery Unit **[verify current name]**.
- **Limits:** used for patronage; frequent transfers.

**Judicial control:**
- Writs (Art. 199); judicial review; natural justice; Section 24A General Clauses Act; suits against government; contempt.
- **Limits:** delay, cost, judicial overreach, stay orders that stall administration.

## 7. Separation of powers and accountability (asked 2017, 2026)

- **Montesquieu, *The Spirit of the Laws* (1748):** liberty requires separating legislative, executive and judicial powers. US Constitution (1787) built **checks and balances** (Madison, *Federalist* No. 47 and 51).
- **Watergate (1972–74):** break-in at the Democratic National Committee's Watergate offices (June 1972); cover-up by the Nixon White House; Senate Watergate Committee hearings; special prosecutor; *United States v. Nixon* (July 1974) — Supreme Court unanimously ordered release of the tapes, rejecting absolute executive privilege; House Judiciary Committee approved articles of impeachment; Nixon resigned on 9 August 1974. Lessons: an independent judiciary, a free press (Woodward and Bernstein, *Washington Post*), legislative investigation and independent prosecutors together held the head of the bureaucracy to account. Followed by the Ethics in Government Act 1978 (independent counsel, financial disclosure) and the Inspector General Act 1978.
- **Pakistan (2026 framing):** a parliamentary system fuses executive and legislature, so separation is mainly between judiciary and the other two.
  - *Conflicts that strengthened accountability:* judicial review of appointments and transfers; Panama Papers case (2017) disqualification of a sitting PM **[balanced: also criticised]**; suo motu actions on missing persons and environment; Lawyers' Movement (2007–09) restoring judges.
  - *Conflicts that weakened administration:* dismissals of PMs by courts (Gilani 2012 for contempt; Nawaz Sharif 2017) unsettled governments; court-run projects (dam fund 2018) and price-fixing; officials paralysed by contempt and NAB; executive–judiciary confrontation (2022–24) over elections and benches; the 26th Amendment (2024) gave Parliament a role in choosing the Chief Justice through a parliamentary committee; the 27th Amendment (2025) created the Federal Constitutional Court. Supporters say this restores parliamentary supremacy and ends judicial overreach; critics say it weakens judicial independence.
  - *Constitutional evidence:* Arts. 175(3) (separation of judiciary from executive — to be completed by 1996, per the *Al-Jehad Trust*/*Sharaf Faridi* cases **[verify]**), 175A (judicial commission), 199, 184(3), 204 (contempt), 209 (Supreme Judicial Council), 89 (ordinances), 232–234 (emergency).
  - *Empirical evidence:* CPI 2025 score 28/100 (rank 136/182); case backlog; the 2026 KP governor's rule petitions removed from the Peshawar High Court's list after a Federal Constitutional Court order (Pakistan Affairs pack).
  - *Balanced judgement:* institutional conflict has produced episodic accountability but not systemic accountability; durable accountability needs institutions that check each other through rules rather than confrontation.

## 8. Administrative corruption (asked 2018, 2021, 2024)

**Definition:** abuse of entrusted power for private gain (Transparency International); betrayal of public trust for private interest (2024 question wording).

**Types:**
- Petty (bribes for services) vs grand (high-level policy capture, mega-contracts).
- Bribery, embezzlement, fraud, extortion, nepotism and favouritism, kickbacks in procurement, speed money, state capture, influence peddling, conflict of interest.
- Collusive vs coercive.
- Legal corruption (lobbying that buys policy) vs illegal.

**Causes:**
- **Klitgaard (1988):** Corruption = Monopoly + Discretion − Accountability.
- Low pay and poor working conditions.
- Weak institutions and impunity; slow courts.
- Complex procedures and cash-based systems.
- Political financing and patronage.
- Social norms (gift culture, kinship obligations — biradari).
- Lack of transparency; colonial-era secrecy.
- Large informal economy.
- Principal–agent problems.

**Effects:** lower investment and growth (Mauro, 1995); poor services for the poor; distorted public spending (bias to construction); revenue loss; loss of trust and legitimacy; undermines democracy — votes bought, policy captured, citizens disengage (2021 question); organisational demoralisation; brain drain of honest officers.

**How corruption undermines democracy (2021):** distorts elections (money, state resources); captures legislators and policy; weakens rule of law and equality; erodes public trust and participation; fuels populism and anti-democratic interventions (military coups in Pakistan have often cited corruption).

**Control strategies:**
1. **Prevention:** simplify procedures; digitise (e-procurement under PPRA — e-PADS; online land records; e-filing); reduce discretion; adequate pay; rotation in sensitive posts; codes of conduct and asset declarations (Conduct Rules 2026).
2. **Transparency:** RTI, open budgets, open contracting, publication of asset declarations.
3. **Detection:** audit, whistle-blowing protection, citizen complaints, media.
4. **Enforcement:** independent, non-partisan anti-corruption agencies (Hong Kong's ICAC, Singapore's CPIB — success cases); quick trials; asset recovery.
5. **Social accountability:** civil society, social audits, citizen report cards (Bangalore, 1993).
6. **International:** UN Convention Against Corruption (UNCAC, 2003; Pakistan ratified 2007), FATF (money laundering).
7. **Political will and leadership:** apolitical accountability; party financing rules.

**Pakistan facts:** CPI 2025 — score 28/100, rank 136 of 182 ([Profit, 10 Feb 2026](https://profit.pakistantoday.com.pk/2026/02/10/pakistan-improves-one-spot-to-136th-on-2025-corruption-perceptions-index)); CPI 2024 — 27, rank 135 of 180. Transparency International Pakistan's National Corruption Perception Survey separately ranks police, procurement and judiciary among the most corrupt sectors **[verify the latest NCPS ranking]**.

**Citizen-side measures (2024 Q4b):** refuse and report bribes; use online services; RTI requests; join social audits; support honest officials; civic education; media and social-media exposure (responsibly).

## 9. Social audit (asked 2021)

- **Meaning:** independent evaluation of a programme's performance by the community and stakeholders, comparing official records with reality and making results public.
- **Origin:** India — Mazdoor Kisan Shakti Sangathan's public hearings (jan sunwai) in Rajasthan in the 1990s; made mandatory under India's MGNREGA (2005) for every gram panchayat.
- **Steps:** collect official records (RTI) → verify on the ground → public hearing → findings and action → follow-up.
- **Benefits:** transparency, participation, detects leakages, builds trust.
- **Why often ineffective (the 2021 statement):** no legal power to punish; findings ignored by officials; capture by local elites; fear of retaliation; low capacity of communities; no institutional follow-up.
- **Remedies:** legal mandate with action-taken reports; link findings to audit and anti-corruption agencies; protect participants; social audit units independent of implementing departments; publish results.
- **Pakistan:** social audits piloted under devolution (2001–09) and by NGOs (e.g. CIET's social audit of governance, 2002–04 **[verify]**); BISP and health programmes use third-party monitoring.

## 10. Civil society, media and interest groups in good governance (2019 note)

- **Civil society:** organised life between family, market and state — NGOs, CBOs, professional associations, trade unions, religious bodies, think tanks, media.
- **Roles:** service delivery (Edhi Foundation, The Citizens Foundation, Akhuwat microfinance, Indus Hospital); advocacy (HRCP, Aurat Foundation); watchdog (TI Pakistan, FAFEN election observation, PILDAT); research (SDPI, PIDE); mobilisation (Lawyers' Movement 2007–09); disaster relief.
- **Media:** informs, investigates, sets agenda; constraints in Pakistan — PEMRA restrictions, PECA 2016 (amended 2025 to criminalise "fake news" **[verify]**), journalist safety, ownership concentration, polarisation.
- **Interest groups:** business chambers (FPCCI), trade associations (APTMA), farmers' groups, professional bodies (Pakistan Bar Council) — provide expertise but can capture policy (sugar, IPPs).
- **Constraints on civil society in Pakistan:** registration and foreign funding restrictions on INGOs (2015 policy **[verify]**); security; dependence on donors; urban bias.
- **For good governance:** an enabling legal environment, consultation in law-making, access to information, protection of journalists and activists.

## 11. The situation in Pakistan — summary

**Strengths:** many institutions on paper; active (sometimes over-active) courts; vocal media; ombudsmen used by citizens; digital complaint systems; RTI law.
**Weaknesses:** selective and politicised accountability (NAB history); weak parliamentary oversight; audit paras pile up; whistle-blowers unprotected; delays in courts; executive dominance; low trust; civil–military imbalance where some institutions sit outside civilian oversight.
**Way forward:** an independent, apolitical anti-corruption body with clear thresholds and fair trial; strengthen PAC and committees with research staff; enforce Conduct Rules 2026 asset declarations with public disclosure; complete digitisation of land records, procurement and taxes; protect whistle-blowers; empower local governments for citizen accountability; free media within responsible law; follow up audit findings within fixed deadlines.
