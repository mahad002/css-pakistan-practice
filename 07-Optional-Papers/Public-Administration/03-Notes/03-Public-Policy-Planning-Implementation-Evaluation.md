# III. Public Policy Planning, Implementation and Evaluation

Asked 2016, 2017 (note), 2018, 2023 (two questions), 2025. Favourite angle: **why implementation fails in Pakistan** and **how policies are evaluated**.

---

## 1. Key concepts

- **Public policy:** "whatever governments choose to do or not to do" (Thomas Dye, 1972). Also: a purposive course of action by government to deal with a problem (James Anderson).
- **Policy sciences:** Harold Lasswell (1951) — problem-oriented, multidisciplinary, value-explicit.
- **Types (Lowi, 1964/1972):** distributive (subsidies, development funds), regulatory (licensing, price control), redistributive (BISP, progressive tax), constituent (setting up institutions).

## 2. Policy cycle (stages model)

1. **Problem identification / agenda setting** — which issues get attention. Kingdon's **multiple streams** (1984): problem, policy and politics streams meet in a "policy window"; policy entrepreneurs couple them. Cobb and Elder: systemic vs institutional agendas.
2. **Formulation** — options developed by ministries, think tanks, donors.
3. **Decision/adoption** — cabinet, parliament, ECC, CCI.
4. **Implementation** — departments, field offices, street-level bureaucrats.
5. **Evaluation** — did it work?
6. **Termination or change** — feedback.

**Critique of the stages model:** neat but unreal; stages overlap; policy is made in implementation (Lipsky). Alternatives: Sabatier's advocacy coalition framework (1988); punctuated equilibrium (Baumgartner and Jones, 1993); institutional rational choice (Ostrom).

## 3. Models of policy-making / decision-making

| Model | Author | Idea | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rational-comprehensive | Classical | Define goals, list all options, assess all consequences, choose the best | Information and time limits; values conflict |
| Bounded rationality / satisficing | Simon, 1947 | Decision-makers search until a "good enough" option appears | Can settle too early |
| Incrementalism | Lindblom, "The Science of Muddling Through", 1959 | Small changes from the status quo; "successive limited comparisons" | Conservative; ignores big shifts |
| Mixed scanning | Etzioni, 1967 | Broad scan of options, detailed look at a few | Hard to operationalise |
| Optimal model | Dror, 1968 | Rational plus extra-rational (intuition, judgement) | Vague |
| Garbage can | Cohen, March and Olsen, 1972 | "Organised anarchies": problems, solutions, participants, choice opportunities mix randomly | Descriptive, not prescriptive |
| Elite theory | C. Wright Mills | Elites make policy | Overstates unity |
| Group theory / pluralism | Truman, Dahl | Policy as balance of groups | Unequal group power |
| Institutionalism | March and Olsen | Rules and institutions shape choices | |
| Public choice | Buchanan | Self-interested actors | Narrow |

**Pakistan:** budgets and PSDP are incremental; major policies often elite-driven (SIFC, IMF conditionality) or crisis-driven (energy, floods).

## 4. Strategic planning and management

See file 02 §8 (Bryson's cycle, Moore's public value). For policy questions add:
- Strategic planning sets direction (3–10 years); operational planning sets annual targets; budgets fund them.
- Link plan → budget → performance indicators → evaluation (the "results chain").

## 5. Planning process

**Types of planning (asked 2023):**
| Type | Objective |
|---|---|
| Strategic (long-range) | Vision, mission, long-term goals |
| Tactical (medium-range) | Translate strategy into departmental programmes |
| Operational (short-range) | Day-to-day schedules, standing plans, single-use plans |
| Contingency | Prepare for crises (floods, epidemics) |
| Indicative vs imperative | Indicative guides private sector (Pakistan, France); imperative commands (Soviet) |
| Centralised vs decentralised | Planning Commission vs district plans |
| Sectoral vs spatial | Health plan vs regional development |
| Physical vs financial | Projects vs resource allocation |

**Planning steps:** situation analysis → objectives → alternatives → evaluation of alternatives → selection → programmes and budgets → implementation → monitoring and feedback.

## 6. Planning machinery in Pakistan

- **Planning Commission** (Planning Board 1953; Planning Commission 1958) under the Ministry of Planning, Development and Special Initiatives; Deputy Chairman; prepares five-year plans and the annual plan; PSDP.
- **National Economic Council (Art. 156):** chaired by PM; includes CMs; approves national plans and the annual development outlay.
- **ECNEC** (Executive Committee of NEC): chaired by the Finance Minister **[verify current chair]**; approves large projects.
- **CDWP** (Central Development Working Party): chaired by the Deputy Chairman Planning Commission; approves mid-size projects and recommends large ones to ECNEC.
- **DDWP** (Departmental Development Working Party): ministry-level approval of small projects. Exact rupee limits change; check the latest Planning Commission notification **[verify thresholds]**.
- **Provinces:** P&D Boards/Departments, PDWP, provincial ADPs.
- **Project cycle forms:** PC-I (project document), PC-II (feasibility study), PC-III (progress monitoring), PC-IV (completion), PC-V (annual operation after completion).
- **Plans:** First Five-Year Plan 1955–60; Second (1960–65) is often called the most successful (Harvard Advisory Group); Vision 2025 (2014); 12th Plan (2018–23); "Uraan Pakistan" (2024–29) **[verify]**.
- **Problems:** plans not linked to budgets; PSDP cuts mid-year; political "schemes" for MNAs; weak appraisal; project delays and cost overruns; data gaps.

## 7. Policy analysis (asked 2016, 2025)

**Meaning:** systematic assessment of options *before* (ex ante) or *after* (ex post) a decision to advise decision-makers. Dunn: "an applied discipline that uses multiple methods of inquiry to produce and transform policy-relevant information".

**Dunn's five procedures:** problem structuring, forecasting, recommendation (prescription), monitoring, evaluation.

**Steps of policy analysis (Patton and Sawicki; Bardach's "Eightfold Path"):**
1. Define the problem.
2. Assemble evidence.
3. Construct alternatives.
4. Select criteria (effectiveness, efficiency, equity, feasibility, legality).
5. Project outcomes.
6. Confront trade-offs.
7. Decide.
8. Tell the story (communicate).

**Tools:** cost–benefit analysis (NPV, benefit–cost ratio), cost-effectiveness analysis, regulatory impact assessment, multi-criteria analysis, decision trees, forecasting, stakeholder analysis, SWOT.

**Policy analysis vs policy evaluation (2016 framing):** analysis asks how far a policy achieves its objectives and which option is best; evaluation asks whether implementation achieved results and maximised appropriate values (efficiency, equity, legitimacy).

## 8. Policy implementation (asked 2016, 2018, 2025)

**Definition:** carrying out a basic policy decision (Mazmanian and Sabatier, 1983).

**Landmark:** Pressman and Wildavsky, *Implementation* (1973), on a federal jobs programme in Oakland — many "decision points" and "clearances" multiply the chance of failure.

**Top-down approach:** Van Meter and Van Horn (1975); Mazmanian and Sabatier. Clear objectives, causal theory, adequate resources, committed officials, supportive groups and stable conditions lead to success.
**Hogwood and Gunn (1984) — conditions for "perfect implementation":** no crippling external constraints; adequate time and resources; the required combination of resources available; valid cause–effect theory; direct links (few intervening links); minimal dependency relationships; agreed objectives; tasks specified in sequence; perfect communication and coordination; perfect compliance.
**Bottom-up approach:** Michael Lipsky, *Street-Level Bureaucracy* (1980): teachers, police, health workers effectively make policy through discretion under heavy caseloads. Hjern and Porter: implementation structures.
**Synthesis:** Elmore's "backward mapping" (1979); Matland's ambiguity–conflict model (1995): low ambiguity/low conflict = administrative implementation; low ambiguity/high conflict = political; high ambiguity/low conflict = experimental; high/high = symbolic.
**When top-down works best (a 2026 MCQ):** when goals are clear and uncontroversial.

**Techniques at the start of implementation (asked 2023):** detailed work plan and timeline (Gantt, PERT/CPM); clear allocation of responsibility (RACI); pilot projects; resource mobilisation and staffing; training; communication strategy; stakeholder engagement; baseline data and M&E framework; legal/administrative rules; risk register; quick wins.

**Factors fatal to implementation in Pakistan (asked 2018; part of 2025):**
1. Vague or conflicting objectives; policies written to please donors.
2. Political instability and changes of government; reversal of predecessor's projects.
3. Weak capacity: too few trained staff, generalists in technical posts.
4. Poor coordination between federal and provincial agencies after the 18th Amendment.
5. Insufficient and unpredictable funds (PSDP cuts).
6. Corruption and rent-seeking; elite capture.
7. Frequent transfers of key officials.
8. Lack of data and monitoring.
9. Resistance by vested interests (e.g. tax reforms, energy subsidies, land records).
10. Weak local government — no tier close to citizens.
11. Legal delays (stay orders).
12. Over-centralisation and file culture.

**Examples:** National Education Policy 2009 and the gap in Article 25A; National Action Plan 2014 (good on paper, uneven delivery); Single National Curriculum (2020) contested by provinces; circular debt despite repeated power-sector plans; success stories — polio campaign improvements, BISP (data-driven targeting through NSER), NADRA, Punjab land records digitisation (PLRA), Rescue 1122.

**Improving implementation:** clear targets and owners; delivery units (UK Prime Minister's Delivery Unit 2001 under Michael Barber — copied in Punjab's education roadmap 2011–18); stable tenure; digital tracking; third-party validation; citizen feedback; adequate budgets; capacity building; empowered local governments.

## 9. Program evaluation (asked 2023, 2025)

**Definition:** systematic assessment of the design, implementation and results of a programme to improve it or judge its worth.

**Ways/types of evaluation:**
| Type | Question | When |
|---|---|---|
| Needs assessment | Is there a problem and need? | Before |
| Evaluability assessment | Can it be evaluated? | Before |
| Formative | How can we improve it? | During |
| Process / implementation | Was it delivered as planned? | During |
| Summative | Did it work overall? | End |
| Outcome / impact | What changed because of it? (attribution) | After |
| Cost–benefit / cost-effectiveness | Was it worth the money? | After |
| Meta-evaluation | Synthesis of evaluations | Any |

**Approaches and how they differ:**
- **Objectives-based (Tyler):** measure against stated goals.
- **Goal-free (Scriven):** look at all effects, intended or not.
- **Theory-based / logic model:** inputs → activities → outputs → outcomes → impact; test each link.
- **Experimental (RCTs) and quasi-experimental** (difference-in-differences, regression discontinuity): strongest causal claims; costly; ethical limits. (Banerjee, Duflo, Kremer — Nobel 2019.)
- **Participatory / empowerment evaluation:** stakeholders involved; builds ownership; less objective.
- **Utilisation-focused (Patton):** designed around intended users.
- **Realist evaluation (Pawson and Tilley, 1997):** what works for whom, in what circumstances (context–mechanism–outcome).
- **OECD-DAC criteria:** relevance, coherence (added 2019), effectiveness, efficiency, impact, sustainability.

**Monitoring vs evaluation:** monitoring is continuous tracking of inputs and outputs; evaluation is periodic, deeper judgement of outcomes and causes.

**Pakistan:** PC-III and PC-IV forms (largely compliance, not learning); Projects Wing of Planning Commission; Punjab Monitoring and Implementation Unit; donors' evaluations (World Bank IEG, ADB); BISP evaluations by Oxford Policy Management showing effects on consumption and women's empowerment **[verify]**; weak culture of independent evaluation; few ex-post evaluations published.

**Problems:** vague objectives; no baseline; political sensitivity; weak data; evaluation seen as fault-finding; no follow-up.

## 10. Role of donors and international institutions

**Actors:** IMF (stabilisation programmes; Pakistan's 24th programme, the $7bn Extended Fund Facility approved September 2024 **[verify]**), World Bank (budget support, Country Partnership Framework), ADB, UNDP, DFID/FCDO, USAID (largely wound down in 2025 **[verify]**), JICA, China (CPEC, outside the classic donor model), Gulf states (deposits, investment via SIFC).

**Roles:** finance; conditionality (tax reform, energy pricing, SOE reform, central bank autonomy — SBP Act amendment 2022); technical assistance; policy ideas (NPM, decentralisation, good governance); capacity building; evaluation.

**Benefits:** money in crises; global expertise; discipline for reform; data and evaluation culture.

**Criticisms:** one-size-fits-all (Washington Consensus); weak ownership; project proliferation and parallel units that bypass government; austerity hurts the poor; reforms reverse after the programme ends; aid volatility; geopolitical motives.

**Way forward:** country-owned strategy; align aid with national plans and budgets (Paris Declaration 2005 principles: ownership, alignment, harmonisation, results, mutual accountability); use country systems; build capacity rather than consultants.

## 11. Policy-making and implementation in Pakistan

**Formal process:** ministry drafts → inter-ministerial consultation → Law and Justice Division vetting → Cabinet (or ECC for economic matters) → Parliament if legislation needed → CCI if a Part II federal subject or an interprovincial matter → notification → implementation by departments and provinces.

**Actors:** PM Office, cabinet committees, ECC, NEC/ECNEC, CCI, SIFC (since 2023, civil–military investment body), provincial cabinets, bureaucracy, military, courts (suo motu history), IMF and donors, business lobbies, media, think tanks (PIDE, SDPI, PIDE's reports on governance).

**Features/problems:** top-down and elite-driven; weak evidence base; ad hocism; policy reversals; weak parliamentary committees; provincial capacity gaps after 2010; poor consultation; heavy role of external actors.

**Recent examples to cite:** Digital Nation Pakistan Act 2025; contributory pension scheme (new employees from July 2024); rightsizing of the federal government (2024–25); Punjab Local Government Act 2025; civil service reform package (presented September 2026).

**Way forward:** policy units in each ministry with economists and data scientists; regulatory impact assessment for new laws; a parliamentary budget office; open consultation; ex-post evaluation of major policies; stable fiscal space; empowered local governments for delivery.
