# VI. Administrative Law

Asked 2016 (judicial review, Marbury v. Madison), 2017 (judicial activism note), 2019 (grievance redress), 2024 (discretionary powers), 2025 (delegation of authority and legislation). The courts were restructured by the 26th (October 2024) and 27th (November 2025) Amendments — update this when you revise.

---

## 1. Meaning

- **Ivor Jennings:** the law relating to the administration — it determines the organisation, powers and duties of administrative authorities.
- **K.C. Davis:** the law concerning the powers and procedures of administrative agencies, especially judicial review of administrative action.
- **H.W.R. Wade:** the law relating to the control of governmental power; its primary purpose is to keep the powers of government within legal bounds and protect citizens against abuse.
- **Working definition:** the body of law that creates administrative bodies, gives them powers, sets procedures, and provides remedies for citizens against unlawful administrative action.

## 2. Scope and significance

**Scope:**
1. Organisation and powers of administrative authorities.
2. Delegated (subordinate) legislation.
3. Administrative adjudication — tribunals.
4. Administrative discretion and its control.
5. Principles of natural justice.
6. Judicial review and remedies (writs).
7. Liability of the state (contract and tort).
8. Ombudsman and non-judicial remedies.
9. Public corporations and regulators.
10. Right to information.

**Why it grew (significance):** the welfare state and regulatory state multiplied government functions; legislatures cannot write every detail; specialised, quick and cheap decisions needed; citizens need protection from a powerful executive.

## 3. Nature and contents

- **Public law**, mainly judge-made in common-law countries, statutory in others.
- **Dicey's rule of law (1885):** supremacy of ordinary law over arbitrary power; equality before law (officials subject to ordinary courts); constitution as the result of ordinary law. Dicey was hostile to the French *droit administratif* (separate administrative courts, Conseil d'État), seeing it as privilege for officials. Later writers showed French administrative courts protect citizens well.
- **Lord Hewart, *The New Despotism* (1929):** warned of executive power through delegated legislation and tribunals → **Donoughmore Committee on Ministers' Powers (1932)** accepted delegation with safeguards.
- **Franks Committee (1957):** tribunals should follow **openness, fairness and impartiality** → Tribunals and Inquiries Act 1958.

**Principles of natural justice:**
1. *Nemo judex in causa sua* — no one should be judge in their own cause (rule against bias: pecuniary, personal, official).
2. *Audi alteram partem* — hear the other side: notice, chance to respond, disclosure of evidence.
3. **Reasoned decisions** — in Pakistan, **Section 24A of the General Clauses Act 1897** (inserted 1997) requires authorities to exercise powers reasonably, fairly and justly, and to give reasons for orders.

**Grounds of judicial review (Lord Diplock, *CCSU v Minister for the Civil Service* — the GCHQ case, 1984):**
1. **Illegality** — acting beyond powers (ultra vires), error of law, improper purpose, irrelevant considerations.
2. **Irrationality** — *Wednesbury* unreasonableness (*Associated Provincial Picture Houses v Wednesbury Corporation*, 1948): a decision so unreasonable no reasonable authority could reach it.
3. **Procedural impropriety** — breach of natural justice or statutory procedure.
4. **Proportionality** (suggested by Diplock; now used in human-rights cases).
Also: legitimate expectation; abuse of discretion; fettering discretion.

## 4. Administrative discretion (asked 2024)

**Meaning:** freedom given to an official to choose among courses of action within the law (K.C. Davis: "a public officer has discretion whenever the effective limits on his power leave him free to make a choice").

**Merits:** flexibility for varied cases; use of expertise; speed; individual justice; handles situations law cannot foresee; allows innovation.
**Demerits:** arbitrariness and inconsistency; corruption and favouritism; political pressure; unequal treatment; difficult to review.

**Controls:**
- **Legislative:** clear standards in statutes; laying of rules.
- **Judicial:** review on the grounds above; Art. 199 writs; Section 24A GCA.
- **Administrative:** guidelines, SOPs, hierarchical review, recorded reasons.
- **Transparency:** RTI, digitisation (removes face-to-face discretion), audit, ombudsman.
- **Davis' prescription (*Discretionary Justice*, 1969):** confine, structure and check discretion.

**Pakistan:** wide discretion in land, licences, tax assessments, police FIR registration, postings and transfers. Courts have restrained discretionary allotment of plots and discretionary funds **[cite cases only if verified]**. A balanced 2024 answer: support *structured* discretion — rules plus reasons plus review — not unlimited discretion or none.

## 5. Delegation of authority and legislation (asked 2025)

### (a) Delegated legislation
- **Meaning:** law made by an authority other than the legislature under powers given by an Act ("subordinate legislation"): rules, regulations, by-laws, orders, notifications, SROs.
- **Why:** pressure on legislative time; technical subjects; flexibility; emergencies; experimentation.
- **Dangers:** executive law-making without debate; excessive delegation (abdication of legislative function); poor publicity.
- **Types:** rules, regulations, by-laws (local bodies), statutory orders, "Henry VIII clauses" (power to amend the parent Act — criticised).
- **Controls:**
  1. *Parliamentary* — laying requirements; scrutiny committees (UK Joint Committee on Statutory Instruments). Pakistan has a Senate Committee on Delegated Legislation **[verify]**.
  2. *Judicial* — substantive ultra vires (beyond the Act or unconstitutional) and procedural ultra vires (consultation, publication not done).
  3. *Procedural* — publication in the Gazette, prior consultation, pre-publication for comments.
- **Pakistan:** most taxes and duties are adjusted through SROs (FBR); courts have held that taxes cannot be imposed except by Act of Parliament (Art. 77) **[verify case]**; ordinances under Art. 89 are executive legislation, not delegated legislation — they lapse after 120 days unless extended once by resolution.

### (b) Delegation of authority (management sense)
- **Meaning:** a superior assigns part of his authority and tasks to a subordinate while keeping ultimate accountability. Responsibility can be shared; accountability cannot be delegated.
- **Principles of effective delegation:**
  1. Delegation by results expected (clear goals).
  2. Parity of authority and responsibility.
  3. Absolute responsibility of the superior.
  4. Unity of command.
  5. Scalar principle (clear line of authority).
  6. Authority-level principle (decide at the level where authority lies).
  7. Clarity in writing; appropriate controls; matching person to task; trust.
- **Steps in the process:** (1) determine results/tasks; (2) assign duties; (3) grant authority; (4) create accountability/obligation; (5) establish controls and feedback; (6) support and review.
- **Barriers:** fear of losing control; distrust of subordinates; lack of capacity; fear of audit/NAB; subordinates' fear of criticism.
- **Pakistan:** Rules of Business and "Delegation of Financial Powers Rules" allocate powers; over-centralisation means files rise to secretaries and ministers for small decisions. The 2026 package proposes cutting decision tiers from five to three.

## 6. Administrative tribunals

- **Meaning:** bodies outside the ordinary court hierarchy that decide disputes between citizens and administration (or between employees and government) in specialised areas.
- **Reasons:** expertise, speed, low cost, informality, relieve courts.
- **Criticism:** lack of independence (members appointed by government), variable quality, limited appeal, procedural shortcuts.
- **Pakistan:**
  - **Article 212:** Parliament/Assemblies may create administrative courts and tribunals for service matters, compensation claims, and matters relating to public property; their jurisdiction excludes ordinary courts; appeal lies to the Supreme Court only if it raises a substantial question of law of public importance.
  - **Federal Service Tribunal** (Service Tribunals Act 1973) and provincial service tribunals.
  - Others: customs, income tax and sales tax Appellate Tribunals (Appellate Tribunal Inland Revenue), Competition Appellate Tribunal, environmental tribunals, Banking Courts, labour courts/NIRC.
  - **Ombudsmen** (quasi-judicial, not tribunals strictly): Wafaqi Mohtasib (President's Order 1 of 1983), Federal Tax Ombudsman (2000), Banking Mohtasib, Insurance Ombudsman, Federal Ombudsman for Protection against Harassment (2010 Act). **Federal Ombudsmen Institutional Reforms Act 2013** — time limits (60 days), representation to the President. Provincial ombudsmen in all provinces.

## 7. Dispute settlement and grievance redress (asked 2019)

**Methods:**
1. Internal departmental appeals and representations.
2. Ombudsman complaints (free, no lawyer needed).
3. Service tribunals for employees.
4. Courts — writs under Art. 199 (High Courts).
5. Alternative dispute resolution (ADR): mediation, arbitration; Alternative Dispute Resolution Act 2017 (federal) **[verify]**; *jirga* and *panchayat* (informal, sometimes against rights).
6. Digital channels: **Pakistan Citizen's Portal** (launched October 2018) — complaints routed to departments; Punjab's Citizen Feedback Monitoring Program.
7. Parliamentary questions and committees.
8. Media and civil society.

**Adequacy in Pakistan:** strengths — many channels; ombudsman decisions fast and cheap; Citizen's Portal widely used. Weaknesses — implementation of ombudsman orders weak; case backlogs in courts (over 2 million cases across courts **[verify]**); tribunals' independence questioned; low awareness among the poor; digital divide; complaints closed without real resolution.

## 8. Judicial review and Marbury v. Madison (asked 2016)

- **Case (1803):** William Marbury was appointed a justice of the peace in the last hours of John Adams' presidency; the new Secretary of State James Madison withheld his commission. Marbury sought a writ of mandamus from the Supreme Court under the Judiciary Act of 1789.
- **Chief Justice John Marshall held:** Marbury had a right to the commission; but the section of the Judiciary Act giving the Court original jurisdiction to issue mandamus was **unconstitutional**, because it enlarged the Court's original jurisdiction beyond Article III. So the Court could not issue the writ.
- **Significance:** established that courts can declare acts of the legislature void if they conflict with the Constitution — judicial review. "It is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is" (Marshall; safe to paraphrase).
- **Development in administrative law:** judicial review extended from statutes to executive and administrative action; US Administrative Procedure Act 1946.
- **Pakistan:**
  - **Article 199** — High Courts issue orders in the nature of habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, certiorari and quo warranto.
  - **Article 184(3)** — original jurisdiction of the Supreme Court in matters of public importance involving fundamental rights; used heavily for "suo motu" actions (2005–2023). The **26th Amendment (2024)** created constitutional benches; the **27th Amendment (2025)** created a **Federal Constitutional Court** for constitutional interpretation and fundamental-rights matters; the Supreme Court remains the final court of appeal in civil and criminal cases (from the Pakistan Affairs pack) **[verify article numbers after the amendments]**.
  - Landmark examples: *Maulvi Tamizuddin Khan* (1955, Federal Court overturned the Sindh Chief Court on a technical point and so let the Assembly's dissolution stand; necessity was then invoked in the Governor-General's Reference of 1955); *Dosso* (1958, Kelsen's "successful revolution" theory used to validate martial law); *Asma Jilani* (1972, rejected that theory and called Yahya Khan a usurper); *Al-Jehad Trust* (1996, judges' appointments); *Shehla Zia* (1994, right to life includes clean environment); *Anita Turab* (2013, tenure and postings of civil servants **[verify]**).

## 9. Judicial activism (2017 note)

- **Meaning:** courts going beyond narrow interpretation to shape policy and protect rights — public interest litigation, suo motu, relaxed standing.
- **For:** protects the weak; fills governance gaps; checks executive abuse (e.g. missing persons, environment, human rights).
- **Against:** judicial overreach; breaches separation of powers; courts lack expertise (dam fund 2018, price controls); delays in executive decisions; officials fear contempt; politicisation of courts.
- **Pakistan arc:** activism peaked 2007–2019 (Iftikhar Chaudhry, Saqib Nisar); restrained by the Supreme Court (Practice and Procedure) Act 2023 (committee to decide suo motu and benches); then the 26th and 27th Amendments restructured constitutional jurisdiction.
- **Balance:** activism as a remedy for governance failure, but the cure is stronger executive and legislature, not courts as administrators.

## 10. Administrative ethics

- **Meaning:** principles of right conduct in public office — integrity, impartiality, accountability, respect for law and citizens.
- **Approaches:** deontological (duty, rules — Kant); consequentialist (outcomes — utilitarianism); virtue ethics (character — Aristotle); Islamic ethics (*amanah*, *adl*, *ihsan*).
- **Friedrich–Finer debate (1940–41):** Carl Friedrich: inner check — professional standards and conscience; Herman Finer: external controls by elected representatives. Both needed.
- **Nolan Principles (UK, 1995) — seven principles of public life:** selflessness, integrity, objectivity, accountability, openness, honesty, leadership.
- **Dilemmas:** loyalty to minister vs public interest; whistle-blowing; conflicts of interest; gifts; confidentiality vs transparency; "dirty hands".
- **Instruments:** codes of conduct; asset declarations; conflict-of-interest rules; ethics training; whistle-blower protection (Pakistan: Public Interest Disclosures Act 2017 **[verify]**); independent ethics bodies.
- **Pakistan:** **Civil Servants Conduct Rules 2026** replaced the 1964 rules (assets declared annually online for BS-17 and above, FBR audit of declarations; recusal for conflicts of interest; gifts barred; foreign honours need approval; no blogs or vlogs without permission; no political activity; no outside employment without approval; no memoirs disclosing confidential information) ([LCCI summary](https://lcci.pk/pakistans-civil-servants-conduct-rules-2026-assets-social-media-politics-and-outside-employment-full-breakdown/); [Daily Ausaf](https://dailyausaf.com/en/pakistan/federal-govt-unveils-civil-servants-conduct-rules-2026/)). Also the E&D Rules 2020; NAB law; Toshakhana rules.
