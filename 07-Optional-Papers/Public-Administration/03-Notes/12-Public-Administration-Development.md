# XII. Public Administration and Development

Asked 2017 (Riggs' prismatic-sala model), 2019 and 2026 (development administration vs administrative development — same question), 2023 (globalisation), 2026 (globalisation note). The 2019/2026 question is the most likely repeat in the whole paper.

---

## 1. Role of public administration in development

- **Development:** sustained improvement in the well-being of people — economic growth plus human development (health, education, freedom). **Amartya Sen, *Development as Freedom* (1999):** development is expanding people's capabilities.
- **Mahbub ul Haq:** creator of the **Human Development Index** (UNDP, first report 1990); *Reflections on Human Development* (1995); *Human Development in South Asia* reports (from 1997) — on the syllabus reading list.
- **Roles of PA:**
  1. Planning and policy for growth (Planning Commission, five-year plans).
  2. Mobilising resources (taxation, aid, investment facilitation).
  3. Building infrastructure (roads, dams, energy).
  4. Delivering social services (education, health, social protection — BISP).
  5. Regulating markets and protecting property rights.
  6. Maintaining law and order (precondition for investment).
  7. Nation-building and integration.
  8. Managing crises and climate adaptation.
  9. Promoting equity — reaching poor and remote regions.
  10. Partnering with private sector and civil society.
- **The developmental state** (Chalmers Johnson, *MITI and the Japanese Miracle*, 1982; Peter Evans, *Embedded Autonomy*, 1995): a meritocratic, insulated yet connected bureaucracy steered rapid industrialisation in Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, Singapore. Lesson for Pakistan: a capable, merit-based bureaucracy with "embedded autonomy" — close to business but not captured.
- **Pakistan's own history:** the 1960s "decade of development" (Second Five-Year Plan; Harvard Advisory Group; Mahbub ul Haq's 1968 "22 families" speech exposed inequality); Planning Commission once seen as a model; decline after the 1970s.

## 2. Concept of development administration

- **Origin of term:** U.L. Goswami (India, 1955) is credited with first use; developed by the **Comparative Administration Group (CAG)** of the American Society for Public Administration (1960; Ford Foundation funded, 1962–71) — Fred Riggs (chair), Edward Weidner, Ferrel Heady, Joseph LaPalombara, Ralph Braibanti (who wrote on Pakistan).
- **Weidner (1962):** the process of guiding an organisation toward the achievement of progressive political, economic and social objectives authoritatively determined.
- **Riggs:** administration of development programmes and the strengthening of administrative capabilities.
- **Features:** goal-orientation (development goals); change-orientation; innovation; client/citizen orientation; participation; flexibility and decentralisation; commitment of officials; temporal urgency; ecological awareness (administration shaped by its society).
- **Contrast with traditional (law-and-order) administration:**

| Traditional administration | Development administration |
|---|---|
| Maintain order, collect revenue | Bring socio-economic change |
| Status quo | Change |
| Rule-bound, hierarchical | Flexible, results-oriented |
| Centralised | Decentralised, participatory |
| Neutral | Committed to development goals |
| Generalist | Specialist + generalist |

## 3. Development administration vs administrative development (2019, 2026)

- **Development administration** = administration *of* development: using the administrative system to achieve development goals (programmes, projects, policies).
- **Administrative development** = development *of* administration: building the capacity of the administrative system itself — institutions, skills, procedures, structures, values (merit, rationality, accountability).
- **Relationship:** they are interdependent — the "chicken and egg" problem (Riggs). Development needs capable administration; administrative capacity grows through development tasks. Riggs warned that if bureaucracy develops faster than political institutions, it dominates politics (an "imbalanced" polity) — a good description of Pakistan's 1950s–60s.

| | Development administration | Administrative development |
|---|---|---|
| Object | Society/economy | The administrative system |
| Aim | Growth, welfare, change | Capacity, efficiency, rationality |
| Examples | Green Revolution programmes, BISP, motorways | Civil service reform, PFM reform, training, e-government |
| Orientation | Outward | Inward |
| Measured by | HDI, GDP, poverty | Government effectiveness, service quality |

**Role of bureaucracy in development administration in Pakistan (critical analysis):**
- *Positive role:* built early state (1947–58) with little capacity; Green Revolution and industrial growth in the 1960s; Tarbela and Mangla dams; NADRA and BISP as modern success; disaster response (2005 earthquake — ERRA; 2022 floods); polio campaign.
- *Negative role:* colonial elitism and distance from citizens; resistance to devolution; generalists in technical sectors; corruption and rent-seeking; alliance with military regimes (bureaucratic-military oligarchy — Hamza Alavi's "overdeveloped state", 1972); status quo orientation; red tape; poor implementation.
- *Constraints faced:* political instability; politicisation; low pay; fiscal crisis; weak local government; security challenges.
- *Judgement:* the bureaucracy can lead development only after administrative development — merit, specialisation, performance management, accountability, and partnership with elected local government. The 2024–26 reforms (specialised cadres, NES, performance pay) are attempts at administrative development; implementation is the test.

## 4. Fred Riggs' prismatic-sala model (asked 2017)

- **Works:** *The Ecology of Public Administration* (1961); *Administration in Developing Countries: The Theory of Prismatic Society* (1964).
- **Ecological approach:** administration must be studied in its social, economic, political and cultural environment.
- **The prism analogy:** white light (fused) passes through a prism and splits into colours (diffracted). Inside the prism the light is partly fused and partly diffracted.
  - **Fused society:** traditional, agrarian; one structure performs many functions (the king or chief as judge, administrator, priest).
  - **Diffracted society:** modern, industrial; specialised structures for each function.
  - **Prismatic society:** transitional — mixes traditional and modern features (most developing countries).
- **Sala:** the administrative sub-system of the prismatic society (Spanish word for room/office; between the "chamber" of fused societies and the "bureau/office" of diffracted ones).
- **Features of prismatic society:**
  1. **Heterogeneity** — modern and traditional coexist (cities vs villages; modern laws vs tribal customs).
  2. **Formalism** — gap between formal rules and actual behaviour; laws on paper, not in practice.
  3. **Overlapping** — modern structures exist but traditional ones still decide (a ministry exists, but family ties decide appointments).
  Sub-features of overlapping: **nepotism** (appointments by kinship), **poly-communalism** (many ethnic/religious communities competing — "clects", Riggs' term for groups mixing traditional and modern features), **poly-normativism** (multiple norms), **bazaar-canteen model** (economy where prices are negotiated and favoured groups get subsidised access — "canteen" for insiders, high prices for outsiders), **authority vs control** (formal authority without real control), **double-talk**.
- **Application to Pakistan:** formalism (laws like RTI and Article 140A not implemented); overlapping (biradari and *sifarish* in recruitment; jirgas alongside courts); heterogeneity (modern Islamabad vs tribal districts); bazaar-canteen (licences, plots, subsidies for insiders); clects (ethnic parties, religious groups).
- **Criticisms:** abstract jargon; Western-centric (assumes diffraction = development); static; negative image of developing countries; hard to test empirically; ignores colonial history and global economy. **Value:** still the best vocabulary for formalism and overlapping in South Asian administration.

## 5. Development administration vs development management

- **Development administration (1950s–70s):** state-led, top-down, planning-centred, focused on capacity of central bureaucracy; associated with modernisation theory and foreign aid; criticised for technocracy and failure to reach the poor.
- **Development management (1990s– ):** Derick Brinkerhoff and Benjamin Crosby, Brinkerhoff and Coston (1999); a broader, more pluralistic approach:
  1. Multiple actors — state, market, NGOs, communities (governance).
  2. Participatory and bottom-up methods.
  3. Results-based management, project cycle, logframes.
  4. Political-economy awareness; managing stakeholders.
  5. Values: empowerment, sustainability, gender equity.
  6. Influenced by NPM and good governance agendas.
- **Brinkerhoff and Coston's four faces of development management:** an instrument of foreign aid agendas; a toolkit (management techniques); a process intervention (participation, empowerment); and a values-based political enterprise.

| | Development administration | Development management |
|---|---|---|
| Era | 1950s–70s | 1990s– |
| Actor | State bureaucracy | State + market + civil society |
| Approach | Top-down planning | Participatory, adaptive |
| Tools | Five-year plans, public enterprises | Projects, logframes, results-based management, partnerships |
| Theory base | Modernisation | Governance, NPM, human development |
| Focus | Growth, capacity | Empowerment, sustainability, results |

## 6. Changing role of PA in development

- From **provider** to **enabler, regulator and partner** (World Bank, *World Development Report 1997: The State in a Changing World* — match the state's role to its capability, then raise capability).
- From planning to markets (1980s Washington Consensus) and back to a "smart state" (post-2008, East Asian lessons).
- **Sustainable Development Goals (2015–2030):** 17 goals; SDG 16 — peace, justice and strong institutions; Pakistan's Parliament adopted the SDGs as national goals in 2016 **[verify]**.
- **Climate adaptation** — PA as manager of climate risk (NDMA, National Adaptation Plan 2023).
- **Digital transformation** — digital public infrastructure.
- **New Public Governance** — co-production and networks.
- **Localisation** — local governments as development actors.

## 7. Globalisation and public administration (asked 2023, 2026)

- **Meaning:** growing interconnection of economies, societies and polities through flows of trade, capital, people, technology and ideas (Anthony Giddens: "intensification of worldwide social relations").
- **Causes (2026 note):** technology (ICT, internet, container shipping); trade liberalisation (GATT → WTO 1995); capital mobility and financial deregulation; multinational corporations and global value chains; end of the Cold War; international institutions (IMF, World Bank); migration; cultural flows (media); global problems needing cooperation (climate, pandemics).
- **Effects on public administration:**
  1. **Policy convergence and transfer** — NPM, PPPs and e-government spread worldwide.
  2. **External constraints** — IMF/World Bank conditionality; FATF (Pakistan on the grey list 2018–2022); WTO rules; credit ratings.
  3. **Competition for investment** — ease of doing business, special economic zones (CPEC SEZs), SIFC.
  4. **New tasks** — managing trade disputes, migration, cyber-security, transnational crime, pandemics (COVID-19), climate finance.
  5. **Multi-level governance** — local–national–global links.
  6. **Professional networks and training abroad.**
  7. **Hollowing out of the state** (Rhodes) — functions moved up (international bodies), down (local), out (agencies, private).
  8. **Accountability gaps** — decisions shaped by external actors.
  9. **Citizen expectations** rise with global standards.
- **Why administrators must pay attention:** policy space depends on global context (oil prices, interest rates); international commitments (UNCAC, Paris Agreement, SDGs, GSP+ conditions with the EU) create obligations; opportunities (remittances, IT exports, climate funds); risks (capital flight, sanctions).
- **Pakistan examples:** FATF action plan led to legal and institutional reforms (AML Act amendments 2020); EU GSP+ (since 2014) linked to 27 conventions; IMF programmes shape budget and energy pricing; CPEC brought Chinese standards and PPP models; overseas Pakistanis (about $38bn remittances in FY2025 **[verify]**).
- **Critique:** globalisation can reduce democratic control; imposes one-size reforms; increases inequality. **Glocalisation:** adapt global ideas to local context.

## 8. Issues and challenges of public administration in Pakistan

| Challenge | Detail |
|---|---|
| Colonial legacy | Elitist, controlling, distant from citizens; outdated laws |
| Politicisation | Postings and transfers as patronage; short tenures |
| Civil–military imbalance | Military role in administration and economy (SIFC, 27th Amendment); periods of martial law |
| Capacity | Generalists in technical posts; weak training of lower staff; poor data |
| Corruption | CPI 2025: 28/100, rank 136 |
| Fiscal crisis | Debt servicing crowds out development; PSDP cuts |
| Weak local government | Punjab without elected LGs since 2019; LG Act changed again in 2025 |
| Centre–province friction | 18th Amendment implementation; NFC; water; governor's rule in KP (2026) |
| Implementation gap | Good policies on paper, poor delivery |
| Accountability | Selective; slow courts; NAB controversies |
| Human development | HDI rank 168 of 193 (UNDP HDR 2025) ([UNDP Pakistan](https://www.undp.org/pakistan/press-releases/human-development-progress-slows-35-year-low-according-undps-2025-human-development-report)) |
| Population and urbanisation | 241.5 million in 2023 census; growth 2.55% a year; fast-growing cities without metropolitan governance |
| Climate | Floods 2022 (losses estimated over $30bn) and 2025; heatwaves; water stress |
| Security | Militancy in KP and Balochistan |
| Technology | Digital divide; cyber risks; data protection law pending |

**Is PA in Pakistan in a "prolonged crisis" without a paradigm? (2016)** *For:* reforms copied without fit (isomorphic mimicry); no settled model (TPA, NPM fragments, governance rhetoric coexist); politicisation; poor outcomes. *Against:* islands of excellence (NADRA, Motorway Police, Rescue 1122, BISP, SBP, PITB) show capacity exists; constitutional framework (1973, 18th Amendment) provides a paradigm of federal parliamentary governance; recent reforms (digital governance, contributory pension, rightsizing, the 2026 package) show direction. *Judgement:* a crisis of implementation and political settlement more than of paradigm.

**Way forward (use in conclusions):**
1. Political consensus on depoliticising the civil service (fixed tenures, independent boards).
2. Implement specialised cadres and performance management (2026 package) with legal backing.
3. Empower and protect local governments (constitutional amendment).
4. Fiscal reform: broaden tax base; cut losses of SOEs; efficient spending.
5. Digital governance with data protection law.
6. Fair, apolitical accountability.
7. Evidence-based policy: evaluation units, data.
8. Climate-resilient administration.
9. Citizen-centred services with feedback.
10. Civilian supremacy with institutional cooperation.
