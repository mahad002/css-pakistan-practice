# X. Governance and Administrative Reforms

Eight questions 2016–2026: privatisation (2017), decentralisation (2017, 2019), fixing bureaucracy (2018), re-engineering (2020), governance challenges (2020), good governance vs e-governance (2022), PPPs (2026), digital governance (2026, plus 2024 part-question). Expect one question from here every year.

## 1. Meaning of administrative reform

- **Gerald Caiden (*Administrative Reform*, 1969):** "the artificial inducement of administrative transformation against resistance". Three elements: deliberate (artificial), aims at transformation, meets resistance.
- **UN definition:** deliberate use of authority to apply new measures to an administrative system to change its goals, structures and procedures for developmental purposes.
- **Reform vs change:** change can happen naturally; reform is planned and political.

## 2. Theories of administrative reform

| Theory/approach | Idea |
|---|---|
| Rational/planning | Reform is designed by experts to fix identified problems (Weberian, NCGR-type commissions) |
| Public choice / NPM | Reform shrinks the state and introduces markets and incentives |
| Institutional theory (March and Olsen) | Reforms follow "logic of appropriateness"; path dependence — past choices constrain reform |
| Isomorphism (DiMaggio and Powell, 1983) | Organisations copy others: coercive (donor/IMF conditions), mimetic (copying "best practice"), normative (professional norms) — explains why Pakistan adopts reforms that do not fit |
| Garbage can | Reforms are solutions looking for problems |
| Political economy | Reforms succeed when they create winners who support them and compensate losers; elites block reforms that threaten rents |
| Incremental vs big-bang | Small steps (Lindblom) vs comprehensive change during a crisis or "window" |
| "Isomorphic mimicry" (Andrews, Pritchett and Woolcock, 2013) | Governments adopt the form of reforms without the function — "capability trap"; remedy: **PDIA** (problem-driven iterative adaptation) |

## 3. Types of administrative reforms

- **Structural** — reorganising ministries, merging/abolishing bodies (rightsizing 2024–25), creating agencies.
- **Procedural** — simplifying rules, one-window operations, e-Office, delegation.
- **Personnel/behavioural** — recruitment, training, pay, ethics, attitude change.
- **Financial** — budget systems, PFM, audit.
- **Technological** — e-government, digital services.
- **Accountability/transparency** — RTI, ombudsman, anti-corruption.
- **Decentralisation reforms.**
- **Market-type reforms** — privatisation, contracting out, PPPs.
- **Regulatory reforms** — independent regulators, deregulation.
- By scope: comprehensive vs sectoral; by direction: top-down vs bottom-up; by pace: incremental vs radical.

## 4. Privatization (asked 2017)

- **Meaning:** transfer of ownership or management of public enterprises/services to the private sector. Forms: full or partial sale (divestiture), management contracts, leases, concessions, contracting out, vouchers, deregulation of entry.
- **Rationale:** efficiency (public choice, property-rights theory); reduce fiscal burden of loss-making SOEs; raise revenue; attract investment and technology; reduce corruption and political hiring; consumer choice.
- **Global:** Thatcher's UK (British Telecom 1984, British Gas 1986); post-communist "shock therapy" (Russia's voucher privatisation, 1990s, criticised for creating oligarchs); Chile.
- **Pakistan:**
  - Nationalisation 1972–74 under Bhutto (31 industrial units, banks, insurance, later rice husking and flour mills) **[verify count]** created a large SOE sector.
  - **Privatisation Commission** (1991; Privatisation Commission Ordinance 2000).
  - Major sales: banks (MCB 1991, ABL, UBL 2002, HBL 2004), PTCL (26% to Etisalat, 2005–06 — dispute over unpaid $800m balance **[verify]**), KESC (2005), PSO and SSGC partial.
  - **PIA:** Arif Habib-led consortium won 75% stake with a **Rs135bn bid on 23 December 2025** in a televised auction (reserve price Rs100bn); most of the money to be reinvested in PIA; handover targeted April 2026 ([Dawn](https://www.dawn.com/news/1962791); [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2025/12/26/why-is-the-sale-of-pakistans-national-airline-stirring-a-political-storm)). Whether handover completed: **[verify]**.
  - SOE losses: power distribution companies (DISCOs), Pakistan Steel Mills, Railways; the SOE (Governance and Operations) Act 2023 and SOE policy 2023 **[verify]**.
- **Implications for bureaucracy:** role shifts from producer to regulator and contract manager; fewer posts in SOEs; need for regulatory skills; accountability through contracts and regulators.
- **Against:** private monopolies replace public ones; job losses; underpricing and corruption in sales; loss of strategic assets; services for the poor may suffer; weak regulators. **For:** losses stop; better service (telecom after liberalisation); fiscal space.
- **Conditions for success:** competition and strong independent regulation; transparent process; social safety net for workers; sequencing (restructure before sale).

## 5. Regulation and deregulation

- **Regulation:** government rules that shape private behaviour for public goals — economic (prices, entry), social (health, safety, environment), administrative (paperwork).
- **Theories:** public interest theory (correct market failures); capture theory (George Stigler, 1971 — regulators serve the industry); public choice (regulators maximise their own interests).
- **Regulatory state** (Majone, 1994): the state as rule-maker through independent agencies rather than owner.
- **Pakistan regulators:** NEPRA (1997, electricity), OGRA (2002, oil and gas), PTA (1996, telecom), SECP (1997), PEMRA (2002), Competition Commission of Pakistan (2007; Act 2010), DRAP (2012), State Bank (banking). Supreme Court's 2017 ruling on regulator control (that regulatory bodies should sit with relevant ministries rather than Cabinet Division) **[verify]**.
- **Problems:** political interference in tariffs; capture; weak capacity; litigation and stay orders; overlapping jurisdiction.
- **Deregulation:** removing or simplifying rules to cut costs and encourage competition — e.g. telecom liberalisation (2003–04 policy) gave Pakistan cheap mobile services; ease of doing business reforms; Board of Investment's regulatory guillotine; deregulation of petroleum prices debated. Risks: under-regulation (financial crisis 2008).
- **Better regulation:** regulatory impact assessment (RIA); sunset clauses; consultation.

## 6. Decentralization (asked 2017, 2019; part of 2025)

**Rondinelli's forms (1983):**
1. **Deconcentration** — transfer of administrative responsibility to field offices of the same central ministry (weakest form). Example: federal regional passport offices; provincial departments' district offices.
2. **Delegation** — transfer of responsibility to semi-autonomous bodies outside the regular hierarchy (authorities, corporations) that remain accountable to the centre. Example: WAPDA, NHA, development authorities.
3. **Devolution** — transfer of authority, resources and decision power to elected, legally autonomous sub-national governments. Example: 18th Amendment to provinces; LGO 2001 and Art. 140A to local governments.
4. **Privatisation/divestment** — transfer to private or voluntary sector.

**Other types:** political (power to elected local bodies), administrative (functions), fiscal (revenue and spending), market (privatisation).

**Benefits:** closer to citizens; better information; accountability; participation; innovation; reduces central overload; national integration in diverse societies.
**Risks:** elite capture; inequality between regions; coordination problems; weak local capacity; fiscal indiscipline.

**Which suits Pakistan? (2017)** A strong answer argues **devolution** is constitutionally required (Art. 140A) and best for accountability and ethnic-regional diversity, but must be phased with capacity building and fiscal transfers; deconcentration alone keeps the colonial model; delegation to authorities has fragmented cities.

## 7. Partnerships and collaboration; PPPs (asked 2026)

- **Collaborative governance** (Ansell and Gash, 2008): public agencies engage non-state stakeholders in collective, consensus-oriented decision-making.
- **Public–Private Partnership (PPP):** long-term contract in which the private party provides a public asset or service, bears significant risk and management responsibility, and is paid by users or government on performance. Models: BOT (build–operate–transfer), BOOT, DBFO (design–build–finance–operate), BOO, concession, management contract, lease; availability payments vs user fees. UK PFI (1992) a cautionary example (expensive).
- **Pakistan:** Public Private Partnership Authority Act 2017 (federal P3A; amended 2021 **[verify]**); Punjab PPP Act 2014 (amended 2019); Sindh PPP Act 2010 (Sindh has a strong record — e.g. Hyderabad–Mirpurkhas road, Thar coal) **[verify examples]**; motorway M-9 Karachi–Hyderabad and Lahore–Sialkot motorway as BOT; IPPs under the 1994 Power Policy (expensive capacity payments — renegotiated in 2024–25 **[verify]**); Sehat Card (insurance partnership); Adopt-a-School programmes; Punjab Education Foundation (vouchers, partner schools).
- **Is PA still central? (2026)** Yes — and more demanding:
  - Government defines the public need and output standards.
  - Appraises value for money and fiscal risk (contingent liabilities).
  - Designs and enforces contracts; regulates prices and quality.
  - Protects equity and access for the poor.
  - Remains accountable to citizens when the partner fails ("you can contract out services, not responsibility").
  - Manages networks and dispute resolution.
  So PPPs shift PA from rowing to steering and regulating — they do not remove it.
- **Risks:** hidden debt; renegotiation in favour of private partner; capture; weak contract management; user-fee exclusion.

## 8. Business (process) re-engineering (2020 note)

- **Meaning:** fundamental rethinking and radical redesign of business processes to achieve dramatic improvements in cost, quality, service and speed (Hammer and Champy, *Reengineering the Corporation*, 1993; Hammer, "Don't Automate, Obliterate", *Harvard Business Review*, 1990).
- **Key words:** fundamental, radical, dramatic, processes.
- **Principles:** organise around outcomes, not tasks; let those who use the output perform the process; capture information once at source; integrate parallel activities; put decision points where work is done; use IT as an enabler.
- **Public sector re-engineering:** one-stop shops; online services; removing redundant approvals; case-worker models.
- **Pakistan examples:** NADRA's CNIC and e-Sahulat; FBR's IRIS online filing; Punjab e-Khidmat Markaz (multiple services in one place); e-Office in federal ministries; Punjab Land Records Authority replaced patwari manual records; Pakistan Single Window for trade (2022).
- **Criticisms:** high failure rate in companies (many projects did not deliver); job cuts and low morale; ignores human side; in government, law and accountability limit radical redesign. Lesson: combine with change management and incremental improvement (TQM).

## 9. Quality assurance

- **Total Quality Management (TQM):** W. Edwards Deming (14 points; PDCA cycle — plan, do, check, act), Joseph Juran (quality trilogy), Philip Crosby (zero defects). Continuous improvement (kaizen), customer focus, employee involvement, data-based decisions.
- **ISO 9001** quality management certification (some Pakistani public bodies: e.g. passport offices, NADRA centres **[verify]**).
- **Citizen's Charter** (UK, John Major, 1991): published service standards, information, choice, courtesy, redress, value for money. India's Sevottam model.
- **Benchmarking; service standards; customer satisfaction surveys; Common Assessment Framework (EU).**
- **Limits in government:** many "customers" with conflicting demands; quality hard to define for coercive services (police, tax).

## 10. Good governance

- **Meaning:** governance that is participatory, consensus-oriented, accountable, transparent, responsive, effective and efficient, equitable and inclusive, and follows the rule of law (UNESCAP's eight characteristics).
- **World Bank's six Worldwide Governance Indicators:** voice and accountability; political stability and absence of violence; government effectiveness; regulatory quality; rule of law; control of corruption.
- **Good governance vs e-governance (2022):** good governance is the *goal* (quality of governance); e-governance is a *tool* — using ICT to deliver services, inform and engage citizens and run government. E-governance can promote transparency, speed and reduce discretion, but without good governance (rule of law, accountability) it can digitise inefficiency or enable surveillance.

## 11. E-governance and digital governance (asked 2022, 2024, 2026)

**Concepts:**
- **E-government:** use of ICT inside government and for services.
- **Models:** G2C (government to citizen), G2B, G2G, G2E (to employees).
- **Stages (Layne and Lee, 2001):** catalogue (information) → transaction → vertical integration → horizontal integration. UN stages: emerging, enhanced, transactional, connected.
- **Digital governance / digital-era governance** (Dunleavy et al., 2006): reintegration of services, needs-based holism, digitisation — a post-NPM model. Data-driven policy, platforms, AI, digital public infrastructure (DPI: identity, payments, data exchange).

**How digital governance changes bureaucracy (2026):**
- Decision-making: data analytics and algorithms support or automate decisions (tax risk scoring, benefit targeting); faster, evidence-based; but algorithmic bias and opacity.
- Accountability and transparency: digital trails, open data, dashboards, online grievance systems — but accountability for automated decisions is unclear.
- Hierarchy flattens; information flows horizontally; street-level discretion shrinks ("system-level bureaucracy", Bovens and Zouridis, 2002).
- Service delivery shifts to online channels and one-stop portals.
- Sustainability: needs continuous funding, skills, maintenance, cyber-security.

**Challenges of digital governance in developing countries (2026 note):**
1. Digital divide — access, literacy, gender gap (women's mobile ownership much lower than men's in Pakistan **[cite GSMA figure only if verified]**).
2. Infrastructure — connectivity, power outages.
3. Capacity — few skilled staff; reliance on vendors.
4. Finance — high upfront and maintenance costs.
5. Legal framework — data protection law (Pakistan's Personal Data Protection Bill still pending as of 2026 **[verify]**), cyber law (PECA 2016, amended 2025).
6. Cyber-security and data breaches (NADRA data leak inquiries **[verify]**).
7. Resistance to change; loss of rent-seeking opportunities.
8. Interoperability — siloed systems.
9. Ethics — surveillance, privacy, exclusion of those without IDs, algorithmic bias.
10. Internet shutdowns and social media restrictions undermine trust.

**Pakistan's digital governance:**
- **NADRA** (2000): CNIC database, among the largest; NSER for BISP targeting; e-Sahulat.
- **Digital Nation Pakistan Act 2025:** National Digital Commission, Pakistan Digital Authority, digital identity and data exchange framework **[verify details]**.
- **National AI Policy 2025.**
- **Pakistan Citizen's Portal** (2018) — complaints.
- **FBR:** IRIS, POS integration, track-and-trace.
- **Raast** (SBP instant payment system, 2021).
- **e-Office** in federal ministries; Punjab's PITB apps (e-Khidmat, e-Stamp, e-Registration).
- **UN E-Government Development Index:** Pakistan's ranking is low-middle **[cite the 2024 EGDI rank only after checking]**.

## 12. Administrative reform in Pakistan — record

| Phase | Reform | Result |
|---|---|---|
| 1950s | Egger (1953), Gladieux (1955) reports | Partly implemented; CSP unchanged |
| 1959–62 | Ayub: Cornelius Commission, Basic Democracies, training institutes | Bureaucracy strengthened as partner of martial law |
| 1973 | Bhutto reforms | Unified grades, groups, lateral entry; politicisation |
| 1978–88 | Zia: Anwarul Haq Commission; military induction | Militarisation of civil posts |
| 1996–97 | Fakhar Imam / various committees; Accountability Ordinance 1997 | Limited |
| 2000–08 | Musharraf: NRB, LGO 2001, Police Order 2002, NCGR (2006–08), Federal Tax Ombudsman (2000), NAB (1999) | Devolution later reversed; NCGR not implemented |
| 2010 | 18th Amendment | Devolution to provinces; Concurrent List abolished |
| 2013–18 | PML-N: delivery units, e-governance in Punjab, Vision 2025 | Service improvements in Punjab; companies controversy |
| 2018–22 | PTI: Task Force (Ishrat Husain), E&D Rules 2020, Directory Retirement 2020, restructuring proposals, Citizen's Portal, Ehsaas | Partial |
| 2024–26 | PML-N-led coalition: rightsizing (82 bodies, 150,000 posts), contributory pension, Conduct Rules 2026, PIA privatisation, Digital Nation Act 2025, civil service package (Sep 2026, pending) | In progress |

**Why reforms fail in Pakistan:** no political ownership; reforms driven by donors or military regimes and reversed by successors; bureaucratic resistance; reforms designed in form not function (isomorphic mimicry); no implementation unit; no funding; lack of sequencing; frequent changes of government.

**How to make reform work:** political consensus (charter on governance); a reform delivery unit with authority; sequencing (basics first — merit, budget execution, digitisation); pilots and scaling; protect reformers; measure and publish results; involve provinces; link to IMF/donor support without letting donors own the agenda.

## 13. Governance challenges in Pakistan (2020 note)

Political instability and polarisation; civil–military imbalance; corruption and elite capture; weak rule of law and delayed justice; fiscal crisis and debt; weak local government; poor service delivery in health and education (about 25 million out-of-school children **[verify latest]**); population growth (2.55% a year, 2023 census); climate vulnerability (2022 floods, 2025 floods); security (KP and Balochistan militancy); low tax base; centre–province tensions (governor's rule in KP, Oct 2026).
