# IV. Budgeting and Financial Management

Nine questions in eleven years. The **federal budget process in Pakistan** came in 2016, 2019 and 2022 (2016 and 2022 word for word). Budget systems (line-item, performance, PPBS, ZBB, incremental) came in 2016, 2017, 2021, 2022 and 2025. Learn the evolution table and the Pakistan process cold.

---

## 1. What a budget is

- A statement of estimated revenue and expenditure for a financial year, approved by the legislature. In Pakistan the **Annual Budget Statement** (Art. 80) for the year 1 July – 30 June.
- **Aaron Wildavsky:** a budget is a series of goals with price tags attached; it is political — "who gets what".
- **V.O. Key (1940), "The Lack of a Budgetary Theory":** "On what basis shall it be decided to allocate X dollars to activity A instead of activity B?" Every budget reform tries to answer this (asked 2022).
- **Verne Lewis (1952):** answered Key with economic reasoning — relative value, incremental analysis, relative effectiveness.

## 2. Functions: budget as a policy tool and a managerial tool

**Allen Schick, "The Road to PPB" (1966): three orientations of budgeting:**
1. **Control** — preventing overspending and misuse (line-item).
2. **Management** — efficient use of resources (performance budgeting).
3. **Planning** — choosing among policy objectives (PPBS).

**As a policy tool:**
- Expresses priorities (defence vs education).
- **Fiscal policy (Musgrave's three functions):** allocation (public goods), distribution (taxes and transfers), stabilisation (counter-cyclical spending — relevant to the 2016/2022 question on the business cycle).
- Development (PSDP).
- Signals to markets and IMF.

**As a managerial tool:**
- Plans operations; allocates resources to units.
- Controls spending (releases, re-appropriation rules).
- Measures performance (output budgeting).
- Coordinates departments.
- Basis for accountability (audit against appropriations).

## 3. Principles of budgeting (asked 2025)

**Classical (traditional) principles (Sundelson, Neumark, and others):**
1. **Annuality** — one fiscal year.
2. **Unity/comprehensiveness** — all revenue and expenditure in one budget.
3. **Universality/gross budgeting** — show gross amounts, no netting.
4. **Non-assignment (non-earmarking)** — revenue goes to a common fund (Federal Consolidated Fund, Art. 78).
5. **Specificity** — spend only on purposes approved.
6. **Balance** — revenue matches expenditure (now replaced by sustainable deficit rules).
7. **Publicity and clarity.**
8. **Accuracy** — realistic estimates.
9. **Prior authorisation** — legislature approves before spending.
10. **Legislative control and audit.**

**Harold Smith's executive-budget principles (1945):** executive programming, executive responsibility, reporting, adequate tools, multiple procedures, executive discretion, flexibility in timing, two-way budget organisation. (Shows the move from legislative control to executive management.)

**Modern PFM principles (OECD, 2015):** fiscal rules; alignment with strategic priorities; medium-term framework; transparency; inclusive debate; comprehensive accounting; performance information; fiscal risk management; integrity of forecasts; effective implementation.

## 4. Evolution of budget systems

| System | When/where | Focus | Question it answers | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Line-item** | US cities 1900s; US Budget and Accounting Act 1921; Taft Commission 1912 | Inputs: salaries, supplies by object | What will we buy? | Control, simplicity, audit-friendly | Ignores results; encourages incrementalism and "use it or lose it" spending |
| **Incremental** | Wildavsky (1964) described it | Last year's base + small change | How much more? | Stable, low conflict, low info cost | Locks in past; no review of base |
| **Performance** | Hoover Commission 1949; US Budget and Accounting Procedures Act 1950 | Activities, workload, unit costs | What will we do? | Links money to output | Measuring outputs is hard; output ≠ outcome |
| **PPBS (programme)** | Rand Corporation; McNamara at US Defense Dept 1961; Johnson extended 1965; dropped 1971 | Objectives, programmes, multi-year, cost–benefit | What are we trying to achieve? | Planning, rational comparison of options | Data-heavy, centralised, politically naive |
| **MBO** | Nixon 1973 (Drucker's idea) | Objectives agreed by manager and subordinate | | | Short-lived |
| **Zero-base (ZBB)** | Peter Pyhrr at Texas Instruments (1969–70); Carter in Georgia, then federal 1977 | Every activity justified from zero; "decision packages" ranked | Should we do this at all? | Questions the base; finds waste | Huge paperwork; little real reallocation |
| **Target-based / top-down** | 1980s cutbacks | Ceilings set first | What can we afford? | Fiscal discipline | Arbitrary cuts |
| **Outcome-based / results-based** | New Zealand 1989 (Public Finance Act), US GPRA 1993, UK PSAs 1998 | Outcomes and results; performance contracts | What difference will it make? | Accountability for results | Attribution and gaming |

**Short-note material:**
- **Incremental budgeting (2016):** base + increment; Lindblom/Wildavsky logic; "fair share" and "base" concepts; defended as politically rational; criticised for inertia. Pakistan's current budget is largely incremental (salaries, pensions, interest).
- **PPBS (2016):** five elements — define objectives; programme structure; multi-year programme and financial plan; analysis of alternatives (cost–benefit); programme memoranda. Failed because of data demands and politics, but left programme classification and policy analysis units.
- **Dissensus budgeting (2016):** budgeting when there is no consensus on priorities or on the size of government — conflict between executive and legislature, coalition partners or centre and provinces; Wildavsky described how the US budget consensus broke down in the 1970s–80s (deficits, continuing resolutions, shutdowns). Features: delays, stop-gap spending, top-down ceilings, budget summits, use of fiscal rules to force agreement. Pakistan parallels: coalition bargaining over PSDP schemes; federal–provincial disputes over NFC; IMF as external arbiter **[concept is not standard textbook terminology; frame carefully]**.
- **ZBB (2017):** steps — identify decision units; prepare decision packages (minimum level, current level, enhanced level); rank packages; allocate funds down the ranking. Variants: "sunset" laws, periodic spending reviews. Pakistan: never fully applied; rightsizing 2024–25 had a ZBB flavour.
- **Performance budgeting (2021, 2025):** elements — classification by functions, programmes, activities; performance measures (inputs, outputs, outcomes, efficiency); unit costs; performance targets; reporting and audit of performance. Pakistan: the Output-Based Budgeting (OBB) introduced in the federal budget under the MTBF (from 2009–10 in some ministries, all ministries by 2012–13 **[verify]**) shows outputs, KPIs and targets for each ministry in the "Green Book"; provinces adopted similar MTBF/OBB. Problems: KPIs not used in allocation decisions; weak data; no consequence for missing targets; budget still controlled by line items.

## 5. Audit and accounting in government

**Accounting:**
- Cash vs accrual basis: Pakistan uses modified cash, moving toward accrual (IPSAS) **[verify stage]**.
- **Controller General of Accounts (CGA)** prepares accounts (separated from audit in 2001 under the CGA Ordinance); AGPR (Accountant General Pakistan Revenues) for federal; provincial Accountants General.
- **PIFRA** (Project to Improve Financial Reporting and Auditing, World Bank, from 1996): computerised SAP-based financial management system (FABS); new accounting model (NAM).
- **Principal Accounting Officer:** the Secretary of each Division.

**Audit:**
- **Auditor-General of Pakistan (AGP), Arts. 168–171:** appointed by the President on the PM's advice; four-year term **[verify current term]**; audits federal and provincial accounts; reports to the President (federal) and Governors (provincial), who lay them before the National Assembly/Provincial Assemblies.
- **Audit Ordinance 2001** sets functions.
- **Types:** compliance/financial audit; performance audit (economy, efficiency, effectiveness — the "3Es"); IT audit; forensic audit; special audits.
- **Public Accounts Committee (PAC):** standing committee of the National Assembly (by convention chaired by the opposition leader or an opposition member since 2008); examines audit reports; directs recoveries.
- **Internal audit** in ministries — weak.
- **Problems:** backlog of paras; delayed PAC discussion; repetition of the same irregularities; weak follow-up; officials fear audit, which slows decisions.

## 7. The federal budget process in Pakistan (asked 2016, 2019, 2022)

**Legal basis:** Constitution (above); **Public Finance Management Act 2019** (PFM Act); Fiscal Responsibility and Debt Limitation Act 2005 (merged into the PFM framework and amended 2022 **[verify]**); Budget Manual.

**Stages and calendar:**
1. **Preparation (October–April):**
   - Finance Division issues the **Budget Call Circular** (BCC) with ceilings and forms (around October–December).
   - **Budget Strategy Paper (BSP)** sets macro framework and indicative ceilings, approved by cabinet (under the PFM Act, usually by March/April).
   - Ministries submit Medium-Term Budget Estimates (MTBF: three-year rolling).
   - Planning Commission prepares PSDP proposals; **Annual Plan Coordination Committee (APCC)** and **NEC** approve the development outlay (May/June).
   - FBR prepares revenue proposals; IMF consultations in programme years.
2. **Presentation (early–mid June):** Finance Minister presents the budget speech, the Finance Bill (tax measures), the Annual Budget Statement, demands for grants, PSDP, and supporting documents ("Green Book", "Pink Book" etc.). The Economic Survey is released the day before.
3. **Legislative approval (June):** general debate in NA; Senate gives recommendations on the Finance Bill within 14 days; NA votes demands for grants and cut motions; Finance Bill passed; must be done before 30 June.
4. **Execution (July–June):** releases by Finance Division; AGPR payments; re-appropriations within rules; supplementary grants (Art. 84) — often criticised as a "parallel budget".
5. **Accounting and audit:** CGA accounts; AGP audit; PAC review; ex-post approval of excess spending.

**Can it fund government and stabilise the economy? (2016/2022 framing)**
- **Revenue side:** tax-to-GDP about 10–11% (FBR), among the lowest in the region **[verify FY26 figure from Economic Survey]**; narrow base; heavy reliance on indirect taxes and withholding; agriculture, retail and real estate under-taxed; provinces collect little of their own.
- **Expenditure side:** interest payments take the largest share of federal current spending (more than half of net federal revenue in recent years **[verify]**); defence; pensions; subsidies (power circular debt); little left for development — PSDP is the first to be cut.
- **Stabilisation role weak:** pro-cyclical — spending grows in booms and is cut in crises; IMF programmes force contraction; fiscal rules routinely breached.
- **NFC effect:** after the 7th award most of the divisible pool goes to provinces; the federation borrows to meet its obligations.

**Problems of the process:**
1. Unrealistic revenue targets.
2. Weak parliamentary scrutiny — a short window, few amendments, no independent parliamentary budget office.
3. Large supplementary grants and mid-year mini-budgets.
4. Disconnect between plan and budget; PSDP politicised.
5. Off-budget liabilities: circular debt, SOE losses, guarantees.
6. Low transparency of tax expenditures (exemptions).
7. Limited citizen participation.
8. Late releases to ministries.

**Reforms:** PFM Act 2019 (BSP, medium-term framework, cash management through Treasury Single Account, limits on supplementary grants); MTBF and output-based budgeting; FBR digitisation and track-and-trace; broadening the base (traders, agricultural income tax by provinces under the IMF programme **[verify]**); SOE reform (SOE Act 2023 **[verify]**); citizen's budget documents.

## 8. Budget deficit: why it matters and how to control it (asked 2023)

**Why it matters:** growing debt and interest burden ("crowding out" of development spending); inflation if monetised; external crises and IMF dependence; loss of policy space; intergenerational burden; credit ratings.

**Strategies:**
- **Revenue:** broaden the tax base, cut exemptions, digitise collection, property and agricultural income tax, better compliance.
- **Expenditure:** rationalise subsidies (target them), rightsizing (2024–25), pension reform (contributory pension for new entrants from July 2024; pension calculated on average of last 24 months' pay), SOE privatisation (PIA sold December 2025), austerity committees.
- **Institutional:** fiscal rules (FRDL Act 2005; primary balance targets), medium-term frameworks, independent fiscal councils, Treasury Single Account, debt management office.
- **International examples:** US Gramm–Rudman–Hollings Act 1985 (automatic cuts), Budget Enforcement Act 1990 (PAYGO), sequestration 2013; Sweden's expenditure ceiling (1990s); EU Stability and Growth Pact (3% deficit, 60% debt).

## 9. Planning and budgeting in Pakistan — link

- Two budgets in practice: **current** (Finance Division) and **development** (Planning Commission/PSDP). Separation causes the "recurrent cost" problem: new schools and hospitals built from PSDP without funds to run them.
- MTBF (introduced 2009–10) tried to link them with three-year ceilings.
- **Way forward:** unified budget; programme budgeting for major sectors; spending reviews; outcome targets in performance agreements; linking provincial transfers to service outcomes in the next NFC.
