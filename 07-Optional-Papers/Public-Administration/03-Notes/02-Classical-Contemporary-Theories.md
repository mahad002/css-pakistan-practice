# II. Classical and Contemporary Theories and Concepts

Asked every year 2018–2026 (13 questions). Weber, Taylor, Fayol and Mayo are the core. Leadership theory is the second favourite. In 2025 the examiner asked scientific management, network governance and public choice together.

---

## 1. Bureaucracy — Max Weber (1864–1920)

**Source:** *Economy and Society* (published 1922, after his death).

**Three types of authority (legitimate domination):**
1. **Traditional** — custom and inheritance (monarchs, tribal chiefs, sardars).
2. **Charismatic** — personal qualities of a leader (prophets, revolutionary leaders); unstable, needs "routinisation".
3. **Legal-rational** — rules enacted by procedure; obedience to the office, not the person. Bureaucracy is its purest form.

**Features of the ideal-type bureaucracy:**
1. Fixed jurisdictional areas set by rules.
2. Hierarchy of offices.
3. Written documents (files).
4. Specialised training.
5. Full-time career; salary and pension; promotion by seniority or merit.
6. Impersonality: rules applied without hatred or passion ("sine ira et studio").
7. Separation of office from the private property of the official.
8. Appointment (not election) on technical qualification.

**Why Weber thought it superior:** precision, speed, clarity, continuity, discretion, unity, reduced friction and cost — "technically superior to any other form of organisation", like a machine compared with non-mechanical production.

**Weber's worries:** the "iron cage" of rationality; officials dominating politicians; need for strong parliamentary leadership.

**Criticisms (dysfunctions):**
- **Robert Merton (1940):** "bureaucratic personality"; trained incapacity; goal displacement — rules become ends in themselves.
- **Philip Selznick (TVA study, 1949):** delegation creates sub-goals; co-optation.
- **Alvin Gouldner (1954):** mock, representative and punishment-centred bureaucracy.
- **Michel Crozier (1964):** vicious circles of rules and power in French bureaucracy.
- **Human relations school:** ignores informal groups and feelings.
- **Warren Bennis (1966):** bureaucracy will die out in fast-changing environments.
- **Public choice:** officials maximise budgets (Niskanen).
- **Developing countries:** Riggs — formalism; rules on paper, personal rule in practice.

**Relevance today (21st-century question):** Weberian virtues (merit, impartiality, rule of law) are what developing countries most lack. Evans and Rauch (1999) found "Weberian" features (merit recruitment, career rewards) linked to faster growth. The "neo-Weberian state" (Pollitt and Bouckaert) keeps Weber's core while adding citizen orientation and results.

**Pakistan:** formally Weberian (Civil Servants Act 1973, rules, files, hierarchy) but in practice personalised: transfers by political patronage, *sifarish*, short tenures (often under a year for DCs and police chiefs), and "file culture" delays.

## 2. Scientific Management — F.W. Taylor (1856–1915)

**Source:** *The Principles of Scientific Management* (1911). Worked at Midvale Steel and Bethlehem Steel (pig-iron loading, shovelling studies).

**Four principles:**
1. Develop a science for each element of work (replace rule of thumb) — time and motion study.
2. Scientifically select, train and develop workers.
3. Cooperate with workers to ensure work follows the science.
4. Divide work and responsibility between management (planning) and workers (doing).

**Techniques:** time study, motion study (Frank and Lillian Gilbreth), standardisation of tools, differential piece-rate, functional foremanship (eight foremen), task planning, exception principle. Henry Gantt: Gantt chart.

**Contribution to PA:** efficiency movement in US government (Taft Commission 1912); budgeting and O&M (organisation and methods) units; work measurement; modern performance management and lean methods descend from Taylor.

**Criticisms:** treats workers as machines ("economic man"); ignores social needs; deskilling; resisted by unions (US House hearings 1911–12); efficiency at the expense of democracy and equity.

**Pakistan:** O&M Wing of the Establishment Division; time-bound service standards (e.g. passport and NADRA processing times); Punjab's e-Khidmat centres.

## 3. Administrative Management / "Principles" school

### Henri Fayol (1841–1925)
*Administration Industrielle et Générale* (1916; English 1949).
**Five functions:** planning (prévoyance), organising, commanding, coordinating, controlling.
**Fourteen principles:** division of work; authority and responsibility; discipline; unity of command; unity of direction; subordination of individual interest to general interest; remuneration; centralisation; scalar chain (with "Fayol's bridge"/gangplank for horizontal communication); order; equity; stability of tenure; initiative; esprit de corps.

### Luther Gulick and Lyndall Urwick
*Papers on the Science of Administration* (1937). POSDCORB. Gulick sat on the Brownlow Committee (1937) on reorganising the US executive. Principles: span of control, unity of command, departmentalisation by purpose, process, persons or place.

### Mooney and Reiley
*Onward Industry* (1931): coordinative, scalar and functional principles.

**Simon's critique ("The Proverbs of Administration", 1946):** principles contradict each other — narrow span of control means more levels, which slows communication; specialisation by purpose conflicts with specialisation by process. They are proverbs, not science.

### Comparison (asked 2022)

| | Weber | Taylor | Fayol / Gulick |
|---|---|---|---|
| Level | Whole organisation, authority | Shop floor, task | Top management |
| Core idea | Legal-rational authority | One best way to do a job | Universal principles of management |
| Discipline | Sociology | Engineering | Management practice |
| Shared | Rationality, hierarchy, specialisation, efficiency, closed-system view | | |
| Critique | Rigid, impersonal | Mechanistic | Proverbs, untested |

**Why critics say they do not fit Pakistan's practice:** written rules coexist with patronage; hierarchy is used for control, not coordination; principles assume a neutral, rule-following culture that Pakistan's "administrative culture" does not provide (see §10).

## 4. Human Relations Approach

### Elton Mayo and the Hawthorne studies (1924–1932)
At Western Electric's Hawthorne Works near Chicago; Mayo, Fritz Roethlisberger and William Dickson (Harvard) joined from 1927.
**Phases:**
1. **Illumination experiments (1924–27):** output rose in both test and control groups whatever the lighting — something other than physical conditions mattered.
2. **Relay assembly test room (1927–32):** six women; output rose with almost every change, even when breaks were removed — attention, group cohesion and participation mattered.
3. **Mass interviewing (1928–30):** over 20,000 interviews; workers valued being listened to.
4. **Bank wiring observation room (1931–32):** 14 men; informal group set its own output norms and punished "rate-busters" and "chiselers".
**Conclusions:** workers are social beings; informal groups shape output; morale and supervision matter; communication and participation raise productivity. The "Hawthorne effect": behaviour changes because people know they are being studied.
**Book:** Roethlisberger and Dickson, *Management and the Worker* (1939).

**Others:** Mary Parker Follett (conflict by integration; "power with" not "power over"; law of the situation); Chester Barnard (*The Functions of the Executive*, 1938 — organisation as a cooperative system; acceptance theory of authority; zone of indifference).

**Criticisms:** poor scientific method (small samples); manipulation of workers ("cow sociology"); ignores conflict of interests and unions; overstates happiness → productivity.

**Contribution:** motivation theory, participative management, organisational behaviour; the later behavioural approach (Simon, Argyris, McGregor, Likert).

## 6. Leadership

**Definition:** the ability to influence a group towards goals (Robbins). "A process, not a position" (2018 question).

**Three phases of leadership theory (asked 2018):**
1. **Trait theories (to late 1940s):** leaders are born with traits — intelligence, dominance, self-confidence, energy. Stogdill's review (1948) found no consistent set of traits.
2. **Behavioural theories (1940s–60s):**
   - Lewin, Lippitt and White (1939): autocratic, democratic, laissez-faire.
   - Ohio State studies: initiating structure and consideration.
   - Michigan studies: job-centred vs employee-centred.
   - Blake and Mouton's Managerial Grid (1964): concern for people × concern for production; 1,1 impoverished; 9,1 task; 1,9 country club; 5,5 middle-of-the-road; 9,9 team (ideal).
3. **Contingency/situational theories (1960s–70s):**
   - **Fiedler (1967):** LPC (least preferred co-worker) scale; effectiveness depends on leader–member relations, task structure and position power; task-oriented leaders do best in very favourable or very unfavourable situations.
   - **Hersey and Blanchard situational leadership (1969):** style matches follower readiness (maturity): telling (S1, low readiness), selling (S2), participating (S3), delegating (S4, high readiness).
   - **House's path–goal theory (1971):** leader clears the path to goals; four styles — directive, supportive, participative, achievement-oriented — chosen by subordinate and task characteristics.
   - Vroom–Yetton (1973): decision participation model.
   - Tannenbaum and Schmidt continuum (1958).

**Hersey–Blanchard vs House (asked 2019):** both contingency; both choose style by situation. H–B focuses on follower readiness and is simple to apply; path–goal looks at subordinate needs *and* task features and rests on expectancy theory. For Pakistan: H–B suits a service with mixed capacity (direct new recruits, delegate to experienced officers); path–goal suits reform units with ambiguous tasks. A sensible answer picks one and justifies.

**Newer theories:**
- **Transactional vs transformational:** James MacGregor Burns, *Leadership* (1978); Bernard Bass (1985). Transformational = the "four I's": idealised influence, inspirational motivation, intellectual stimulation, individualised consideration. Transactional = contingent reward and management by exception.
- **Authoritarian (autocratic):** decisions by the leader alone; quick but demotivating (2021 short note: compare with transformational).
- **Servant leadership:** Robert Greenleaf (1970).
- **Charismatic:** Weber; House (1977).
- **Distributed/shared leadership (asked 2023):** leadership spread across the organisation; followers lead through expertise and initiative; Kelley's types of followers (1988) — exemplary followers act like leaders. Public examples: Rescue 1122 field teams; polio vaccinators' community leadership.
- **Adaptive leadership:** Heifetz (1994) — technical vs adaptive problems.
- **Ethical/authentic leadership.**

**Qualities of effective public administrators:** integrity; vision; communication; technical competence; political sensitivity; empathy; decisiveness; ability to build networks; resilience. Robert Katz (1955) three skills: technical, human, conceptual.

## 7. Network Governance

- **Meaning:** public problems are handled through networks of interdependent organisations — government agencies, NGOs, firms, communities — linked by trust and exchange rather than hierarchy or market.
- **Thinkers:** Rhodes (1997, "governing without government"); Kickert, Klijn and Koppenjan (*Managing Complex Networks*, 1997); Agranoff and McGuire (*Collaborative Public Management*, 2003); Goldsmith and Eggers (*Governing by Network*, 2004).
- **Assumptions:** no single actor has the resources to solve "wicked problems" (Rittel and Webber, 1973); actors cooperate because cooperation advances their own goals (this was a 2026 MCQ).
- **Network management:** activating, framing, mobilising, synthesising (Agranoff and McGuire).
- **Advantages:** pooled resources; flexibility; local knowledge; legitimacy.
- **Problems:** blurred accountability ("problem of many hands"); slow consensus; capture by powerful members; coordination cost.
- **Pakistan:** NCOC during COVID-19 (federal, provincial, military, health agencies); polio eradication (government, WHO, UNICEF, Gates Foundation, community workers); NDMA–PDMA–NGO networks in floods.

## 8. Strategic Management

- **Strategic planning** = a disciplined effort to produce fundamental decisions and actions shaping what an organisation is and does, and why (Bryson, 1988).
- **Evolution (asked 2020):**
  1. Budget control and long-range planning (1950s) — forecasting.
  2. Corporate strategic planning (1960s–70s): Harvard Policy Model, SWOT (Andrews), Ansoff.
  3. Strategic management (1980s): Porter's competitive strategy; implementation and control added.
  4. Public-sector adoption (1980s–90s): Bryson; NPM; US GPRA 1993.
  5. Emergent and collaborative strategy (1990s– ): Mintzberg (strategy emerges from practice); Mark Moore's public value (1995) — strategic triangle: public value, legitimacy and support (authorising environment), operational capacity.
- **Bryson's ten-step cycle:** initiate agreement; identify mandates; clarify mission and values; assess environment (SWOT); identify strategic issues; formulate strategies; review and adopt plan; establish vision; implement; reassess.
- **Tools:** SWOT, PESTEL, stakeholder analysis, balanced scorecard (Kaplan and Norton 1992), scenario planning, logframe.
- **Collaborative strategy — opportunities:** shared resources, legitimacy, innovation, coverage. **Constraints:** conflicting mandates, power imbalance, accountability gaps, short political cycles, lack of trust and data.
- **Public vs private difference:** many stakeholders; political mandates; no single bottom line; annual budgets; leadership turnover.
- **Pakistan:** Vision 2025 (2014); "Uraan Pakistan" five-year plan (launched 31 December 2024, "5Es": exports, e-Pakistan, environment, energy, equity and empowerment) **[verify the 5E names]**; ministry strategic plans under performance agreements.

## 9. Public Choice Theory

- **Core idea:** apply economics to politics — voters, politicians and bureaucrats are rational, self-interested utility maximisers.
- **Founders:** James Buchanan and Gordon Tullock, *The Calculus of Consent* (1962) (Buchanan: Nobel 1986); Anthony Downs, *An Economic Theory of Democracy* (1957) and *Inside Bureaucracy* (1967); Tullock, *The Politics of Bureaucracy* (1965); William Niskanen, *Bureaucracy and Representative Government* (1971) — bureaucrats maximise budgets; Vincent Ostrom, *The Intellectual Crisis in American Public Administration* (1973) — polycentric, democratic administration. Dunleavy's bureau-shaping model (1991) corrected Niskanen: senior officials prefer to shape their bureaus (keep policy work, shed routine delivery) rather than maximise budgets.
- **Concepts:** government failure; rent-seeking (Tullock, Krueger); log-rolling; median voter; principal–agent problem; Downs' types of officials (climbers, conservers, zealots, advocates, statesmen).
- **Prescriptions:** competition, contracting out, privatisation, smaller government, decentralisation, user charges → feeds NPM.
- **Criticisms:** too narrow a view of human motives (ignores public service motivation); empirical support for budget maximisation is weak; ideological bias toward small government.
- **Pakistan:** rent-seeking in licences and permits; SOE losses; political hiring in PIA, Steel Mills.

## 10. Types of Organisational Structure

- **Formal vs informal** organisation.
- **Line, staff and auxiliary agencies:** line delivers services (ministries, police); staff advises (Prime Minister's Office, Planning Commission); auxiliary serves other agencies (Establishment Division, Pakistan Public Works Department).
- **Functional, divisional (by product/area/client), matrix, project/team, network/virtual.**
- **Tall vs flat** (span of control). The 2026 reform package proposes cutting federal decision tiers from five to three (flattening).
- **Mechanistic vs organic** (Burns and Stalker, 1961): mechanistic for stable environments; organic for changing ones.
- **Mintzberg's five configurations (1979):** simple structure; machine bureaucracy (e.g. FBR, NADRA); professional bureaucracy (hospitals, universities); divisionalised form; adhocracy (task forces).
- **Contingency theory:** Lawrence and Lorsch (1967) — the right structure depends on environment, technology, size.
- **Forms of public organisation in Pakistan:** ministries and divisions; attached departments; subordinate offices; autonomous bodies; statutory corporations; companies (Section 42 not-for-profit; SOEs); regulatory authorities; commissions.

## 12. Administrative Culture of Pakistan

**Meaning:** shared values, attitudes and practices of officials and their relations with citizens and politicians.

**Features:**
1. **Colonial, elitist and "viceregal"** (Braibanti; Kennedy): the officer as ruler, not servant.
2. **Generalist dominance** over specialists (PAS vs technical cadres).
3. **Status and hierarchy** — protocol, "grade consciousness".
4. **Personalism and *sifarish*** — favours and patronage outweigh rules.
5. **Risk aversion and file culture** — fear of NAB and audit leads to inaction ("policy paralysis").
6. **Secrecy** — Official Secrets Act 1923; weak RTI use.
7. **Rent-seeking and corruption.**
8. **Weak performance culture** — ACR/PER inflation; promotion by seniority.
9. **Politicisation** — loyalty to patrons; frequent transfers.
10. **Ethnic and regional quotas** seen by some as reducing merit, by others as ensuring representation.

**Causes:** colonial legacy, long military rule, low pay, weak accountability, social structure (biradari, feudal influence).
**Reform levers:** merit-based postings with fixed tenure; performance management; digitisation that removes discretion; training on service ethic; protection from political pressure; transparency.

## 13. Inter-governmental Relations (IGR) at Federal and Provincial Level

**Constitutional framework:**
- **Legislative lists:** Federal Legislative List Part I and Part II (Fourth Schedule); Concurrent List abolished by the 18th Amendment (2010); residual subjects go to provinces (Art. 142).
- **Council of Common Interests (CCI, Art. 153–154):** PM (chair), four CMs, three federal members; supervises Part II of the Federal List (railways, minerals, oil and gas, electricity, WAPDA, census); should meet at least once every 90 days; permanent secretariat. Disputes go to Parliament in joint sitting.
- **National Economic Council (NEC, Art. 156):** reviews the economy; approves plans.
- **National Finance Commission (NFC, Art. 160):** divides the divisible pool; 7th Award (2009/10) — provinces' share 56% in 2010–11, 57.5% from 2011–12; horizontal formula: population 82%, poverty/backwardness 10.3%, revenue collection/generation 5%, inverse population density 2.7%. Article 160(3A): provincial share cannot be reduced below the previous award.
- **Article 144:** Parliament can legislate on a provincial subject if provinces ask by resolution.
- **Articles 148–149:** federal directions to provinces in certain cases.
- **Article 158:** priority of the producing province for natural gas. **Article 161:** net hydel profits and gas royalties to provinces.
- **Emergency (Arts. 232–234):** in October 2026 the President proclaimed an emergency and governor's rule in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (Arts. 232 and 234), approved by a joint sitting of Parliament (see the Pakistan Affairs pack).
- **Inter-Provincial Coordination Division** supports CCI and IPCC meetings.
- **Indus River System Authority (IRSA, 1992)** applies the 1991 Water Apportionment Accord.
- **Federal Constitutional Court** (27th Amendment, November 2025) now hears disputes between governments **[verify the article]**.

**Issues:** CCI meets irregularly; federal ministries continue on devolved subjects (education, health — HEC, Ministry of National Health Services); fiscal imbalance (provinces get most revenue but collect little themselves; federation left with a deficit after debt service and defence); water disputes (canals dispute between Sindh and Punjab, 2025); census disputes (2017, 2023); policing and counter-terrorism coordination; the 2026 governor's rule in KP.

**Models of IGR (Deil Wright):** coordinate-authority, inclusive-authority, overlapping-authority (most realistic). Pakistan has moved from inclusive (centralised pre-2010) toward overlapping after the 18th Amendment.

**Way forward:** regular CCI meetings with a strong secretariat; provincial own-source revenue (agricultural income tax — now being enforced under IMF programme **[verify]**); a new NFC award linking transfers to service outcomes; IGR forums at sector level (health, education ministers' conferences); data sharing.

## 14. Integrating classical and contemporary theories (asked 2026)

- **Contrast:** classical = closed system, hierarchy, efficiency, one best way; contemporary = open system, networks, citizen focus, contingency, values.
- **Integration routes:**
  1. **Contingency theory** — use bureaucratic structure for routine, high-volume tasks (NADRA, passports) and organic/network structures for innovation and crises.
  2. **Neo-Weberian state** — keep merit, legality and hierarchy; add results, citizen voice, digital tools.
  3. **Systems theory** (Katz and Kahn 1966; Easton) — inputs, conversion, outputs, feedback; classical tools sit inside an open system.
  4. **Ambidextrous organisations** — separate units for exploitation (efficiency) and exploration (innovation).
  5. **Human relations + scientific management** — measure work but involve staff (TQM, lean).
- **Pakistan example:** NADRA uses strict standard procedures (Taylor/Weber) with customer feedback and digital channels (NPM/NPS); Punjab Safe Cities Authority links police hierarchy with tech partners (network).
