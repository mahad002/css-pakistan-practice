# I. Public Administration: Concepts, Approaches and Context

Most-asked section (15 questions 2016–2026). Learn the definitions, the dichotomy debate, the bureaucracy–democracy tension and the TPA → NPM → NPS → governance sequence by heart.

---

## 1. Definitions

| Thinker | Definition (paraphrased) | What it stresses |
|---|---|---|
| Woodrow Wilson, "The Study of Administration" (1887) | Detailed, systematic execution of public law; administration is "government in action" | Execution; separate from politics |
| Frank Goodnow, *Politics and Administration* (1900) | Politics expresses the will of the state; administration executes that will | Dichotomy |
| L.D. White (1926, first US textbook) | All operations aimed at fulfilling or enforcing public policy | Breadth; management |
| Luther Gulick (1937) | The part of the science of administration concerned with government, mainly the executive branch | POSDCORB functions |
| Dwight Waldo (1948/1955) | The organisation and management of people and resources to achieve the purposes of government | Values and politics |
| Nicholas Henry | A mix of theory and practice that promotes understanding of government's relationship with society and makes policy more responsive | Theory + practice |
| Rosenbloom | Use of managerial, political and legal theories to carry out legislative, executive and judicial mandates | Three lenses |

**Working definition for answers:** Public administration is the organisation, management and delivery of public policy and services by government and its partners, under law and political control, for the public interest.

**Two meanings.** (1) The *activity* — what officials do. (2) The *discipline* — the study of it. Write "Public Administration" (capitals) for the discipline.

**Integral vs managerial view.** Integral (L.D. White, Marshall Dimock): every activity of government staff, from peon to secretary. Managerial (Simon, Gulick): only the managerial work of those at the top.

## 2. Nature and scope

**Scope (POSDCORB, Gulick 1937):** Planning, Organising, Staffing, Directing, Coordinating, Reporting, Budgeting. Criticised (by Lewis Meriam and later Simon) as technique-only: it ignores the substance of services (health, police) and the politics of policy.

**Wider modern scope:**
1. Policy formulation, implementation and evaluation.
2. Organisation and management of public bodies.
3. Personnel / HRM.
4. Public finance and budgeting.
5. Administrative law and accountability.
6. Comparative and development administration.
7. Intergovernmental relations and local government.
8. Governance: networks, partnerships, regulation, digital government.

**Role in society:** maintain law and order; deliver services; regulate markets; redistribute (social protection, e.g. BISP); lead development; manage crises (floods 2022, 2025; COVID-19's NCOC); preserve continuity when governments change; give citizens a channel to the state.

**Is it a science or an art?** Science claims: Taylor, Gulick, Urwick ("principles"). Rejected by Robert Dahl (1947: values, human behaviour and culture prevent universal laws) and Simon (1946: the principles are "proverbs" that contradict each other). Balanced view: a social science with an art of practice.

**Interdisciplinary nature (asked 2026).** Links with:
- **Political science** — power, constitutions, legislatures (the parent discipline).
- **Economics** — budgets, public goods, market failure, public choice.
- **Law** — administrative and constitutional law; judicial review.
- **Sociology** — bureaucracy, groups, culture (Weber himself was a sociologist).
- **Psychology** — motivation, leadership, decision-making (Maslow, Simon).
- **Management/business** — NPM, strategic management, HRM.
- **History** — the colonial roots of Pakistan's administration.
- **Information technology** — e-government, data.

## 3. Public vs private administration

| Point | Public | Private |
|---|---|---|
| Goal | Public interest, many goals | Profit, clear bottom line |
| Accountability | Parliament, courts, auditors, media | Owners and market |
| Legal framework | Acts only within law (ultra vires) | May do anything not forbidden |
| Monopoly | Often (police, passports) | Competition |
| Equal treatment | Required (Art. 25) | Can discriminate commercially |
| Transparency | Public scrutiny, RTI | Commercial secrecy |
| Measurement | Hard to measure outcomes | Profit, market share |

Sayre's "law": public and private management are "fundamentally alike in all unimportant respects". Graham Allison (1980) used it to argue they differ in the things that matter.

**Publicness** (Bozeman, *All Organizations Are Public*, 1987): organisations are more or less public depending on how far political authority affects them — ownership, funding and control. A spectrum, not a binary.

## 4. Issues in theory and practice

### (a) Politics versus Administration (the dichotomy)

- **Wilson (1887):** administration lies outside politics; administrative questions are not political questions. Learn from Prussian and French administration without importing their politics. ("If I see a murderous fellow sharpening a knife cleverly, I can borrow his way of sharpening the knife without borrowing his probable intention" — safe to paraphrase, not quote word for word.)
- **Goodnow (1900):** will (politics) vs execution (administration).
- **Why it was attractive:** to end the US spoils system and protect a merit service.

**Attack on the dichotomy (1940s):**
- Paul Appleby, *Big Democracy* (1945) and *Policy and Administration* (1949): administration is policy-making; officials shape policy at every step.
- Dwight Waldo, *The Administrative State* (1948): administration carries values and political theory.
- Simon: the fact–value distinction replaces politics–administration.
- Svara (2001): "complementarity" — elected officials and administrators share the policy space; four zones: mission (mostly politicians), policy (shared), administration (mostly officials), management (officials).

**Pakistan:** the dichotomy breaks down in both directions. Politicians interfere in postings and transfers; senior bureaucrats and the military have shaped policy since the 1950s (Ghulam Muhammad, Iskander Mirza, Ayub). Courts have tried to protect tenure (the *Anita Turab* case, 2013, on transfers and postings) **[verify citation]**.

**2016 question link:** "easier to make a constitution than to run it" is Wilson's own line. The answer: running the constitution is administration; success depends on a competent, neutral, accountable service.

### (b) Democracy versus Bureaucracy (asked 2020 and 2022)

| Bureaucracy | Democracy |
|---|---|
| Hierarchy | Equality |
| Expertise, secrecy | Participation, openness |
| Appointed for career | Elected for a term |
| Rule-bound, impersonal | Responsive to voters |
| Permanence | Change of governments |

**Arguments that they conflict:** officials are unelected but hold power (Weber's fear of an "iron cage"; Michels' "iron law of oligarchy"); expertise gives them an information advantage over ministers; they can resist reform.

**Arguments that they need each other:** democracy needs a neutral, competent machine to deliver its promises; bureaucracy gives equal treatment under law (a democratic value); rules prevent favouritism. Weber saw bureaucracy as the most rational form of legal authority.

**Reconciling them:** political control (ministers, parliament), representative bureaucracy, citizen participation, transparency (RTI), judicial review, performance management.

**Pakistan:** the "viceregal" tradition (Kennedy, Braibanti) left a powerful, elitist service; long military rule weakened elected control; elected governments then politicised postings. Result: neither a neutral expert bureaucracy nor effective democratic control.

### (c) Efficiency versus Equity

- **Efficiency:** best ratio of output to input (Taylor, Gulick: efficiency is "axiom number one").
- **Equity:** fair treatment and fair outcomes, particularly for the disadvantaged (Frederickson, New Public Administration; Rawls).
- **Tension:** the cheapest school location may not serve the poorest village; user fees raise efficiency but exclude the poor.
- **Okun** (*Equality and Efficiency: The Big Tradeoff*, 1975): the "leaky bucket".
- **Pakistan examples:** BISP targeting (equity) vs administrative cost; NFC formula (82% population vs poverty weight); privatisation of SOEs (efficiency) vs job losses.

### (d) Other value tensions worth naming
Accountability vs discretion; centralisation vs decentralisation; neutrality vs responsiveness; continuity vs change.

## 5. Core values of public administration (asked 2025)

| Value | Meaning | Pakistan reference |
|---|---|---|
| Rule of law | Officials act only under law; equal application; challengeable in court | Art. 4 (right to be dealt with in accordance with law), Art. 25 |
| Efficiency | Value for money; timeliness | Performance agreements; Rightsizing 2024–25 |
| Effectiveness | Achieving intended outcomes | Outcome-based budgeting |
| Equity and fairness | Equal treatment, attention to disadvantaged | Quotas for regions, women, minorities |
| Responsiveness | Acting on citizens' needs and complaints | Pakistan Citizen's Portal (2018) |
| Accountability | Answering for actions | AGP, PAC, NAB, ombudsman |
| Transparency | Open information | Art. 19A; RTI Act 2017 |
| Integrity/ethics | Honesty, no conflict of interest | Civil Servants Conduct Rules 2026 |
| Neutrality | Serve any lawful government impartially | Conduct Rules ban political activity |

## 6. Approaches / paradigms

### (a) Traditional Public Administration (TPA), c. 1880s–1970s
**Pillars:** Wilsonian dichotomy; Weberian bureaucracy; Taylor's scientific management; "principles" (Fayol, Gulick, Urwick).
**Features:** hierarchy; rules; input control; career civil service; neutral experts; one best way; public interest defined by politicians.
**Criticisms:** rigid, slow, rule-bound ("red tape"); inward-looking; ignored citizens; costly; produced the fiscal crises of the 1970s.

**Phases of the discipline (Nicholas Henry's five paradigms):**
1. Politics/administration dichotomy (1900–1926).
2. Principles of administration (1927–1937).
3. PA as political science (1950–1970).
4. PA as management (1956–1970).
5. PA as public administration (1970– ).

**New Public Administration (Minnowbrook, 1968):** younger scholars (Frank Marini, H. George Frederickson) at Syracuse; stressed relevance, values, **social equity**, change, client focus. Response to the turmoil of 1960s America. (Asked 2024 as "classical vs New PA".)

### (b) New Public Management (NPM), 1980s–
**Origins:** Thatcher (UK, Next Steps agencies 1988), Reagan (US), New Zealand reforms (1984–); public choice and agency theory; fiscal stress.
**Christopher Hood (1991), seven doctrines:** hands-on professional management; explicit standards and performance measures; output controls; disaggregation of units; competition; private-sector management styles; discipline and parsimony in resource use.
**Osborne and Gaebler, *Reinventing Government* (1992), ten principles:** catalytic ("steer rather than row"); community-owned; competitive; mission-driven; results-oriented; customer-driven; enterprising; anticipatory; decentralised; market-oriented.
**Tools:** contracting out, privatisation, agencies, performance pay, user charges, citizen's charters, TQM.
**Criticisms:** citizens are not customers; fragmentation and loss of coordination; accountability blurred by contracts; cost-cutting hurts equity; transfers private values (profit) into public life; assumes strong institutions to write and monitor contracts — weak in developing countries (Schick, "Why most developing countries should not try New Zealand's reforms", 1998).

**NPM in Pakistan:** autonomous bodies and companies (e.g. Punjab's public sector companies — later criticised by audit for cost and weak oversight); performance agreements with ministries (2019 onward); privatisation (PTCL 2005–06, PIA December 2025); citizen feedback systems (Punjab's "Citizen Feedback Monitoring Program", 2014 **[verify date]**). Verdict: partial and selective; core civil service unchanged.

### (c) New Public Service (NPS)
**Janet and Robert Denhardt** (article 2000; book *The New Public Service: Serving, Not Steering*, 2003). Seven principles:
1. Serve citizens, not customers.
2. Seek the public interest.
3. Value citizenship over entrepreneurship.
4. Think strategically, act democratically.
5. Recognise that accountability is not simple.
6. Serve rather than steer.
7. Value people, not just productivity.
**Roots:** democratic citizenship theory, communitarianism, organisational humanism, postmodern PA.

### (d) Governance approach / New Public Governance
- **Governance** = the process of governing through networks of state, market and civil society actors, not only through government hierarchy. R.A.W. Rhodes (1996): "governing without government".
- **World Bank (1992), *Governance and Development*:** "the manner in which power is exercised in the management of a country's economic and social resources for development".
- **UNDP good governance characteristics:** participation, rule of law, transparency, responsiveness, consensus orientation, equity, effectiveness and efficiency, accountability, strategic vision.
- **World Governance Indicators (World Bank):** voice and accountability; political stability; government effectiveness; regulatory quality; rule of law; control of corruption.
- **New Public Governance** (Stephen Osborne, 2006, 2010): a plural state; inter-organisational relationships; trust and relational contracts; co-production.

**Comparison table (memorise):**

| | TPA | NPM | NPS / NPG |
|---|---|---|---|
| Theory base | Political theory, Weber | Economics, public choice | Democratic theory, networks |
| Citizen as | Client/subject | Customer | Citizen/co-producer |
| Role of government | Rowing | Steering | Serving / brokering |
| Mechanism | Hierarchy | Markets, contracts | Networks, partnerships |
| Accountability | Hierarchical, to politicians | To customers, results | Multiple: law, community, values |
| Key value | Rules, equity of process | Efficiency, results | Public interest, participation |
| Main critique | Rigid | Fragmenting, inequitable | Vague, slow |

### (e) Normative vs empirical approaches (asked 2021)
- **Normative:** what administration *ought* to be — values, ethics, justice (Waldo, Frederickson, Rawls).
- **Empirical:** what administration *is* — observation, data, testing (Simon, behaviouralism).
- **Implication for Pakistan:** normative debates (merit, neutrality) are rich; empirical evidence (evaluations, data) is thin, so reforms are designed without knowing what works.

### (f) Public interest (asked 2019)
- Aggregative (utilitarian) view: sum of private interests.
- Unitary/normative view: a common good above individuals.
- Procedural view: whatever emerges from fair process.
- **Rawls, *A Theory of Justice* (1971):** justice as fairness; the "veil of ignorance"; two principles — equal basic liberties; inequalities allowed only if they benefit the least advantaged (difference principle) and offices are open to all under fair equality of opportunity. So the public interest is *not* simply a sum; it gives priority to the worst-off.

### (g) Market failure (asked 2022)
Four classical types: **public goods** (non-rival, non-excludable — defence, street lighting); **externalities** (pollution, smog in Lahore); **natural monopoly** (electricity grids, water pipes); **information asymmetry** (medicines, banking). Plus incomplete markets and inequality. Each justifies a state role: provision, tax/regulation, regulators (NEPRA, OGRA), disclosure (DRAP, SECP). Counter-idea: **government failure** (public choice).

## 7. Islamic concept of public administration

Never asked 2016–26 as a full question; keep one short note ready.

**Principles:**
- Sovereignty belongs to Allah; rulers hold authority as a trust (*amanah*) (Qur'an 4:58: give trusts to those entitled and judge with justice).
- **Shura** (consultation): Qur'an 42:38 and 3:159.
- **Adl** (justice) and equality before law.
- **Accountability (*muhasabah*)**: the ruler is answerable to God and people; the Caliph Umar was questioned publicly about his garment.
- **Merit**: appoint the most capable and trustworthy (the story of Musa: "the strong and trustworthy", Qur'an 28:26).
- Welfare of the people; protection of non-Muslims (dhimmis).

**Institutions under Hazrat Umar (634–644):** *diwan* (registers and stipends); provinces under governors (*wali*) with separate judges (*qadi*) — an early separation of judicial and executive functions; *bait-ul-mal* (treasury); *hisbah* (market inspection, later the *muhtasib*); census and land survey; public complaints heard at Hajj; asset declarations of governors on appointment.

**Hazrat Ali's letter to Malik al-Ashtar** (governor of Egypt): treat people with mercy; choose officials by merit and test them; pay them well so they are not tempted to corruption; supervise them through secret inspectors; care for the poor.

**Later thinkers:** al-Mawardi (*al-Ahkam al-Sultaniyya*, 11th c.) on duties of rulers and offices; Nizam al-Mulk (*Siyasatnama*); Ibn Khaldun (*Muqaddimah*) on the cycle of state and the role of *asabiyyah*.

**Pakistan:** the institution of Wafaqi Mohtasib (1983) takes its name from the *muhtasib*. Objectives Resolution (Art. 2A) sets the frame of authority as a trust.

## 8. Historical roots of public administration in Pakistan

| Period | Feature | Legacy |
|---|---|---|
| Mauryan (Kautilya's *Arthashastra*, c. 300 BCE) | Elaborate bureaucracy, spies, revenue | Ancient tradition of a strong revenue state |
| Sultanate and Mughal (1206–1857) | Mansabdari system (Akbar), ranked officials; revenue system of Todar Mal; subah–sarkar–pargana units; qazi courts | District (sarkar) and tehsil (pargana) structure; land revenue records |
| East India Company (1757–1858) | Collector-magistrate; covenanted civil service; Cornwallis code (1793) | Fusion of revenue, magistracy and police control in one district officer |
| British Crown (1858–1947) | Indian Civil Service (ICS); open competition from 1855 (after the Northcote–Trevelyan logic and the Macaulay Report 1854); Police Act 1861; CrPC 1898; Punjab "non-regulation" system | The deputy commissioner, steel frame, elitism, "viceregal" style |
| Pakistan (1947– ) | Civil Service of Pakistan (CSP) from ICS; Police Service of Pakistan; later 1973 reforms | See file 08 |

**Key colonial features carried into Pakistan:** a generalist elite cadre; centralised control; the district as the main unit; rule-based but distant from citizens; colonial laws still in use (Police Act 1861 in some areas, Penal Code 1860, Land Revenue Act 1967 builds on older law).

## 9. Ready-made answer outlines

**Q. Is the politics–administration dichotomy dead?**
Intro (Wilson, Goodnow) → reasons for the dichotomy → attack (Appleby, Waldo, Simon) → modern position (Svara's complementarity; NPM's "let managers manage" partly revives it) → Pakistan (politicisation of postings; bureaucratic/military dominance) → way forward (fixed tenure, independent boards for postings, clear roles in Rules of Business) → conclusion: dead as a description, alive as a normative ideal of neutrality.

**Q. NPM in developing countries.**
Define NPM (Hood) → why adopted (fiscal crisis, donors) → evidence (NZ, UK success; mixed elsewhere) → preconditions missing (Schick 1998: informal economies, weak contract enforcement, weak rule-based bureaucracy) → Pakistan cases (companies in Punjab, PIA, performance agreements) → verdict: selective adoption after basic Weberian reforms ("Weberianism first").

## 10. Criticisms to quote across the section
- Simon: principles are "proverbs".
- Dahl: no science without comparative study, values and behaviour.
- Waldo: the "administrative state" is political.
- Schick: developing countries should get the basics right before NPM.
