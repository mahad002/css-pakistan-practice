## Answer 1. "Bureaucracy and democracy are antithetical." Discuss with reference to the expectations of citizens and the civic culture in Pakistan. *(CSS 2020, 2022)*

**Outline**
1. Introduction and thesis
2. Why they seem antithetical: the theory
3. Why they need each other
4. Pakistan: high expectations, weak civic culture
5. How the tension shows in Pakistan
6. Reconciling bureaucracy and democracy
7. Conclusion

**Introduction**
Democracy rests on equality, participation and the will of the majority. Bureaucracy rests on hierarchy, expertise and rules. Max Weber admired bureaucracy as the most rational form of legal authority, yet feared an "iron cage" in which unelected officials would dominate elected leaders. The claim that the two are antithetical captures a real tension. But it is only half true. Democracy without a capable bureaucracy cannot deliver its promises, and bureaucracy without democratic control becomes arbitrary. **In Pakistan the problem is not that bureaucracy and democracy are opposites, but that the country has had neither a neutral, competent bureaucracy nor effective democratic control over it.**

**1. Why they seem antithetical**
- *Hierarchy versus equality.* Bureaucracy is a pyramid of command. Democracy treats every citizen as equal.
- *Expertise versus participation.* Officials hold specialised knowledge and files. Citizens and even ministers depend on them. Robert Michels' "iron law of oligarchy" warned that every large organisation ends up ruled by a few.
- *Permanence versus accountability.* Officials stay for 30 years. Politicians face voters every five years. Permanent officials can wait out reformers.
- *Secrecy versus openness.* Weber noted that bureaucracies guard information as "official secrets" to protect their power.
- *Generalised rules versus particular needs.* Rules treat all cases alike; democratic politics responds to specific groups.

The statement's description — bureaucracy "elitist, specialised and informed", democracy "communal, pluralist and ill-informed" — fits this classical view.

**2. Why they need each other**
- *Delivery.* Elected governments make promises; only an administrative machine can build schools, collect taxes and run hospitals. Woodrow Wilson's point in 1887 was that it is harder to run a constitution than to frame one.
- *Equal treatment.* Impersonal rules are themselves democratic. A merit-based official who treats rich and poor alike serves the democratic value of equality before law (Article 25).
- *Continuity.* Democracies change governments; the bureaucracy provides memory and stability.
- *Protection against spoils.* The US Pendleton Act (1883) and the UK Northcote–Trevelyan Report (1854) created merit services precisely to protect democracy from patronage.
- *Information for public debate.* Expert analysis improves democratic choices.

So the relationship is better described as a **creative tension**: democracy supplies legitimacy and direction; bureaucracy supplies competence and continuity.

**3. Pakistan: high expectations, weak civic culture**
Citizens' expectations are high. A young population of more than 240 million (2023 census) expects jobs, electricity, schools and justice. Expectations are shaped by social media and by service standards seen abroad.

The civic culture is weak. Almond and Verba distinguished participant, subject and parochial cultures. Pakistan's culture is mixed: participant in cities, but subject and parochial in much of the countryside, where *biradari*, landlords and tribal elders mediate between citizen and state. Citizens approach officials through *sifarish* rather than rights. Trust in institutions is low. In such a culture neither democratic control nor bureaucratic rule-following works as theory expects.

**4. How the tension shows in Pakistan**
- *Bureaucratic dominance.* In the 1950s civil servants became heads of state (Ghulam Muhammad, Iskander Mirza). Hamza Alavi called Pakistan an "overdeveloped state" in which a military-bureaucratic oligarchy dominated weak political parties.
- *Politicisation after 1973.* Bhutto's reforms removed constitutional guarantees for civil servants. Since then elected governments have used postings and transfers as patronage. Deputy commissioners and police officers often serve less than a year.
- *Military rule.* Long periods of military government weakened both democratic institutions and an independent civil service.
- *Risk aversion.* Fear of NAB, audit and courts makes officials avoid decisions, so citizens see a slow, unresponsive state.
- *Missing local democracy.* Punjab has had no elected local government for most of the time since 2019, so citizens meet the state mainly through bureaucrats.

The result is a lose–lose: elected leaders cannot get delivery, and officials cannot act on professional judgement.

**5. Critical analysis**
The statement exaggerates. "Democracy is ill-informed" is an elitist claim; citizens often understand local needs better than officials. And bureaucracy is not always "informed": Pakistan's generalist officers frequently lack technical knowledge. Modern theory (Svara's "complementarity") holds that politicians and officials share the policy space: politicians lead on mission and policy; officials lead on administration and management. The real question is how to design that partnership.

**6. Reconciling bureaucracy and democracy**
1. *Fixed tenure and rules-based postings*, with reasons recorded, as the Supreme Court has urged.
2. *Independent selection boards* for senior posts. The September 2026 reform proposal for a National Executive Service should place selection with an independent body, not ministers alone.
3. *Representative bureaucracy* — regional and gender quotas give legitimacy; merit within quotas keeps quality.
4. *Transparency* — Article 19A and the Right of Access to Information Act 2017; publication of decisions.
5. *Citizen engagement* — Pakistan Citizen's Portal, participatory budgeting, social audits.
6. *Elected local governments* under Article 140A, so that service delivery is democratically accountable close to citizens.
7. *Performance management* that rewards results, not loyalty.
8. *Ethics* — enforcement of the Civil Servants Conduct Rules 2026, which bar political activity and require asset declarations.

**International comparison**
Other democracies have managed the tension in different ways. The UK keeps a permanent, politically neutral civil service under ministers who answer to Parliament; officials advise privately and implement loyally, and a Civil Service Commission protects merit. The United States places about 4,000 political appointees on top of a merit service, gaining responsiveness at the cost of some continuity. India's IAS, which shares Pakistan's colonial roots, has kept a stronger constitutional footing (Article 311 protections) and, after the Supreme Court's *Prakash Singh* judgment (2006) on police, a push for fixed tenures. The lesson for Pakistan is that neutrality and democratic control can coexist when the rules on appointments, tenure and removal are clear and respected by all parties.

**Conclusion**
Bureaucracy and democracy pull in different directions, but each needs the other. Weber's warning and Wilson's hope are both right. Pakistan's difficulty is a mismatch between high citizen expectations and a weak civic culture, made worse by a politicised service and interrupted democracy. The answer is not to choose one over the other but to build a professional, neutral bureaucracy under democratic, rules-based control, with citizens able to hold both to account.

*(about 1,090 words including outline)*

## Answer 2. Differentiate between development administration and administrative development. Critically analyse the role of bureaucracy in development administration in emerging economies like Pakistan. *(CSS 2019, 2026)*

**Outline**
1. Introduction and thesis
2. Development administration: meaning and features
3. Administrative development: meaning
4. The difference (table) and the link
5. Role of bureaucracy in Pakistan's development: positive record
6. Negative record and constraints
7. Critical assessment
8. Way forward
9. Conclusion

**Introduction**
After the Second World War newly independent states expected their bureaucracies to lead rapid development. The Comparative Administration Group, led by Fred Riggs, coined and developed the idea of "development administration". Riggs also warned that development needs a developed administration — a "chicken and egg" problem. **Development administration is administration of development; administrative development is development of administration. In Pakistan the bureaucracy has delivered development where it was itself capable and insulated, and failed where it lacked capacity, accountability and political protection.**

**1. Development administration**
Edward Weidner (1962) defined it as guiding an organisation towards progressive political, economic and social objectives. The term is credited to U.L. Goswami (1955). Its features:
- goal orientation — growth, welfare and social change;
- change orientation rather than maintenance of order;
- innovation and flexibility;
- citizen orientation and participation;
- commitment of officials to development goals;
- awareness of the environment (Riggs' ecological approach).

Unlike traditional "law and order and revenue" administration inherited from the colonial state, development administration is outward-looking and dynamic.

**2. Administrative development**
Administrative development means building the capacity of the administrative system itself: merit recruitment, training, rational structures, sound financial management, accountability, and values such as integrity. Civil service reform, budgeting reform and e-government are examples.

**3. The difference**

| | Development administration | Administrative development |
|---|---|---|
| Object | Society and economy | The administrative system |
| Aim | Growth, welfare, change | Capacity, efficiency, integrity |
| Direction | Outward | Inward |
| Examples | Green Revolution, motorways, BISP | Civil service reform, PFM Act 2019, e-Office |
| Measure | GDP, HDI, poverty | Government effectiveness, service quality |

The two depend on each other. A weak administration cannot deliver development. Development tasks, in turn, build administrative skills. Riggs added a warning: if bureaucracy grows faster than political institutions, it will dominate politics. Pakistan's early history illustrates this.

**4. Bureaucracy and development in Pakistan: the positive record**
- *State-building, 1947–58.* With few officers and no capital, the bureaucracy settled millions of refugees and created a federal government.
- *The 1960s.* Planning Commission and the Second Five-Year Plan (1960–65) produced high growth; Mangla and Tarbela dams were built under the Indus Waters Treaty works.
- *Modern successes.* NADRA built one of the largest identity databases; BISP (2008) uses data-driven targeting to reach poor women; Rescue 1122 transformed emergency services; the polio programme improved coverage; disaster agencies responded to the 2005 earthquake (ERRA) and the 2022 floods.

**5. The negative record**
- *Elitism and colonial culture.* The district officer was a ruler, not a servant. Ralph Braibanti and Charles Kennedy describe a "viceregal" tradition.
- *Alliance with authoritarian rule.* Alavi's "military-bureaucratic oligarchy" governed in the 1950s–60s; development gains were unequal (the "22 families").
- *Generalist dominance.* Generalist officers head technical ministries, slowing complex projects.
- *Politicisation and short tenures.* Officers move before projects mature.
- *Corruption and rent-seeking.* Licences, land and procurement offer rents; Pakistan scored 28/100 in Transparency International's CPI 2025.
- *Resistance to devolution.* Bureaucracy has often preferred deconcentration to devolution and has run districts in the absence of elected local governments.
- *Implementation gap.* Policies are written well but delivered poorly — out-of-school children and an HDI rank of 168 of 193 (UNDP 2025) show the outcome.

**6. Constraints the bureaucracy faces**
Political instability; military interventions; fiscal crisis that cuts development budgets; low pay; legal and audit fear; weak data; security threats in KP and Balochistan.

**7. Critical assessment**
The record supports Riggs' thesis. Where administrative development came first — NADRA's technical staff, SBP's professional cadre, BISP's data systems — development administration succeeded. Where the administration remained formalistic and politicised, development failed. Riggs' "prismatic" features — formalism (rules on paper), overlapping (kinship deciding appointments) and heterogeneity — explain why imported development models did not work. The lesson for emerging economies is that East Asian developmental states (Japan, South Korea) had meritocratic bureaucracies with "embedded autonomy" (Peter Evans): close to business, but not captured by it.

**8. Way forward**
1. *Administrative development first*: implement specialised cadres and performance management proposed in the September 2026 reform package, with legal backing and independent selection.
2. *Fixed tenure* for officers leading development projects.
3. *Devolution* to elected local governments under Article 140A, with capacity building.
4. *Evidence-based planning*: link PSDP to evaluation; publish PC-IV completion reports.
5. *Digital governance* to cut discretion and corruption.
6. *Partnerships* with private sector and civil society (development management), with strong contract oversight.
7. *Accountability that is fair* — protect good-faith decisions while punishing corruption.

**Lessons from East Asia**
South Korea and Malaysia show how administrative development can precede and drive development administration. South Korea created an Economic Planning Board in 1961, recruited elite officials through highly competitive examinations, protected them from day-to-day politics and held them to export and growth targets — Peter Evans' "embedded autonomy". Malaysia's Performance Management and Delivery Unit (PEMANDU, 2009) tracked a few national priorities with public targets. Pakistan already has some of the building blocks: a competitive CSS examination, a Planning Commission with a long history, and delivery-unit experience from Punjab's education roadmap (2011–18). What it lacks is continuity of policy, protection of officials who perform, and specialisation — which is why the 2026 proposals for specialised cadres and performance contracts matter for development, not only for the civil service.

**Conclusion**
Development administration and administrative development are two sides of one coin. Pakistan's bureaucracy has proved it can deliver when it is capable and insulated, as NADRA and BISP show. Its failures come from a colonial, politicised and generalist structure that was never fully reformed. For Pakistan to develop, it must first develop its administration — and then let that administration serve citizens rather than rule them.

*(about 1,030 words including outline)*

## Answer 3. Discuss the federal budget process in Pakistan, with attention to its ability to fund government activity and help regulate the economy's business cycle. Are you satisfied with it? *(CSS 2016, 2019, 2022)*

**Outline**
1. Introduction and thesis
2. Constitutional and legal framework
3. Stages of the budget process
4. Ability to fund government
5. Ability to stabilise the business cycle
6. Weaknesses
7. Reforms so far and way forward
8. Conclusion

**Introduction**
Aaron Wildavsky called the budget a series of goals with price tags attached. It is the government's most important policy document. Pakistan's federal budget, presented each June for the year starting 1 July, follows a constitutional process modelled on Westminster. **The process is formally sound and has improved since the Public Finance Management Act 2019, but it neither raises enough revenue to fund the state nor acts counter-cyclically; it is pro-cyclical, IMF-driven and weakly scrutinised. I am therefore only partly satisfied.**

**1. Framework**
- **Constitution:** Article 78 (Federal Consolidated Fund and Public Account); Article 80 (Annual Budget Statement); Article 81 (charged expenditure — debt servicing, salaries of judges and constitutional office-holders — which may be discussed but not voted); Article 82 (demands for grants, which the National Assembly may approve, reduce or refuse but not increase); Article 73 (Finance Bill as a money bill; the Senate makes recommendations within 14 days); Article 84 (supplementary and excess grants); Article 160 (National Finance Commission).
- **Laws:** Public Finance Management Act 2019; Fiscal Responsibility and Debt Limitation Act 2005.
- **Institutions:** Finance Division, FBR, Planning Commission, NEC, Auditor-General (Article 168), Public Accounts Committee.

**2. Stages**
*Preparation.* The Finance Division issues a Budget Call Circular in the autumn. A Budget Strategy Paper sets the macroeconomic framework and indicative ceilings and goes to the cabinet in the spring. Ministries prepare three-year estimates under the Medium-Term Budgetary Framework, with output targets. The Planning Commission prepares the Public Sector Development Programme (PSDP), approved by the Annual Plan Coordination Committee and the National Economic Council. FBR prepares tax proposals. In IMF programme years, the budget is negotiated with the Fund.

*Presentation.* The Economic Survey is released, then the Finance Minister presents the budget speech, the Finance Bill and the Annual Budget Statement in early June.

*Approval.* General debate; Senate recommendations; voting on demands for grants and cut motions; passage of the Finance Bill before 30 June.

*Execution.* The Finance Division releases funds; AGPR makes payments; supplementary grants authorise extra spending.

*Audit.* The Auditor-General audits accounts and reports to the President, who has them laid before the National Assembly; the PAC examines them.

**3. Ability to fund government**
- *Revenue is too low.* Pakistan's tax-to-GDP ratio has hovered around 10–11% for FBR taxes, among the lowest in the region. The base is narrow: agriculture, retail and real estate are under-taxed; withholding and indirect taxes carry the load; exemptions are large.
- *Interest payments dominate.* Debt servicing takes the largest single share of federal current spending, followed by defence, pensions and subsidies (including circular-debt payments in the power sector).
- *The NFC squeeze.* Under the 7th NFC Award provinces receive 57.5% of the divisible pool. The federation, left with large fixed obligations, borrows to close the gap.
- *Development is the residual.* The PSDP is cut mid-year when revenue falls short, so projects stall and costs rise.

So the budget funds salaries, interest and defence, but not development or adequate services.

**4. Ability to regulate the business cycle**
Musgrave assigned budgets three functions: allocation, distribution and stabilisation. For stabilisation, governments should spend more and tax less in downturns and save in booms. Pakistan does the opposite:
- In booms (2004–07, 2016–17, 2021–22) spending and imports rise, deficits widen.
- In crises (2008, 2018–19, 2022–23) IMF programmes require spending cuts and new taxes, deepening the slowdown.
- Automatic stabilisers (unemployment insurance, progressive income tax) are weak.
- Monetary policy and the IMF, not the budget, end up managing the cycle.

**5. Weaknesses of the process**
1. Unrealistic revenue targets and frequent "mini-budgets".
2. Weak parliamentary scrutiny: about two to three weeks of debate, few amendments, no independent parliamentary budget office, standing committees not involved in pre-budget review.
3. Large supplementary grants that act as a parallel budget.
4. Split between current and development budgets; the recurrent cost of new projects ignored.
5. Off-budget liabilities: circular debt, losses of SOEs, guarantees.
6. Poor transparency of tax expenditures.
7. Output-based budgeting exists on paper, but allocations are still incremental and line-item.
8. Little citizen participation.

**6. Reforms so far**
The PFM Act 2019 introduced the Budget Strategy Paper, cash management through a Treasury Single Account and limits on supplementary grants. Output-based budgeting and the MTBF have added performance information. Recent measures address the expenditure side: rightsizing of federal bodies (2024–25), a contributory pension scheme for employees appointed from 1 July 2024, and privatisation of PIA (December 2025).

**7. Way forward**
1. Broaden the tax base: provincial agricultural income tax enforcement, property tax on market values, digitised retail taxation, removal of exemptions.
2. A medium-term fiscal framework with binding expenditure ceilings and an independent fiscal council to assess forecasts.
3. A Parliamentary Budget Office and pre-budget hearings by standing committees.
4. Counter-cyclical rules: a stabilisation fund built up in good years.
5. Unify current and development budgets in programme budgets.
6. Spending reviews (a zero-based approach to old programmes).
7. Publish a tax-expenditure statement and SOE fiscal risk statement.
8. Link the next NFC award to service outcomes and provincial revenue effort.

**The provincial dimension**
The federal budget cannot be judged alone. After the 7th NFC Award, provinces receive most of the divisible pool, and under recent IMF programmes they are asked to run cash surpluses that help meet the national fiscal target. Provincial budgets follow the same constitutional pattern (Articles 120–124) and face similar problems: optimistic revenue estimates, large development programmes cut mid-year, and little own-source revenue from agriculture and property. Provincial Finance Commission awards to local governments are irregular. A national fiscal framework agreed in the CCI or NEC, with clear sharing of responsibilities for debt, development and social spending, would make both levels of budgeting more credible.

**Conclusion**
Pakistan's budget process is constitutionally clear and has modern tools on paper. In practice it struggles to raise revenue, is crowded out by interest and fixed costs, and amplifies rather than smooths the business cycle. I am satisfied with the legal framework, not with the outcomes. Real improvement depends on broadening the tax base, stronger parliamentary scrutiny and fiscal rules that bind governments in good times as well as bad.

*(about 1,100 words including outline)*

## Answer 4. Compare and contrast the bureaucratic model with New Public Management. Is NPM relevant to developing countries like Pakistan? *(CSS 2020; similar 2023, 2025)*

**Outline**
1. Introduction and thesis
2. The bureaucratic model
3. New Public Management
4. Comparison table
5. NPM's relevance to developing countries: arguments for
6. Arguments against
7. Pakistan's experience: indicators
8. Way forward: neo-Weberian sequencing
9. Conclusion

**Introduction**
For most of the twentieth century public administration followed Weber's bureaucratic model. From the 1980s, Thatcher's Britain, New Zealand and Reagan's America led a shift to New Public Management, which borrowed tools from business. Donors then spread NPM to developing countries. **NPM is relevant to Pakistan only selectively: its tools work where a basic Weberian bureaucracy already exists, and fail where merit, rule of law and contract enforcement are weak. Pakistan needs "Weber first, then NPM".**

**1. The bureaucratic model**
Weber's ideal type has fixed jurisdictions, hierarchy, written files, specialised training, full-time careers with salaries and pensions, appointment on merit and impersonal application of rules. Its strengths are predictability, equal treatment, continuity and control of corruption. Its weaknesses, identified by Merton and others, are rigidity, goal displacement ("red tape") and slow response.

**2. New Public Management**
Christopher Hood (1991) listed seven doctrines: hands-on professional management; explicit standards and performance measures; emphasis on output controls; disaggregation into agencies; competition; private-sector management styles; and parsimony in resource use. Osborne and Gaebler's *Reinventing Government* (1992) asked governments to "steer rather than row" and to be mission-driven, results-oriented and customer-driven. Theoretical roots lie in public choice and principal–agent theory.

**3. Comparison**

| Feature | Bureaucratic model | NPM |
|---|---|---|
| Theory base | Sociology, legal-rational authority | Economics, public choice |
| Control | Inputs and procedures | Outputs and results |
| Structure | Unified hierarchy | Agencies, contracts, decentralisation |
| Staff | Career service, seniority | Contracts, performance pay |
| Citizen as | Subject/client | Customer |
| Service delivery | Direct by state | Mixed: contracting, PPPs, privatisation |
| Accountability | Hierarchical, legal | Managerial, to results and customers |
| Key value | Equity of process, legality | Efficiency, effectiveness |
| Weakness | Rigid, slow | Fragmentation, equity loss, weak accountability |

Both seek efficiency; both are top-down in their own way. NPM keeps hierarchy in contracts but replaces rules with targets.

**4. Arguments that NPM is relevant to developing countries**
- Fiscal crisis demands value for money.
- Loss-making state enterprises need commercial discipline or privatisation.
- Citizens want faster, customer-friendly services.
- Results-based management helps donors and governments track outcomes.
- Some success stories: telecom liberalisation in Pakistan produced cheap mobile services; one-stop shops and e-services cut delays.

**5. Arguments against**
- *Missing preconditions.* Allen Schick (1998) argued that developing countries should not try New Zealand's reforms: NPM assumes a functioning rule-based bureaucracy, enforceable contracts and reliable data. Where informal practices dominate, giving managers freedom increases corruption.
- *Capture and rent-seeking.* Contracting out in weak systems turns into patronage.
- *Equity.* User fees and market logic exclude the poor.
- *Fragmentation.* New agencies and companies weaken coordination.
- *Accountability gaps.* Contracts blur who is responsible.
- *Imported models.* Andrews, Pritchett and Woolcock call this "isomorphic mimicry": adopting the form of reforms without the function.

**6. Pakistan's experience — key indicators**
- *Agencies and companies.* Punjab created many public-sector companies in 2010–18 to bypass rigid departments; audits and courts later questioned their costs and accountability.
- *Performance agreements.* Federal ministries signed performance agreements (from 2019); impact on outcomes is unclear.
- *Privatisation.* Banks and PTCL were sold earlier; PIA was sold in December 2025 for Rs135bn for a 75% stake — a test of NPM-style restructuring.
- *Citizen feedback.* The Pakistan Citizen's Portal (2018) and Punjab's proactive feedback calls are NPM-style customer tools that have worked reasonably well.
- *Digital services.* NADRA, FBR's e-filing and e-Khidmat centres combine Weberian standardisation with customer focus.
- *Weak preconditions.* CPI 2025 score of 28/100, politicised postings, weak contract enforcement and slow courts mean core Weberian features are still missing.
- *Recent reforms.* The September 2026 civil service package proposes performance-based promotion, a 5% incentive pool, twice-yearly assessments and contract-based senior posts (National Executive Service) — classic NPM tools that will succeed only with merit and independent oversight.

**7. Critical analysis**
Pakistan has adopted NPM "à la carte" — the parts that suit donors and politicians — without fixing the bureaucratic core. Islands of excellence show that hybrid models work: standardised, rule-based back offices (Weber) with customer-focused front ends (NPM). The neo-Weberian state (Pollitt and Bouckaert) offers a better description of what Pakistan needs: keep the state's legal and merit base, add results orientation and citizen voice.

**8. Way forward**
1. Fix Weberian basics: merit recruitment and postings, fixed tenure, predictable rules.
2. Introduce NPM tools selectively — in commercial SOEs, regulatory agencies, and transactional services.
3. Build contract-management and regulatory capacity before outsourcing.
4. Protect equity: targeted subsidies, no user fees for basic services.
5. Use digital tools to standardise and reduce discretion.
6. Evaluate each reform independently and scale what works.

**An illustrative case: Punjab's public sector companies**
Between roughly 2010 and 2018 the Punjab government set up dozens of public sector companies for waste management, transport, energy and other services. The idea was classic NPM: free managers from rigid departmental rules, pay market salaries and focus on results. Some delivered visible services, such as solid-waste management in Lahore through contracts with foreign firms. But audit reports and court proceedings later questioned high salaries, weak boards, procurement practices and overlapping mandates with elected local bodies and departments. The experience shows NPM's promise and its preconditions: autonomy works only when boards are independent, contracts are well written, performance is measured and accountability is enforced.

**Conclusion**
The bureaucratic model and NPM answer different problems: the first prevents arbitrariness, the second fights inefficiency. Developing countries need both, in sequence. For Pakistan, NPM is relevant as a toolkit, not as a paradigm. Without a merit-based, rule-following bureaucracy, NPM's freedom for managers becomes freedom for patronage.

## Answer 5. Discuss the factors that make public policy implementation fail in a country like Pakistan, and suggest how implementation can be improved. *(CSS 2018; part of 2016 and 2025)*

**Outline**
1. Introduction and thesis
2. Implementation in theory: top-down and bottom-up
3. Conditions for successful implementation
4. Factors fatal to implementation in Pakistan
5. Case examples
6. Critical analysis
7. How to improve implementation
8. Conclusion

**Introduction**
Pressman and Wildavsky's study *Implementation* (1973) showed how a well-funded programme in Oakland failed because too many agencies had to agree at too many decision points. Implementation is where policy meets reality. Pakistan is known for good policies on paper — education policies, national action plans, power-sector strategies — but weak delivery. **Implementation fails in Pakistan less because of bad design than because of political instability, weak capacity, poor coordination and lack of accountability for results.**

**1. Implementation in theory**
- *Top-down approach* (Van Meter and Van Horn; Mazmanian and Sabatier): success depends on clear objectives, a valid causal theory, adequate resources, committed implementing officials and supportive groups.
- *Bottom-up approach* (Michael Lipsky, *Street-Level Bureaucracy*, 1980): teachers, police and health workers effectively make policy through discretion under heavy workloads.
- *Synthesis:* Matland's ambiguity–conflict model — policies with low ambiguity and low conflict can be implemented administratively; high-conflict policies need political management; ambiguous ones need experimentation.

**2. Conditions for success**
Hogwood and Gunn's "perfect implementation" conditions include: no crippling external constraints, adequate time and resources, a valid theory of cause and effect, few links in the chain, agreed objectives, clearly sequenced tasks, good communication and compliance. Pakistan often lacks most of these.

**3. Factors fatal to implementation in Pakistan**
1. *Vague or donor-driven objectives.* Policies are drafted to meet external conditions or electoral promises without clear targets or costed plans.
2. *Political instability and reversals.* New governments abandon predecessors' projects; ministers and secretaries change frequently.
3. *Weak capacity.* Generalist officers lead technical programmes; field staff are under-trained; data systems are poor.
4. *Coordination failure.* After the 18th Amendment, many subjects are provincial, but federal ministries still run programmes; agencies duplicate or block each other.
5. *Unreliable funding.* PSDP cuts mid-year; releases are late; recurrent costs of new facilities are not budgeted.
6. *Corruption and elite capture.* Benefits leak to the connected; procurement is slow and distorted.
7. *Frequent transfers.* Officers leave before results appear.
8. *Over-centralisation and file culture.* Small decisions climb the hierarchy; fear of NAB and audit causes delay.
9. *Resistance by vested interests.* Tax reforms, energy pricing and land record digitisation face organised opposition.
10. *Missing local governments.* No elected tier close to citizens to adapt and monitor delivery.
11. *Legal delays.* Stay orders freeze projects and recruitments.
12. *Weak monitoring and evaluation.* PC-III and PC-IV reports are formalities; independent evaluation is rare.

**4. Examples**
- *Article 25A* promises free and compulsory education for ages 5–16, but millions of children remain out of school despite provincial laws.
- *National Action Plan (2014)* had twenty points against terrorism; reviews have repeatedly found progress uneven on madrassa reform and terror financing.
- *Power-sector reforms* have been announced repeatedly while circular debt has persisted.
- *Successes* show what works: BISP's data-driven targeting; NADRA; the polio programme's emergency operations centres; Punjab's education roadmap (2011–18), which used a delivery unit with monthly data on school performance.

**5. Critical analysis**
Not every failure is the bureaucracy's fault. Policies are often designed without consulting implementers (the top-down trap) and without funds. Street-level staff face impossible workloads. At the same time, the bureaucracy's risk aversion and patronage culture turn small problems into failures. Successful cases share three traits: clear, measurable goals; protected leadership with stable tenure; and real-time data with consequences.

**6. Improving implementation**
1. *Design for delivery:* SMART objectives, costed plans, consultation with provinces and field staff, a clear theory of change.
2. *Delivery units* in the PM and CM offices to track a few priorities with data (as Michael Barber's UK Delivery Unit did).
3. *Stable tenure* (at least two to three years) for project leaders.
4. *Predictable funding*: protect priority projects from mid-year cuts; budget recurrent costs.
5. *Coordination*: regular CCI and sectoral ministers' conferences; clear federal–provincial roles.
6. *Digital tracking*: dashboards, GIS monitoring, e-procurement.
7. *Empower local governments* to implement and adapt.
8. *Independent evaluation* with published results and follow-up.
9. *Citizen feedback*: Citizen's Portal data used to rank offices.
10. *Protect good-faith decisions* from punitive accountability so officials take action.

**Applying theory to a Pakistani case**
Matland's ambiguity–conflict model helps diagnose failures. The polio programme is a low-ambiguity but high-conflict policy: the goal (vaccinate every child) is clear, but resistance in some communities and attacks on vaccinators make it political; it improved when emergency operations centres combined top-down data with community engagement through local religious leaders. Tax reform is also high-conflict: the goal is clear, but traders' associations resist, so administrative solutions alone fail without political bargaining. Education reform under Article 25A is high in both ambiguity and conflict — what counts as quality, who pays, which language — so pilots and adaptation are needed.

**A success story: Punjab's education roadmap (2011–18)**
Working with Michael Barber, the Punjab government set a small number of targets (teacher presence, student attendance, facilities), collected monthly data through district monitoring officers, and held regular stocktakes chaired by the Chief Minister. Teacher and student attendance improved measurably. The lessons — few priorities, reliable data, regular high-level review, and consequences — apply to any sector.

**Role of street-level bureaucrats**
Lipsky's insight matters in Pakistan: a policy on school attendance or FIR registration is finally made by the teacher or the station clerk. Training, manageable workloads and clear service standards for these officials are therefore part of implementation design, not an afterthought.

**Conclusion**
Policies fail in Pakistan at the stage where theory says they are most fragile: in the long chain from cabinet room to classroom. The solution is not more policies but fewer, clearer priorities, delivered by stable, capable teams with money, data and authority, and held accountable for results. Implementation is administration — and administration is where Pakistan must invest.

## Answer 6. Corruption is a betrayal of public trust for private gain. Discuss its types, causes and effects in Pakistan's public sector, and suggest measures to control it. *(CSS 2018, 2021, 2024)*

**Outline**
1. Introduction and thesis
2. Meaning and types
3. Causes (Klitgaard and beyond)
4. Effects: on organisations, development and democracy
5. Pakistan's anti-corruption framework and its record
6. Measures to control corruption
7. Role of citizens and civil society
8. Conclusion

**Introduction**
Transparency International defines corruption as the abuse of entrusted power for private gain. In Pakistan it is a daily experience for citizens dealing with police, land records or utilities, and a recurring theme of politics. Pakistan scored 28 out of 100 and ranked 136th of 182 countries in the Corruption Perceptions Index 2025. **Corruption in Pakistan is systemic: it grows from monopoly, discretion and weak accountability, and it can be reduced only by changing systems — not by selective prosecutions.**

**1. Types**
- *Petty corruption:* bribes and "speed money" for routine services.
- *Grand corruption:* kickbacks on mega-contracts, policy decisions bought by interests.
- *Bribery, embezzlement, extortion, fraud.*
- *Nepotism and favouritism:* jobs and postings for relatives and supporters.
- *State capture:* businesses shaping laws and regulations (sugar subsidies, IPP contracts are often cited as examples of policy influenced by interests).
- *Collusive vs coercive corruption.*

**2. Causes**
Robert Klitgaard's formula: **Corruption = Monopoly + Discretion − Accountability.**
- *Monopoly:* single agencies control land records, licences, permits.
- *Discretion:* vague rules and manual processes let officials decide case by case.
- *Weak accountability:* slow courts, politicised anti-corruption bodies, unprotected whistle-blowers.

Other causes:
- low real pay at junior and middle levels;
- complex procedures and cash transactions;
- political financing and patronage networks;
- social norms of gift-giving and kinship obligations;
- colonial secrecy (Official Secrets Act 1923);
- a large informal economy.

**3. Effects**
- *On organisations:* demoralised honest officers; recruitment and postings by connection rather than merit; poor decisions.
- *On development:* Paolo Mauro (1995) found corruption reduces investment and growth; public spending tilts toward construction projects where kickbacks are easier; revenue is lost through tax evasion.
- *On the poor:* bribes act as a regressive tax; services reach the connected first.
- *On democracy:* votes and policies are bought; citizens lose trust; military interventions in Pakistan have repeatedly cited civilian corruption as justification, weakening democracy further.

**4. Pakistan's framework and record**
- **NAB** (National Accountability Ordinance 1999): recovered large sums through plea bargains but criticised for selective, politically timed cases and long detentions. Amendments in 2022 narrowed its scope; litigation on them continued until 2024.
- **FIA** and provincial **Anti-Corruption Establishments** handle lower-level cases.
- **Auditor-General** and **PAC** expose irregularities, but audit paras pile up.
- **Ombudsmen** handle maladministration.
- **Right of Access to Information Act 2017** and Article 19A enable transparency.
- **Civil Servants Conduct Rules 2026** require annual online asset declarations for officers in BS-17 and above, recusal for conflicts of interest and approval for gifts and outside work.
- **Digitisation:** e-procurement (PPRA), land records (PLRA in Punjab), FBR point-of-sale integration, BISP payments through biometrics.
- International commitments: UN Convention against Corruption (ratified 2007); FATF action plan (grey list exit 2022).

The framework is wide but enforcement has been selective, slow and politicised. CPI scores have barely moved over a decade.

**5. Measures to control corruption**
*Prevention (reduce monopoly and discretion):*
1. End-to-end digital services — land, licences, taxes, procurement.
2. Simplify laws and procedures; publish service standards and timelines.
3. Competitive pay for sensitive posts; rotation.
4. Merit-based postings with fixed tenure.

*Transparency:*
5. Publish asset declarations of senior officials and legislators.
6. Open contracting data for all public procurement.
7. Proactive disclosure under RTI law.

*Detection and enforcement (raise accountability):*
8. An independent, non-partisan anti-corruption agency on the Hong Kong ICAC model — with clear thresholds, fair trial and no political timing.
9. Special courts with time limits; asset recovery.
10. Whistle-blower protection with rewards.
11. Follow-up of audit paras within fixed deadlines.

*Political measures:*
12. Transparent party and election financing.
13. Cross-party charter on accountability, so it is not used against opponents.

**6. Role of citizens and civil society**
Citizens can refuse to pay bribes and use online services; file RTI requests; report through the Citizen's Portal; join social audits of schools and health units; support honest officials publicly. Civil society organisations and media can investigate and publish, and professional bodies can enforce codes of ethics.

**International lessons**
Countries that reduced corruption sharply did so through institutions, not campaigns. Hong Kong's Independent Commission Against Corruption (1974) combined investigation, prevention (redesigning procedures in vulnerable departments) and community education, and reported to the head of government rather than to the police it investigated. Singapore's Corrupt Practices Investigation Bureau (1952) worked alongside competitive public pay and strict enforcement at the top. Georgia, after 2004, dismissed its notoriously corrupt traffic police and recruited a new, better-paid patrol police, while moving many services online; petty bribery fell dramatically. The common elements — political will at the top, independent and non-partisan enforcement, simplified and digitised services, and adequate pay — match Klitgaard's formula and are missing or incomplete in Pakistan.

**Corruption in the police and land administration — two examples**
Citizens' surveys by Transparency International Pakistan have repeatedly placed police and land administration among the most corrupt sectors. Both show Klitgaard's formula at work: the station house officer has a monopoly over registering an FIR and wide discretion in investigation; the patwari long had a monopoly over land records. Punjab's computerisation of land records through the Punjab Land Records Authority reduced the patwari's monopoly and discretion, and complaints about bribery for land documents fell, though problems remain. A similar logic — digital FIR registration, online tracking of cases and independent complaint bodies — could reduce police corruption.

**Conclusion**
Corruption in Pakistan is a system problem, not just a moral one. It thrives where officials have monopoly and discretion and face little accountability. The cure lies in removing monopoly through competition and digitisation, structuring discretion through clear rules, and building accountability that is fair and non-partisan. Exemplary punishment matters, but systems matter more.

## Answer 7. Critically evaluate the civil service reform agenda in Pakistan since 2024. Will it create a more efficient and responsive structure? *(Likely 2028; builds on CSS 2016 Q2)*

**Outline**
1. Introduction and thesis
2. Why reform: the problems
3. Earlier reform attempts and why they failed
4. Reforms already in force (2024–26)
5. The September 2026 reform package
6. Critical evaluation: strengths
7. Weaknesses and risks
8. Conditions for success
9. Conclusion

**Introduction**
Pakistan's civil service descends from the Indian Civil Service, once called the "steel frame" of the Raj. Since 1947 almost every government has promised to reform it: the Cornelius Commission (1962), Bhutto's 1973 reforms, Musharraf's National Reconstruction Bureau, the National Commission for Government Reforms (2008) and the Task Force under Dr Ishrat Husain (2019–21). Since September 2024 the federal government has run another reform process. **The 2024–26 agenda tackles the right problems — generalism, weak performance management, an over-large state and an unsustainable pension bill — but its success depends on implementation, depoliticised selection and provincial buy-in, which past reforms never secured.**

**1. The problems**
- *Generalist dominance:* PAS and other generalist officers head technical ministries.
- *Weak performance management:* inflated annual reports, promotion by seniority.
- *Politicisation:* postings and transfers as patronage; short tenures.
- *Bloated structure:* overlapping bodies, many vacant posts, five decision tiers.
- *Fiscal burden:* rising pensions and salaries.
- *Slow recruitment:* the CSS cycle takes about two years.
- *Outdated rules:* conduct rules dating from 1964.

**2. Earlier reforms and why they failed**
Bhutto's 1973 reforms broke the CSP's monopoly but removed constitutional protections, making officers dependent on politicians. NRB's devolution (2001) was reversed after 2008. NCGR's 2008 proposals for a National Executive Service and specialised cadres were shelved. The 2019 Task Force produced new discipline and retirement rules but its restructuring plan was only partly implemented. Common causes: no cross-party ownership, bureaucratic resistance, short governments and lack of an implementation mechanism.

**3. Reforms already in force (2024–26)**
- *Contributory pension:* federal civil employees appointed on or after 1 July 2024 join a defined-contribution scheme (employee 10%, government 12% under rules notified in 2025). Pensions for future retirees are calculated on the average of the last 24 months' pay.
- *Rightsizing:* the cabinet approved abolishing or merging 82 federal bodies in the first phase, and about 150,000 vacant posts were declared for abolition (announced January 2025).
- *Civil Servants Conduct Rules 2026:* replaced the 1964 rules, requiring annual online asset declarations for officers in BS-17 and above, recusal for conflicts of interest, limits on social-media activity and a ban on political activity and unauthorised outside employment.
- *Discipline and retirement rules (2020)* remain the framework for removing poor performers.

**4. The September 2026 reform package**
Presented on 19 September 2026 at a session chaired by the Planning Minister, after two committees reached consensus on 43 of 46 recommendations. Main proposals:
- eleven specialised professional cadres (HR, law, engineering, science and technology, IT and telecom, energy, climate, agriculture and forestry, education, health and others);
- a **National Executive Service**: BS-20 and above filled by competition for five-year contracts with performance targets, open to serving officers, private professionals and academics;
- decision tiers cut from five to three;
- Establishment Division restructured as a Human Resource and Organisation Development Division;
- CSS retained but recruitment by specialisation; MPT pass mark raised from 33% to 40%; analytical reasoning added; negative marking removed; compulsory papers in English or Urdu; a digital recruitment cycle of six months or less;
- twice-yearly performance assessments, ministry targets and 5% of employee-related expenditure for incentives; promotion weighted to performance and skills;
- a National University of Public Administration; a shorter National Management Course;
- an estimated cost of about Rs11bn and a 120-day implementation committee after approval.
As of late November 2026, I could not confirm cabinet approval.

**5. Strengths**
1. *Specialisation* addresses the generalist problem identified by every commission since 1962.
2. *Open competition for top posts* brings outside expertise and competition (an open system at the top, as in the UK Senior Civil Service).
3. *Performance focus* — incentives, targets and twice-yearly reviews replace inflated confidential reports.
4. *Flatter structure* speeds decisions.
5. *Fiscal sustainability* from pension reform and rightsizing.
6. *Ethics* — the Conduct Rules modernise integrity standards for the social-media age.
7. *Faster, digital recruitment* reduces the two-year CSS cycle.

**6. Weaknesses and risks**
1. *Politicisation of contract posts.* If ministers choose NES appointees, five-year contracts may become a new spoils system. Selection must be independent.
2. *Provincial resistance.* PAS and PSP serve in provinces; provinces may see changes as federal overreach after the 18th Amendment.
3. *Bureaucratic resistance.* Generalist services stand to lose posts and influence.
4. *Performance systems without data* invite gaming; ministries lack reliable KPIs.
5. *Pay not reformed.* Specialists and private professionals will not join at current pay scales.
6. *Cost and capacity.* Rs11bn is modest; the HR Division needs new skills.
7. *Urdu option and lower screening standards* raise concerns about quality, though they may widen access.
8. *Implementation history.* Past packages died in implementation.

**7. Conditions for success**
- Cross-party endorsement so that a change of government does not reverse it.
- An independent body (a reconstituted, autonomous Civil Service Commission or FPSC wing) to run NES selection.
- Amendments to the Civil Servants Act 1973 to give legal backing.
- Pay reform for specialists linked to market benchmarks.
- Phased pilots in a few ministries, with published results.
- Consultation with provinces through the CCI.
- Protection of tenure for officers in key posts, so performance can be judged.

**Sequencing the reforms**
A realistic sequence would be: (1) in the first year, pass legal amendments, set up the independent selection body for the National Executive Service, and pilot specialised cadres in two or three ministries such as IT and energy; (2) in the second year, launch the new performance system with twice-yearly reviews and publish ministry targets; (3) from the third year, scale up cadres and contract-based senior posts, linked to a pay review for specialists. Each step should be evaluated publicly. Reforms that try to change everything at once tend to change nothing.

**Conclusion**
The 2024–26 agenda is the most comprehensive since 1973 and, unlike Bhutto's reforms, aims at specialisation and performance rather than control. Pension reform, rightsizing and new conduct rules are already in force. Whether the larger package creates a more efficient and responsive service depends on whether selection is depoliticised, pay matches the new expectations and the reforms survive the next government. The design is promising; the history of implementation is the warning.

## Answer 8. There are three major forms of administrative decentralisation — deconcentration, delegation and devolution. Which suits Pakistan's socio-political context? Discuss the challenges of local governance after devolution. *(CSS 2017; part of 2019, 2025)*

**Outline**
1. Introduction and thesis
2. Meaning of decentralisation; Rondinelli's forms
3. Characteristics of each form (table)
4. Pakistan's experience with each
5. Why devolution suits Pakistan
6. Post-devolution challenges
7. Way forward
8. Conclusion

**Introduction**
Decentralisation means transferring authority and responsibility from central government to lower levels or other bodies. Dennis Rondinelli (1983) distinguished deconcentration, delegation, devolution and privatisation. Pakistan has tried all of them: colonial-style deconcentration through deputy commissioners, delegation to authorities like WAPDA and development authorities, and devolution through the 2001 Local Government Ordinance and the 18th Amendment. **Devolution is the form most suited to Pakistan's diversity, constitution and governance needs — but only if it is protected constitutionally, financed adequately and phased with capacity building.**

**1. The forms**

| | Deconcentration | Delegation | Devolution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Shift of workload to field offices of the same ministry | Transfer of functions to semi-autonomous bodies | Transfer of authority, resources and decisions to elected, legally autonomous sub-national governments |
| Accountability | Upward to the ministry | To the parent ministry through boards | Downward to local voters |
| Autonomy | Lowest | Medium | Highest |
| Pakistan example | Divisional commissioners, DCs, regional passport offices | WAPDA, NHA, LDA, KDA, WASAs | 18th Amendment to provinces; LGO 2001; Article 140A local governments |

**2. Pakistan's experience**
- *Deconcentration:* the colonial district model — the deputy commissioner coordinating everything — still dominates. It provides order and revenue but limited voice.
- *Delegation:* authorities and companies deliver some services efficiently (Rescue 1122, Safe City), but in cities they fragment governance — Karachi's water, building control and waste are handled by bodies outside the elected city government.
- *Devolution:* the 2001 LGO created elected district, tehsil and union governments under the "5D" model (devolution of political power, decentralisation of administrative authority, deconcentration of management functions, diffusion of the power–authority nexus, distribution of resources). It increased local spending and women's representation, but it was imposed by a military government, bypassed provinces and was dismantled after 2009. The 18th Amendment (2010) devolved major subjects to provinces and inserted Article 140A, requiring provinces to devolve "political, administrative and financial responsibility and authority" to local governments.

**3. Why devolution suits Pakistan**
1. *Constitutional mandate* — Articles 32 and 140A.
2. *Diversity* — a multi-ethnic, multi-lingual federation needs local self-government to build ownership.
3. *Accountability* — elected local leaders are closer to citizens than distant secretaries.
4. *Service delivery* — sanitation, water, primary health and schools are local by nature.
5. *Democratic training ground* — local politics produces future leaders and reduces dynastic dominance.
6. *Scale* — provinces like Punjab (over 120 million people) are too large to run from one secretariat.
7. *Evidence* — international cases (India's 73rd and 74th Amendments, Indonesia's 2001 decentralisation) show benefits when local governments have constitutional protection and funds.

Deconcentration alone keeps the colonial model; delegation alone fragments authority without democratic accountability. Devolution, supported by deconcentrated provincial offices and well-governed authorities, is the right core.

**4. Post-devolution challenges**
1. *Irregular elections.* Provinces delay polls and keep rewriting laws. Punjab has had no elected local governments for most of the time since 2019; after laws in 2019, 2022 and 2025, its new Local Government Act 2025 abolished district councils and created municipal corporations, tehsil councils and town committees. Election rules for party-based polls were notified in March 2026 and delimitation was reported complete in November 2026, but no polling date had been confirmed.
2. *Weak constitutional protection.* Article 140A does not list local functions, guarantee tenure or fix a share of resources.
3. *Provincial reluctance.* Provincial legislators see local governments as rivals for development funds.
4. *Fiscal dependence.* Local governments rely on provincial transfers; property tax is poorly collected; Provincial Finance Commission awards are irregular.
5. *Parallel bodies.* Development authorities, water boards and companies take over municipal functions.
6. *Bureaucratic control.* Provincial departments and DCs retain control of staff and budgets.
7. *Capacity.* Few trained planners and accountants at the local level.
8. *Elite capture.* *Biradari* and landlord influence dominate local politics.
9. *Urban governance.* Karachi and Lahore lack unified metropolitan authority.

**5. Way forward**
1. A constitutional amendment protecting local governments: fixed tenure, no dissolution without cause, elections within 120 days of term expiry, a guaranteed share of the provincial divisible pool.
2. Regular, formula-based Provincial Finance Commission awards with performance grants.
3. Transfer municipal authorities to elected city governments; create metropolitan governments for the largest cities.
4. Own-source revenue: digital property cadastre and market-value property tax.
5. A local government service cadre with training and career paths.
6. Clear division of functions among province, district and city.
7. Citizen oversight: participatory budgeting, social audit, digital complaints.

**Lessons from abroad**
India's 73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendments (1992) gave panchayats and municipalities constitutional status, mandatory elections every five years, reserved seats for women and disadvantaged groups, and State Finance Commissions to share revenue. Implementation is uneven, but local elections are now regular across India. Indonesia's "big bang" decentralisation (2001) moved large functions and budgets to districts almost overnight; services improved in some districts and suffered in others, showing the need for capacity and oversight. Pakistan can borrow the constitutional guarantees from India and the lesson about capacity from Indonesia.

**Sequencing devolution**
Devolution should be phased: first municipal services and primary health and education, with clear budgets; then wider functions as capacity is proven through audits and citizen scorecards.

**Conclusion**
Pakistan's socio-political context — federal, diverse, rapidly urbanising — calls for devolution. Deconcentration and delegation have roles, but on their own they keep power far from citizens. The record since 2001 shows that devolution fails when it is imposed, unprotected and unfunded. A constitutionally protected, financially secure and capable local government system would make Article 140A a reality rather than a promise.
