# 4. Feminist Movements

Syllabus section IV. Asked in 8 of 11 papers: women's movement in Pakistan (2016, 2022, 2024, 2026), three waves and their influence on Pakistan (2019, 2023), first and third waves (2022), principles of the waves (2025), APWA and WAF (2018 note), NGO-isation (2026).

## 4.1 The "wave" metaphor

Coined by journalist **Martha Weinman Lear** in 1968 ("The Second Feminist Wave", *New York Times Magazine*). Useful for exams, but note its limits: it is US/UK-centred, hides continuity between waves, and ignores non-Western movements (e.g. Muslim women's activism in India from the 1880s).

## 4.2 First wave (c. 1840s–1920s)

**Goal.** Legal personhood: the vote (suffrage), property rights for married women, access to education and professions, rights in divorce and child custody.

**Background.** Enlightenment ideas (Wollstonecraft, 1792), the anti-slavery movement, industrialisation.

**Key events.**

| Year | Event |
|---|---|
| 1840 | Women delegates (incl. **Lucretia Mott** and **Elizabeth Cady Stanton**) excluded from the World Anti-Slavery Convention in London; they resolve to hold a women's rights meeting |
| 1848 | **Seneca Falls Convention**, New York (19–20 July), led by Stanton and Mott. **Declaration of Sentiments** modelled on the Declaration of Independence; resolution for the vote passed narrowly, supported by Frederick Douglass |
| 1851 | Sojourner Truth's "Ain't I a Woman?" speech (Akron, Ohio) — early intersectional voice |
| 1869 | Stanton and **Susan B. Anthony** found the National Woman Suffrage Association; J.S. Mill, *The Subjection of Women* |
| 1882 | UK Married Women's Property Act |
| 1893 | **New Zealand**: first self-governing country to give women the national vote (Kate Sheppard's campaign) |
| 1897 | **Millicent Fawcett** leads the National Union of Women's Suffrage Societies (constitutional "suffragists") |
| 1903 | **Emmeline Pankhurst** founds the Women's Social and Political Union ("Deeds, not words"; militant "suffragettes") |
| 1906 | Finland: first European country to give women the vote and the right to stand for parliament |
| 1918 | UK Representation of the People Act: vote for women over 30 meeting property qualifications |
| 1920 | US **19th Amendment** |
| 1928 | UK Equal Franchise Act: equal voting age (21) |

**Strategies.** Petitions, conventions, lobbying, newspapers, mass marches, civil disobedience and hunger strikes (suffragettes).

**Achievements.** Votes for women in most Western countries; property and education rights.

**Criticisms.** Mostly white, middle-class, Protestant women; some suffragists used racist arguments; working-class and Black women's concerns sidelined.

**Influence on the subcontinent.** Indian women gained limited franchise in the provinces from 1921 (Madras first); the Government of India Act 1935 widened it. Muslim women's associations formed (All India Muslim Ladies' Conference, 1914); Muslim women joined the Khilafat movement (Bi Amman) and later the Pakistan Movement.

## 4.3 Second wave (c. 1960s–1980s)

**Goal.** Beyond legal equality to **social and cultural equality**: work, pay, family, sexuality, reproduction, violence. Slogan: **"The personal is political."**

**Key texts.** de Beauvoir, *The Second Sex* (1949, forerunner); Friedan, *The Feminine Mystique* (1963); Millett, *Sexual Politics* (1970); Firestone (1970); Germaine Greer, *The Female Eunuch* (1970).

**Key events.**

| Year | Event |
|---|---|
| 1963 | US Equal Pay Act; *The Feminine Mystique* |
| 1964 | US Civil Rights Act, Title VII (bans sex discrimination in employment) |
| 1966 | NOW founded |
| 1968 | Protest at the Miss America pageant, Atlantic City |
| 1970 | UK Equal Pay Act; first UK National Women's Liberation Conference (Ruskin College, Oxford) |
| 1972 | US Congress passes the Equal Rights Amendment (ERA) — never ratified by enough states; Title IX (education) |
| 1973 | *Roe v. Wade*: US Supreme Court recognised a constitutional right to abortion (overturned by *Dobbs* in 2022) |
| 1975 | UN International Women's Year; Mexico City conference; UK Sex Discrimination Act |
| 1979 | CEDAW adopted by UN General Assembly |

**Strands.** Liberal (NOW: legal and workplace equality), radical (consciousness-raising, violence, sexuality), socialist (work and class), Black feminism (Combahee River Collective Statement, 1977; Audre Lorde; bell hooks, *Ain't I a Woman*, 1981).

**Achievements.** Anti-discrimination law, equal pay laws, reproductive rights, recognition of domestic violence and rape within marriage, women's shelters, Women's Studies departments, UN Decade for Women.

**Criticisms.** Dominated by white middle-class women; universalised "sisterhood"; sex wars over pornography; neglect of race, class and sexuality.

## 4.4 Third wave (c. 1990s–2000s)

**Origin of term.** **Rebecca Walker**, "Becoming the Third Wave", *Ms.* magazine (1992), responding to the Anita Hill–Clarence Thomas hearings (1991): "I am not a post-feminist feminist. I am the Third Wave." (2024 MCQ.)

**Goals and themes.**
1. **Diversity and intersectionality**: Kimberlé Crenshaw's "intersectionality" (1989); voices of women of colour, lesbian, working-class and Global South women.
2. **Individual identity and choice**: rejects a single definition of feminism; "girlie" feminism reclaims femininity, make-up, sexuality.
3. **Culture and media**: riot grrrl punk bands, zines, *Bust* and *Bitch* magazines; Eve Ensler's *The Vagina Monologues* (1996).
4. **Postmodern and queer theory**: Butler (1990).
5. **Global feminism**: Beijing 1995; violence against women as a human-rights issue (Vienna 1993: "women's rights are human rights").

**Criticisms.** Too individualistic and consumerist; lacks a clear political agenda; "choice feminism" can excuse any choice; less grassroots organising.

## 4.5 Fourth wave (c. 2012–present) — add it; examiners like it

- **Digital activism**: hashtags, online campaigns. Laura Bates's Everyday Sexism Project (2012). **#MeToo** (phrase used by Tarana Burke from 2006; went viral in October 2017 after Alyssa Milano's tweet on Harvey Weinstein). #TimesUp (2018), #NiUnaMenos (Argentina, 2015).
- Themes: sexual harassment, rape culture, online abuse, body positivity, intersectionality, trans inclusion (contested inside feminism), climate.
- Pakistan: **Aurat March** (since 2018), #MeToo cases (e.g. Meesha Shafi's allegation against Ali Zafar, 2018, still in courts [verify status]), online campaigns after the motorway gang-rape (2020) and Noor Mukadam's murder (2021).

## 4.7 United Nations conferences on women

| Conference | Year | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| **Commission on the Status of Women (CSW)** | est. 1946 | Main UN body; drafted the 1967 Declaration on the Elimination of Discrimination against Women and CEDAW |
| **1st World Conference, Mexico City** | 1975 | International Women's Year; World Plan of Action; UN declares the **Decade for Women (1976–1985)**: equality, development, peace. Led to INSTRAW and UNIFEM (1976) |
| **CEDAW** adopted | 18 Dec 1979 | "International bill of rights for women" (2026 MCQ). In force 3 Sept 1981. 30 articles. Committee of 23 experts. Optional Protocol (1999) allows individual complaints |
| **2nd World Conference, Copenhagen** | 1980 | Mid-decade review; focus on employment, health, education; CEDAW opened for signature at a ceremony there |
| **3rd World Conference, Nairobi** | 1985 | End of the Decade; **Nairobi Forward-Looking Strategies** to 2000 |
| Vienna World Conference on Human Rights | 1993 | "Women's rights are human rights"; led to the UN **Declaration on the Elimination of Violence against Women** (GA, Dec 1993) and a Special Rapporteur on VAW (1994) |
| Cairo ICPD | 1994 | Reproductive rights and women's empowerment at the centre of population policy |
| **4th World Conference, Beijing** | 1995 | **Beijing Declaration and Platform for Action**, adopted by 189 states: 12 critical areas of concern (poverty, education, health, violence, armed conflict, economy, power and decision-making, institutional mechanisms, human rights, media, environment, the girl child). Adopted **gender mainstreaming** as the global strategy. Benazir Bhutto addressed it as Pakistan's PM |
| Beijing+5 (New York) | 2000 | Review; also UNSC Resolution **1325** on women, peace and security (Oct 2000) and MDG 3 |
| Beijing +10, +15, +20, +25 | 2005–2020 | Reviews at CSW; Generation Equality Forum (Mexico City and Paris, 2021) |
| **UN Women** | created July 2010, operational 2011 | Merged DAW, INSTRAW, OSAGI and UNIFEM |
| **SDG 5** | 2015 | "Achieve gender equality and empower all women and girls" (2026 MCQ) — targets on discrimination, violence, child marriage, unpaid care, leadership, reproductive health |
| Beijing+30 | 2025 | Reviewed at CSW69 (March 2025) |

**Pakistan and CEDAW.** Pakistan **acceded on 12 March 1996** with a declaration that accession is subject to the Constitution, and a reservation on Article 29(1) (disputes to the International Court of Justice). It has not joined the Optional Protocol. It reports periodically to the CEDAW Committee.

## 4.8 Feminist movements in Pakistan (the highest-value topic in this section)

### Phase 1: Pre-partition roots (1880s–1947)

- Reformers: Sir Syed's movement (education, mainly for boys); Nazir Ahmad's novel *Mirat-ul-Uroos* (1869) on educated housewives; Maulvi Mumtaz Ali, *Huquq-e-Niswan* (1898); Ashraf Ali Thanvi's *Bahishti Zewar* (domestic and religious education for women); **Sheikh Abdullah and Waheed Jahan Begum** founded the Aligarh girls' school (1906), later Aligarh Women's College.
- Women's organisations: Anjuman-e-Khawateen-e-Islam; **All India Muslim Ladies' Conference** (1914).
- **Pakistan Movement**: Muslim League women's sub-committee (1938); **Fatima Jinnah**, **Begum Jahanara Shahnawaz**, **Begum Shaista Ikramullah**, **Lady Abdullah Haroon**, **Begum Salma Tassaduq Hussain**; women picketed and courted arrest in the Punjab civil disobedience against the Khizar ministry (1947). Quaid's 1944 Aligarh speech on women.
- Muslim Personal Law (Shariat) Application Act 1937 gave Muslim women inheritance (except agricultural land, a gap that lasted); Dissolution of Muslim Marriages Act 1939.

### Phase 2: Welfare and reform under the new state (1947–1970s)

- Two women in the first Constituent Assembly: Begum Jahanara Shahnawaz and Begum Shaista Ikramullah (2025 MCQ).
- **APWA (All Pakistan Women's Association)**, founded 1949 by **Begum Ra'ana Liaquat Ali Khan**: refugee relief, schools, industrial homes, health; later pushed for family law reform. Women's National Guard and Pakistan Women's Naval Reserve (1949).
- Muslim Personal Law of Shariat (West Punjab) 1948, extending inheritance to agricultural land, after women's lobbying [verify date and scope].
- **Rashid Commission** on Marriage and Family Laws (1955) → **Muslim Family Laws Ordinance 1961** (Ayub Khan): registration of marriages, restrictions on polygamy (arbitration council permission), procedure for talaq, rights of grandchildren to inherit. Opposed by religious groups; supported by APWA.
- **Fatima Jinnah**'s 1965 presidential campaign against Ayub, backed by the combined opposition (including some religious parties) — showed acceptance of a woman leader.
- 1973 Constitution: Articles 25, 27, 32, 34, 35; 10 reserved seats for women in the National Assembly.
- Character: elite, urban, welfare-oriented, state-friendly. Criticised as not challenging patriarchy.

### Phase 3: Resistance under Zia (1977–1988) — birth of an autonomous movement

- Zia's Islamisation: **Hudood Ordinances (1979)**, especially the Zina Ordinance (rape victims who could not prove rape with four witnesses risked prosecution for adultery); **Qanun-e-Shahadat (Law of Evidence) 1984**, Article 17 (in financial matters two women witnesses in place of one man); proposed Qisas and Diyat law; dress codes and restrictions on women in sport and media.
- **Women's Action Forum (WAF)**, founded **September 1981 in Karachi**, after the Fehmida–Allah Bux case (a couple sentenced under the Zina Ordinance), then chapters in Lahore and Islamabad. Non-hierarchical, non-party, volunteer-based, secular in approach.
- **12 February 1983**: WAF and the Punjab Women Lawyers Association marched on The Mall, Lahore, against the draft Law of Evidence; police baton-charged and arrested women. The poet **Habib Jalib** joined. 12 February is now marked as **National Women's Day** (declared by the government in 2012 [verify year]).
- **Safia Bibi case (1983)**: a blind domestic worker who reported rape was convicted of zina; public outcry led the Federal Shariat Court to overturn the conviction.
- Other groups: Shirkat Gah (1975), Simorgh, ASR, Aurat Foundation (1986), Sindhiani Tehreek (rural Sindhi women in the MRD, 1980s), HRCP (1987).
- Significance: shift from welfare to rights and resistance; linked women's rights to democracy.
- Reference: Khawar Mumtaz and Farida Shaheed, *Women of Pakistan: Two Steps Forward, One Step Back?* (1987).

### Phase 4: Democratisation, NGOs and international frameworks (1988–2007)

- **Benazir Bhutto**: first woman prime minister of a Muslim-majority country (1988). First Women Bank (1989), women's police stations (1994), Lady Health Worker Programme (1994), Ministry of Women Development; signed CEDAW (1996). Her governments did not repeal the Hudood laws.
- Commission of Inquiry for Women (1997, chaired by Justice Nasir Aslam Zahid) recommended repeal of the Hudood laws.
- Rise of donor-funded **NGOs** after Beijing 1995: research, legal aid, shelters, awareness.
- Musharraf era: **National Commission on the Status of Women (NCSW)** (2000); National Policy for Development and Empowerment of Women (2002); **60 reserved seats** in the National Assembly and 33% in local government (2000–2002); **Protection of Women (Criminal Laws Amendment) Act 2006** (moved rape from the Zina Ordinance to the Pakistan Penal Code); **Mukhtaran Mai** (2002) became a global symbol.

### Phase 5: Legislative gains and new activism (2008–2017)

- Women's Parliamentary Caucus (2008); first woman Speaker of the National Assembly, **Dr Fehmida Mirza** (2008).
- Laws: Protection against Harassment of Women at the Workplace Act **2010**; Criminal Law (Amendment) Act 2010 (s. 509 harassment); Acid Control and Acid Crime Prevention Act 2011; Prevention of Anti-Women Practices Act 2011 (forced marriage, marriage with the Quran, depriving inheritance); NCSW Act 2012 (statutory, autonomous); Sindh Domestic Violence Act 2013; Sindh Child Marriages Restraint Act 2013; Balochistan DV Act 2014; Punjab Protection of Women against Violence Act 2016; **Criminal Law (Amendment) (Offences in the name or pretext of Honour) Act 2016** (after Qandeel Baloch's murder, July 2016); Criminal Law (Amendment) (Offences relating to Rape) Act 2016.
- 18th Amendment (2010): women's ministry devolved to provinces; provincial women's commissions.
- **Malala Yousafzai** shot (2012), Nobel Peace Prize (2014).

### Phase 6: Aurat March and digital feminism (2018–present)

- **Aurat March** (Women's March) on 8 March, first in Karachi in 2018 (organised by the collective **Hum Auratein**), spread to Lahore, Islamabad, Hyderabad, Multan, Quetta and other cities. Themes: violence, harassment, workplace rights, bodily autonomy, public space, minority and trans rights.
- Slogans such as "Mera Jism, Meri Marzi" and "Khud khana garam kar lo" sparked a national backlash (2019–2020), TV clashes, court petitions to ban marches, counter "Haya March". Supporters saw it as reclaiming public space; critics as un-Islamic or obscene. Present both views.
- Women Democratic Front (2018), Girls at Dhabas (2015, reclaiming public space), Pakistan #MeToo.
- Digital Rights Foundation (Nighat Dad; Cyber Harassment Helpline, 2016).
- Triggers: motorway gang-rape (Sept 2020) → Anti-Rape (Investigation and Trial) Act 2021; Noor Mukadam's murder (July 2021) → mass mobilisation; Supreme Court upheld Zahir Jaffer's conviction in May 2025.
- Recent laws: Enforcement of Women's Property Rights Act 2020; Protection against Harassment (Amendment) Act 2022 (wider definitions); ICT Child Marriage Restraint Act **2025** (minimum age 18 in Islamabad, assented 30 May 2025); ICT **Domestic Violence (Prevention and Protection) Act 2026** (passed by a joint sitting, assented 26 January 2026).
- Shift (2026 question): from **legal rights** (WAF against discriminatory laws) to **bodily autonomy, public space and consent** (Aurat March), using social media rather than lawyers' petitions alone.

### Strengths and weaknesses of Pakistan's women's movement (2016)

| Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|
| Won major laws (2006, 2010, 2011, 2016, 2020, 2025–26) | Urban, educated, English-speaking leadership; weak rural base |
| Kept issues on the national agenda through crises | Donor dependence and NGO-isation |
| Built alliances with lawyers, journalists, HRCP | Fragmentation; generational splits (WAF vs Aurat March styles) |
| Used courts and media effectively | Seen as "Western" or secular; religious backlash |
| International links (CEDAW, Beijing) give leverage | Implementation of laws is weak; police and courts unresponsive |
| New generation uses digital tools | Online harassment and threats against activists |

### NGO-isation (2026 question)

- **Concept.** Sabine Lang (1997) and Sonia Alvarez (1999, Latin America) described how feminist movements turn into professional NGOs that depend on donor funding, write project proposals and report "deliverables", becoming less political and less mass-based. Islah Jad (Palestine, 2004) used the term in Arab contexts.
- **In Pakistan.** After the 1990s, many activists moved into donor-funded NGOs. Critics (e.g. scholars such as Saba Gul Khattak, Afiya Shehrbano Zia, Saadia Toor [verify specific works]) argue this: (1) shifted accountability upward to donors rather than to women; (2) replaced mobilisation with workshops and "awareness"; (3) made "gender mainstreaming" a technical checklist; (4) exposed activists to the charge of serving foreign agendas; (5) favoured projects donors liked (microcredit, political quotas) over structural issues (land, labour rights).
- **WAD critique link.** WAD argued that women were always integrated into development, but on unequal terms set by global capitalism. NGO projects that "add women" without changing class and global structures repeat WID's mistake. Gender mainstreaming became a donor tool, not a transformative politics.
- **Counter-argument.** NGOs provided legal aid, shelters, data and drafting that produced real laws; donor funds were scarce otherwise; the Aurat March is largely volunteer-run, showing re-politicisation.
- **Balanced conclusion.** NGO-isation depoliticised parts of the movement, but it did not end it; the movement now combines professional advocacy with grassroots, self-funded activism.

### Islamic feminism (useful in Pakistan answers)

Scholars who argue for women's rights from within Islamic sources: **Riffat Hassan** (Pakistani-American theologian), **Asma Barlas**, *Believing Women in Islam* (2002), **Amina Wadud**, *Qur'an and Woman* (1992), **Fatima Mernissi**, *Beyond the Veil* (1975) and *The Veil and the Male Elite* (1987), **Leila Ahmed**, *Women and Gender in Islam* (1992). Critiques: secular feminists question whether religious frameworks can deliver full equality; conservatives reject reinterpretation. Saba Mahmood, *Politics of Piety* (2005) on women's mosque movements, and the Al-Huda movement in Pakistan, show that pious women exercise agency too.

## 4.9 APWA and WAF (2018 short note)

| | APWA | WAF |
|---|---|---|
| Founded | 1949, Karachi, by Begum Ra'ana Liaquat Ali Khan | 1981, Karachi (Lahore and Islamabad chapters soon after) |
| Context | Partition refugees, nation-building | Zia's Islamisation and martial law |
| Approach | Welfare, education, health, crafts; worked with the state | Rights, resistance, lobbying, protest; independent of state and parties |
| Membership | Elite women, wives of officials | Professionals, lawyers, academics, artists |
| Highlights | Schools and colleges, industrial homes; support for MFLO 1961; consultative status at the UN | 12 Feb 1983 protest; campaigns against Hudood laws and Law of Evidence; support for victims |
| Critique | Elitist, charitable rather than political | Urban and elite; secular image limited outreach |
