# 5. Gender and Development

Syllabus section V. **Asked in every paper 2016–2026, often twice.** Master this file first.

Typical questions: WID/WAD/GAD (2016, 2017, 2021, 2025), practical vs strategic needs (2018), modernisation and dependency (2019, 2021), structural functionalism (2019), gender analysis of development theories (2024), world-system theory and gendered globalisation (2025), colonial era and women (2020), colonial and capitalist perspectives (2022, 2023, 2024), SAPs (2022), globalisation (2017, 2018, 2019, 2025), WAD critique of NGO-isation (2026).

## 5.1 Key concepts

| Concept | Meaning | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Gendered division of labour | Work assigned by sex: men "productive" paid work, women "reproductive" unpaid care | Moser; Boserup |
| **Triple roles** | Women do productive (paid or subsistence), reproductive (care, housework) and community-managing work; men mostly productive and community politics | Caroline Moser, *Gender Planning and Development* (1993) |
| **Practical gender needs** | Immediate needs arising from women's existing roles (water, food, health, income) — meeting them does not challenge the division of labour | Maxine Molyneux (1985, "interests"), Moser (1989, "needs") |
| **Strategic gender interests/needs** | Needs that challenge subordination: legal equality, ending violence, control over one's body, land rights, political voice, sharing of care work | Molyneux, Moser |
| Empowerment | Expansion in people's ability to make strategic life choices where this ability was denied: **resources, agency, achievements** | Naila Kabeer (1999); *Reversed Realities* (1994) |
| Feminisation of poverty | Women form a growing share of the poor | Diana Pearce (1978) |
| Double burden / second shift | Women add paid work to unchanged domestic work | Arlie Hochschild, *The Second Shift* (1989) |
| Unpaid care work | Housework and care, uncounted in GDP | Marilyn Waring, *If Women Counted* (1988) |
| Gender mainstreaming | Assess implications for women and men of any planned action | Beijing 1995; ECOSOC 1997 |

**Practical vs strategic: example table (2018 short note).**

| Practical needs | Strategic interests |
|---|---|
| Hand pump near home | Women's say in water committees; men sharing water collection |
| Maternal health clinic | Right to decide on family size; ending child marriage |
| Microcredit for poultry | Joint land titles; equal inheritance enforcement |
| Girls' school | Ending stereotypes in curriculum; women teachers in leadership |
| Women's police desk | Laws against marital rape; women judges and police in senior posts |

Note: practical and strategic are linked — a well-designed practical project can open strategic change (e.g. women's savings groups that become political platforms).

## 5.2 Colonial perspectives of gender (2020, 2022, 2023, 2024)

### Ideas

1. **Civilising mission and "saving" women.** Colonial powers justified rule by claiming to rescue native women from barbaric customs. **Gayatri Spivak**: "white men saving brown women from brown men." **Lord Cromer** in Egypt attacked the veil while opposing women's suffrage at home (Leila Ahmed, 1992).
2. **Orientalism.** **Edward Said** (1978): the West constructed the "Orient" as backward, sensual, passive. Muslim women were pictured as secluded and oppressed (harem imagery).
3. **Colonial knowledge rigidified custom.** British codification of "personal laws" and "customary law" froze fluid practices into rigid rules, often consulting male elites. In Punjab, the Punjab Laws Act 1872 and recorded customs (riwaj-i-am) let agricultural land pass to male agnates, excluding daughters despite Islamic inheritance rules — corrected only in part by the Shariat Act 1937 and later laws.
4. **Land and property.** Revenue settlements registered land in male heads' names; women's informal rights (use, grazing) were lost.
5. **Labour.** Plantation and cash-crop economies drew men into wage labour and left women in subsistence farming; colonial education trained men as clerks.
6. **Sexuality and the body.** The Contagious Diseases Acts (1860s) regulated "prostitutes" around cantonments; colonial law criminalised homosexuality (Section 377, 1860, still in Pakistan's Penal Code) and hijras (Criminal Tribes Act 1871 listed eunuchs).
7. **Reform laws.** Abolition of sati (1829, Lord William Bentinck, with Raja Ram Mohan Roy's campaign); Hindu Widows' Remarriage Act (1856); Age of Consent Act (1891); Child Marriage Restraint Act (Sarda Act, 1929). Ambiguous: some real gains, but they also served colonial claims of superiority, and women were often objects, not subjects, of the debate (Lata Mani, *Contentious Traditions*, 1998).
8. **Nationalist response.** **Partha Chatterjee** (1989): nationalists split the world into the material/outer (where the West could be copied) and the spiritual/inner (home, women) which must stay "pure". Women became symbols of cultural authenticity, limiting their freedom. Muslim reformers promoted educated but domestic "sharif" women.

### Coloniality of gender and "development as new coloniality" (2024)

- **Aníbal Quijano** (2000): "coloniality of power" — colonial hierarchies (race, labour, knowledge) survive after formal independence.
- **María Lugones**, "Heterosexualism and the Colonial/Modern Gender System" (2007): colonialism imposed a binary, patriarchal gender system on societies with different arrangements.
- **Arturo Escobar**, *Encountering Development* (1995): "development" after 1949 (Truman's Point Four speech) constructed the "Third World" as underdeveloped and in need of Western expertise, a new form of control.
- **Chandra Mohanty** (1984): Western feminist development discourse produced a single "Third World woman": poor, ignorant, tradition-bound, victimised.
- **Argument for the thesis**: aid conditionality, donor-set gender agendas, Western indicators, experts from the North, SAP-driven cuts → continuation of colonial dependence.
- **Argument against**: national ownership; South-South cooperation; locally led movements; real gains in health and education; Pakistani women's groups set their own agendas (WAF, Aurat March).
- Balanced answer: development can reproduce coloniality when it is top-down and donor-driven; it can be emancipatory when locally owned and participatory (Arif Hasan, *Participatory Development*, on the FPSC Journalism list; the Orangi Pilot Project).

## 5.3 Capitalistic perspectives of gender (2022, 2023, 2024)

1. **Public/private split.** Industrial capitalism separated home and workplace. Paid work became "men's", unpaid housework "women's"; the "male breadwinner" model and the "family wage".
2. **Unpaid reproductive labour subsidises capital** (Marxist feminists; Dalla Costa and James; Federici). Social reproduction theory (Tithi Bhattacharya, 2017).
3. **Primitive accumulation and witch hunts.** Federici, *Caliban and the Witch* (2004): early capitalism disciplined women's bodies and reproduction.
4. **Reserve army of labour.** Women hired as cheap, flexible labour and dismissed in downturns.
5. **Housewifisation** (Maria Mies, 1986): capitalism defines women as dependent housewives so their income is "supplementary" and their wages can be low, even when they work for global markets (e.g. lace makers of Narsapur in India).
6. **Occupational segregation and wage gap**: feminised sectors (care, garments, teaching) pay less.
7. **Commodification of women's bodies**: advertising, beauty industry, sex trade.
8. **Neoliberal capitalism** (1980s–): flexible, informal, home-based work; care chains; women as entrepreneurs (microcredit) — empowerment or new burden?

**Liberal counter-view.** Markets give women income, independence and bargaining power (Boserup; the World Bank's "smart economics" — investing in women raises growth). Garment work in Bangladesh raised girls' schooling and age at marriage (research by Rachel Heath and Mushfiq Mobarak, 2015). Present both views.

**Pakistan.** Home-based workers (embroidery, bangles, football stitching in Sialkot) paid piece rates without social security; women do most livestock work and cotton picking unpaid or underpaid; garment and textile factories; Baldia factory fire, Karachi (Sept 2012, about 260 dead); laws for home-based workers (Sindh 2018; Punjab [verify year]).

## 5.4 Gender analysis of development theories (2019, 2021, 2024, 2025)

### (a) Modernisation theory

**Core.** Societies move from "traditional" to "modern" through industrialisation, urbanisation, education, secularisation and democracy. **W.W. Rostow**, *The Stages of Economic Growth* (1960): (1) traditional society, (2) preconditions for take-off, (3) take-off, (4) drive to maturity, (5) high mass consumption. Also Talcott Parsons (pattern variables), Daniel Lerner (*The Passing of Traditional Society*, 1958), Alex Inkeles (modern man).

**Gender assumptions.**
- Women are associated with tradition; men with modernity.
- Development will "trickle down" to women automatically.
- Women's subordination is caused by **internal cultural factors** (religion, custom, seclusion) — the 2021 question.
- The Western nuclear family with a male breadwinner is the model.

**Gender critique.**
1. **Ester Boserup**, *Woman's Role in Economic Development* (1970): modernisation often **worsened** women's position. Colonial and development planners gave land titles, technology, extension services and cash crops to men, displacing women from farming roles (especially in Africa's "female farming systems"). This founded WID.
2. Male bias in planning; women's unpaid work invisible in GDP.
3. Blames culture and ignores colonial history and global inequality (dependency critique).
4. Ethnocentric: assumes Western path is universal.
5. Some evidence supports modernisation: education, urbanisation and fertility decline do raise women's status (e.g. Inglehart and Norris, *Rising Tide*, 2003, link modernisation to gender equality values).

**Pakistan.** Green Revolution (1960s) mechanised farming, displacing women's manual roles in some areas; extension services and credit went to male farmers; urban education expanded women's opportunities unevenly.

### (b) Dependency theory

**Core.** Underdevelopment is caused by the periphery's dependent position in the world economy, not by internal culture. **Raúl Prebisch** and Hans Singer (declining terms of trade, 1950); **Andre Gunder Frank**, "The Development of Underdevelopment" (1966); **Samir Amin**; Fernando Henrique Cardoso (dependent development). The core extracts surplus from the periphery through trade, investment and debt.

**Gender analysis.**
- Women of the periphery are doubly exploited: by global capital and by local patriarchy.
- Multinationals relocate labour-intensive production (garments, electronics) to the South, hiring young women as cheap, docile labour (Diane Elson and Ruth Pearson, "Nimble Fingers Make Cheap Workers", 1981).
- Debt and export orientation divert resources from health and education.
- **Strength**: links gender to global inequality (basis of WAD).
- **Critique**: treats women mainly as class/periphery victims; under-theorises patriarchy within households; cannot explain East Asian growth; men dominate its theorists and gender was added late.

### (c) World-system theory (2025)

**Core.** **Immanuel Wallerstein**, *The Modern World-System* (1974): one capitalist world-economy since the 16th century with a **core** (high-wage, high-tech), **semi-periphery** (both; e.g. Brazil, China, India) and **periphery** (raw materials, cheap labour). Unequal exchange moves surplus to the core. States can move between zones.

**Gendered globalisation analysis.**
1. **Household as a unit of the world-system**: Wallerstein and Joan Smith (1992) showed households pool wage and non-wage income (subsistence, unpaid labour). Women's unpaid and informal labour lowers the wage capital must pay — a hidden subsidy.
2. **Feminisation of labour** (Guy Standing, 1989): global competition favours flexible, low-paid, female labour; men's jobs also become "feminised" (insecure).
3. **Global care chains** (Arlie Hochschild, 2000): women from the periphery (Philippines, Sri Lanka) migrate to care for children in the core, leaving their own children with relatives.
4. **Global cities** (Saskia Sassen, 1991): demand for low-paid female service workers in core cities.
5. **Export processing zones and maquiladoras** (US–Mexico border): young women workers, weak unions.
6. **New patriarchal powers** (the 2025 question): corporate and state power over women's labour; fundamentalist backlash as a reaction to globalisation (Valentine Moghadam, *Globalizing Women*, 2005); trafficking and the sex trade; digital platform work; debt.
7. **Maria Mies**: housewifisation on a world scale — the core's consumption depends on cheap labour of periphery women.

**Critique.** Economistic; underplays agency and cultural change; some women gain income and autonomy through export jobs.

**Pakistan.** Semi-peripheral/peripheral position: textile exports (about 60% of exports [verify current share]) rely on low-paid women in stitching and home-based work; Gulf migration of men leaves women managing households (more autonomy, more burden); remittances; women hardly present in high-value sectors.

### (d) Structural functionalism (2019 note)

**Core.** Society is a system of interdependent parts; each institution performs a function for stability (Émile Durkheim; Talcott Parsons; Robert Merton). **Parsons and Bales**, *Family, Socialization and Interaction Process* (1955): the nuclear family has two functions (socialisation of children, stabilisation of adult personalities); the husband plays the **instrumental** role (provider, link to economy), the wife the **expressive** role (care, emotional support). Role differentiation avoids competition and strengthens the family. Also George Murdock (1949): sexual division of labour is universal and practical.

**Gender critique.**
- Treats a 1950s American middle-class family as natural and universal.
- Justifies inequality as "functional"; ignores conflict, power, violence.
- Ignores women's paid work (always common among the poor) and men's emotional roles.
- Cannot explain change.
- Feminists (Oakley, 1974, *Housewife*) showed housework is work and often unsatisfying.
- Still useful: shows how institutions fit together and why gender roles resist change.

**Pakistan.** Mainstream views of the family (man as qawwam/provider; woman as homemaker) resemble functionalist logic; but economic pressure (inflation) pushes more women into paid work, showing the model's limits.

### Summary table: theories in one view (use in the 2024-type question)

| Theory | Cause of underdevelopment | View of women | Gender critique |
|---|---|---|---|
| Modernisation | Internal tradition | Traditional, to be modernised; benefit automatically | Boserup: women lost out; male bias; blames culture |
| Dependency | Exploitation by the core | Exploited periphery labour | Neglects household patriarchy |
| World-system | Unequal exchange in one world-economy | Unpaid/informal labour subsidises capital | Economistic; little agency |
| Structural functionalism | (Social stability theory) | Expressive role in family | Justifies inequality; static |

## 5.5 Gender approaches to development: WID, WAD, GAD (the most repeated question)

### Women in Development (WID) — 1970s

- **Origin.** Boserup (1970); the term was coined by the Women's Committee of the Society for International Development, Washington DC; the **Percy Amendment** (1973) to the US Foreign Assistance Act required US aid to integrate women; USAID's WID office (1974); the UN Decade for Women (1976–85). Grounded in **liberal feminism** and modernisation theory.
- **Assumptions.** Women have been left out of development; integrate them into existing processes. Women are an untapped resource; investing in them increases efficiency.
- **Strategies.** Women-specific projects: income generation, credit, training, education, appropriate technology; women's ministries and units ("national machineries").
- **Moser's policy approaches within WID:** *welfare* (1950s–70s: women as mothers; food aid, nutrition, family planning), *equity* (original WID: equality through state action), *anti-poverty* (income for poor women), *efficiency* (women as efficient contributors, esp. under SAPs), and later *empowerment* (from Southern feminists; leads to GAD).
- **Achievements.** Made women visible in statistics and planning; created women's ministries; funded research.
- **Critique.**
  1. Accepted existing structures; did not question why women were excluded.
  2. Focused on women in isolation, not gender relations; men left out.
  3. Ignored women's reproductive work, so projects added to women's burden.
  4. Treated women as a homogeneous group; ignored class, race, ethnicity.
  5. Women's projects were small, underfunded "add-ons" ("ghettoisation").
  6. Western, modernisation-based; ignored colonial and global causes.
  7. Efficiency logic: women valued for what they produce, not their rights.
  (2026 MCQ: WID was criticised for none of "too radical" or "ignoring women's economic contribution" — its fault was accepting existing structures.)

### Women and Development (WAD) — late 1970s

- **Origin.** Neo-Marxist and dependency thinking; critique of WID at and after the 1975 Mexico City conference; Lourdes Benería and Gita Sen (1981) critiqued Boserup for ignoring capital accumulation and class.
- **Assumptions.** Women have **always** been part of development — the problem is the **unequal terms** of their integration into a global capitalist system that exploits both Third World men and women. Inequality stems from international and class structures.
- **Strategies.** Women-only projects (to avoid male domination), focus on women's productive work, structural change in the international economy, collective organisation.
- **Strengths.** Linked women's subordination to global capitalism and class; recognised women's existing contributions.
- **Critique.**
  1. Focused on class and global structure, under-theorised **patriarchy** and household gender relations.
  2. Also concentrated on productive work, neglecting reproductive work.
  3. Treated women as a uniform group within class.
  4. In practice, many WAD projects resembled WID projects.
  5. Little attention to women's agency.
- **Use in 2026-type question**: WAD warned that adding women to unchanged (global, capitalist) structures depoliticises; this anticipates the critique of donor-led gender mainstreaming and NGO-isation.

### Gender and Development (GAD) — 1980s onward

- **Origin.** Socialist feminism and Southern feminists: **DAWN** (Development Alternatives with Women for a New Era, network launched in Bangalore in 1984 and presented at Nairobi 1985); Gita Sen and Caren Grown, *Development, Crises and Alternative Visions* (1987); Kate Young; Institute of Development Studies, Sussex; Caroline Moser (1993); Naila Kabeer (1994).
- **Assumptions.**
  1. Focus on **gender relations**, not women alone; men included.
  2. Women's subordination results from social relations of gender, class, race, global structures — both production and reproduction.
  3. Women are **agents**, not passive recipients.
  4. The state should promote emancipation.
  5. Holistic: economic, social, political, cultural.
- **Strategies.** Gender analysis; meeting both practical and strategic needs; **empowerment** from below; gender mainstreaming; gender budgeting; legal reform; organising.
- **Tools.** Harvard Analytical Framework (1985: activity profile, access and control profile); Moser framework (triple roles, practical/strategic needs); Kabeer's Social Relations Approach; Longwe's Women's Empowerment Framework (welfare → access → conscientisation → participation → control).
- **Achievements.** Adopted at Beijing (1995) through gender mainstreaming; shaped UNDP's Gender Development Index and Gender Empowerment Measure (1995); gender budgeting in many countries (Pakistan's Gender Responsive Budgeting Initiative, early 2000s).
- **Critique.**
  1. Hard to implement; requires political will to change power relations.
  2. Often reduced in practice to WID-style projects with a new name.
  3. "Gender" can sideline women (men and boys absorb resources).
  4. Mainstreaming became technocratic box-ticking.
  5. Still Western-funded and expert-led.
  6. Limited attention to environment (later WED/ecofeminism) and sexuality.

### WID vs WAD vs GAD — master table

| | WID | WAD | GAD |
|---|---|---|---|
| Period | 1970s | Late 1970s | 1980s– |
| Theory base | Liberal feminism, modernisation | Marxist feminism, dependency | Socialist feminism, post-structuralism |
| Focus | Women | Women and class/global economy | Gender relations (women and men) |
| Problem | Women excluded from development | Women integrated on unequal terms | Unequal power relations at all levels |
| Goal | Efficient, effective development | Fairer international and class structures | Equitable, sustainable development; empowerment |
| Solution | Integrate women into existing processes | Women-only projects; structural change | Transform unequal relations; empower women |
| Strategies | Women's projects, income, credit | Women's organising, productive projects | Gender analysis, mainstreaming, strategic needs |
| View of women | Untapped resource | Exploited workers | Agents of change |
| Needs addressed | Practical | Practical, some productive | Practical and strategic |
| Main critique | Accepts status quo; ignores men, reproduction | Ignores patriarchy | Hard to implement; diluted |

### Pakistani examples

- **WID**: women's development ministry/division (1979), women's vocational centres, First Women Bank (1989), sewing machine schemes, microcredit (Kashf Foundation, 1996).
- **WAD**: critiques of export-led textile growth and of Pakistan's IMF-driven model by labour and left feminist groups; home-based workers' organisation.
- **GAD**: National Policy for Development and Empowerment of Women (2002); Gender Reform Action Plans (GRAP, 2002–05) for mainstreaming in provinces; gender-responsive budgeting; BISP (2008) giving cash to women and linking them to CNICs, bank accounts and voting; reserved seats; NCSW; Punjab Women Empowerment Packages (2012, 2016). Critics note many remain project-based and donor-funded.

### Recent approaches (add for higher marks)

- **Women, Environment and Development (WED)** / ecofeminism (see file 10).
- **Capabilities approach** (Amartya Sen; Martha Nussbaum's ten central capabilities, *Women and Human Development*, 2000).
- **Rights-based approach** (CEDAW, Vienna 1993).
- **"Smart economics"** (World Bank World Development Report 2012: *Gender Equality and Development*) — criticised for instrumentalism.
- **SDG 5** and gender targets across SDGs.

## 5.6 Gender critique of Structural Adjustment Policies (2022)

**What SAPs were.** Loans from the IMF and World Bank in the 1980s–90s debt crisis, conditional on: fiscal austerity (cutting spending and subsidies), user fees for health and education, privatisation of state enterprises, currency devaluation, trade and financial liberalisation, export promotion, labour market deregulation. Later renamed (Poverty Reduction Strategy Papers, 1999; Extended Fund Facilities).

**Gender critique.**
1. **Male bias** (Diane Elson, *Male Bias in the Development Process*, 1991): SAPs assumed that labour moves smoothly from non-tradable to tradable sectors and ignored the unpaid care economy. Cuts did not reduce the need for care; they shifted it to women's unpaid time. Women became the "shock absorbers".
2. **Cuts to health and education + user fees** → girls withdrawn from school first; women delay health care; maternal mortality risk rises.
3. **Removal of food and fuel subsidies** → women, as household managers, cut their own consumption.
4. **Public sector retrenchment** → women lose secure jobs (teaching, health).
5. **Export promotion** → low-wage female jobs in garments and agriculture, poor conditions.
6. **Informalisation** → more women in insecure work without protection.
7. **Increased time poverty and stress**; possible rise in domestic violence.
8. Evidence: UNICEF's **"Adjustment with a Human Face"** (Cornia, Jolly and Stewart, 1987) documented harm to women and children and urged social safety nets.

**Counter-arguments.** Macroeconomic stability eventually benefits everyone; inflation also hurts women; later programmes added social safety nets and protected social spending.

**Pakistan.** Pakistan has had more than 20 IMF programmes since 1958 (the 2024 Extended Fund Facility of about US$7 billion, approved Sept 2024, is commonly called the 24th [verify count]). Effects: electricity and gas price rises, sales tax increases, development spending cuts — hitting women through inflation and service cuts. The response has been to expand **BISP** (women recipients) as a protected social floor, which IMF programmes set as a structural benchmark. Critics say cash transfers do not replace public services.

**Way forward.** Gender impact assessment of IMF/WB conditions; protect health and education budgets; gender-responsive budgeting; count unpaid care (time-use surveys — PBS conducted one in 2007 [verify]); invest in care infrastructure.

## 5.7 Globalisation and gender (2017, 2018, 2019, 2025)

**Definition.** The growing integration of economies, cultures and societies through trade, capital flows, technology, migration and communication.

### Positive effects for women

1. Jobs in export industries and services (garments in Bangladesh — about 4 million workers, mostly women; call centres; IT freelancing — Pakistan is a major freelancing country).
2. Spread of global norms and conventions (CEDAW, Beijing, SDGs); transnational feminist networks.
3. Information and communication: mobile phones, internet, social media; women's online businesses.
4. Migration and remittances can increase women's decision-making (wives of migrants).
5. Access to global markets for women artisans (fair trade).
6. Education and exposure to new ideas; changing attitudes.

### Negative effects (gendered inequalities)

1. **Feminisation of labour** in low-paid, insecure, informal work; global supply chains; Rana Plaza collapse, Bangladesh (2013, over 1,100 dead) and the Baldia fire in Karachi (2012).
2. **Race to the bottom** in wages and labour standards; weak unions in EPZs.
3. **Global care chains** and domestic worker abuse (kafala system in the Gulf).
4. **Trafficking** and sexual exploitation; sex tourism.
5. **Commodification and media**: beauty standards, objectification, fairness creams (South Asia).
6. **Cultural backlash and fundamentalism**: identity politics often target women's dress and mobility.
7. **Agricultural liberalisation** hurts small women farmers; land grabs.
8. **Digital divide** and online harassment.
9. SAPs and debt (above).
10. **Climate change** (a global process) hits women harder (2026 question).

### Theoretical lenses

Liberal (globalisation brings opportunity), Marxist/world-system (exploitation), postcolonial (cultural imperialism and Western feminist dominance), transnational feminism (solidarity across borders, e.g. Mohanty's *Feminism without Borders*, 2003).

### Pakistan

- Textile and garment exports; home-based workers in global value chains (Sialkot footballs, surgical goods).
- Freelancing and digital work open home-based income; mobile internet gender gap fell from 38% (2023) to 25% (2024) per GSMA, as the 2026 paper noted; GSMA's 2026 report put it at 8% for 2025 (press reports).
- Women's labour force participation stays low (about 24% in LFS 2024–25 on the old definition).
- Global media and dramas shift attitudes; also backlash against "Western" culture (Aurat March controversy).
- GSP+ status with the EU (since 2014) ties trade to implementing 27 conventions, including CEDAW — a lever for gender reform.

## 5.8 Ready introductions and conclusions

- *WID/WAD/GAD*: "Development thinking about women has moved from adding women (WID) to questioning the system they were added to (WAD) to transforming the gender relations that shape both (GAD). Pakistan's policies use the language of GAD but are often implemented as WID."
- *Globalisation*: "Globalisation is neither liberator nor oppressor by itself; it widens opportunities for women who have education, mobility and connectivity, and deepens exploitation for those who do not."
