## 6.2 Why Pakistan ranks so low (2018, 2026)

### Pakistan in the Global Gender Gap Report 2025 and 2026

The WEF index (since 2006) measures **gaps** between women and men (not absolute levels) on 14 indicators in four sub-indices: economic participation and opportunity; educational attainment; health and survival; political empowerment. A score of 100% = parity.

**Factors behind the ranking.**

1. **Economic participation (worst area).**
   - Female labour force participation about a quarter of men's rate.
   - Few women in senior roles (legislators, senior officials, managers), technical and professional jobs.
   - Large wage and income gaps; most women in agriculture, often unpaid family workers; informal home-based work.
   - Causes: mobility restrictions and harassment on transport; norms that men are providers; care burden with no childcare; lack of safe transport; skills mismatch; employers' bias.
2. **Educational attainment (improving).**
   - Literacy gap of about 15 points; low enrolment of girls in rural Balochistan, KP and Sindh.
   - Progress: women now match or exceed men in tertiary enrolment, which lifted the 2026 score to a record 91.7%.
   - Causes of the gap: distance to school, lack of female teachers and girls' schools beyond primary, early marriage, poverty, security (attacks on girls' schools in Swat 2008–09 and Gilgit-Baltistan 2018).
3. **Health and survival.**
   - Sex ratio at birth and healthy life expectancy gap; son preference; high maternal mortality; malnutrition and anaemia (NNS 2018: about 41.7% of women of reproductive age anaemic [verify]).
4. **Political empowerment.**
   - Women in parliament mostly through reserved seats; few women ministers; no woman head of state in the last 50 years except Benazir Bhutto as PM (1988–90, 1993–96).
   - Positive: Maryam Nawaz became the first woman Chief Minister of Punjab (February 2024); more women cabinet members in 2024–26 [verify] — this helped the 2026 improvement.

### Why commitments have not translated into results (2018 question)

- Gap between law and implementation (police, courts, parallel jirga justice).
- Devolution after the 18th Amendment (2010) left uneven provincial capacity.
- Low public spending on health (about 1% of GDP) and education (under 2% of GDP) [verify latest Economic Survey].
- Patriarchal social norms (honour, purdah, son preference).
- Poverty, inflation, population growth (2.55% a year, Census 2023).
- Political instability and security problems.
- Weak data and monitoring; time-use and unpaid work not counted.
- Contested interpretation of religion used to resist reforms (e.g. Council of Islamic Ideology opposed the 2016 Punjab Act and the 2025 child marriage law).

### Criticisms of the index (for balance)

- Measures gaps, not levels: a poor country with equal deprivation can score high.
- Narrow indicators (e.g. political empowerment counts only top positions).
- Ignores unpaid work, violence and informal sectors.
- Survey-based items (wage equality perception) can be subjective.
- Yet it is widely cited, tracks change over 20 years, and shapes investor and donor perceptions.

## 6.3 Women's health (2017, 2023, 2024)

### Fathalla's "road to maternal death" (2024)

**Mahmoud Fathalla**, an Egyptian obstetrician and former president of the International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO), argued that maternal deaths are not mainly a medical failure but a social one: societies have not decided that women's lives are worth saving. He described the **"road to maternal death"** as a series of stations [wording of stations varies across sources]:

1. Being born female in a society that gives women **low status** (son preference, less food and education).
2. **Poor health and nutrition** from childhood (anaemia, stunting).
3. **Early and frequent pregnancies** (child marriage, unmet need for family planning).
4. **Unsafe abortion** where safe services are lacking.
5. **No access to skilled care** at delivery (distance, cost, permission from husband or mother-in-law).
6. **Obstetric complications** (haemorrhage, eclampsia, sepsis, obstructed labour) without emergency care → death.

Link with the **"three delays" model** (Deborah Maine and Sereen Thaddeus, 1994): delay in deciding to seek care; delay in reaching a facility; delay in receiving adequate care there.

### Pakistan's women's health problems

| Problem | Detail |
|---|---|
| Maternal mortality | 155 per 100,000 (2023 UN estimate) vs global about 197; Balochistan much higher (PMMS 2019: 298) |
| Births without skilled care | PDHS 2017–18: about 69% of births with a skilled provider, 66% in facilities; lower in rural Balochistan |
| High fertility | TFR 3.6; unmet need for family planning about 17% [verify] |
| Malnutrition | Anaemia and stunting; NNS 2018 |
| Early marriage | Teen pregnancies raise risk; obstetric fistula |
| Mental health | Depression and anxiety high among women (domestic stress, violence) |
| Hepatitis C, TB, breast cancer | Pakistan has among Asia's highest breast cancer rates [verify] |
| Heat and floods | 2022 floods affected about 650,000 pregnant women (UNFPA estimate at the time) |
| Female health workforce | Many women doctors leave practice after marriage ("doctor brides") |
| Social barriers | Need for male permission, purdah, preference for female doctors, distance, cost |

### Programmes

- **Lady Health Worker Programme** (1994): about 90,000–100,000 LHWs delivering family planning and maternal-child health at the doorstep. Praised internationally; workers protested for regular pay.
- Community midwives (2006); Maternal, Newborn and Child Health Programme.
- **Sehat Sahulat Programme / Sehat Card** (health insurance; universal in KP and Punjab at times; scope has changed [verify current]).
- BISP's Benazir Nashonuma (conditional cash for nutrition of pregnant women and young children, 2020).
- National Health Vision 2016–2025; Population Policy; Council of Common Interests' population recommendations (2018).

### Measures to improve (2017: "within available resources")

1. Expand LHWs and community midwives (cheap, effective); regularise pay.
2. Emergency obstetric care within 2 hours of every village; ambulance (Rescue 1122) linkage.
3. Family planning integrated in primary care; engage ulema (Al-Azhar-style fatwas on birth spacing used in Pakistan).
4. Telemedicine to re-employ women doctors from home (e.g. Sehat Kahani).
5. Nutrition: fortification, iron-folic acid, school meals.
6. Enforce minimum marriage age 18.
7. Health spending to at least 3% of GDP over time.
8. Women's health data (repeat PMMS, PDHS).

## 6.4 Women in education

- **Constitution, Article 25-A** (18th Amendment, 2010): free and compulsory education for all children aged 5–16.
- **Gaps**: literacy 52.8% (women) vs 68% (men); rural women far lower; Balochistan women 32.8%.
- **Out-of-school children**: about 25 million, majority girls; dropout rises sharply after primary (few girls' middle and high schools, distance, safety, early marriage).
- **Higher education**: women now about half or more of university enrolment; many in medicine and education; low share in engineering and IT; graduates often do not enter the workforce (high unemployment among educated women — PBS data show about a quarter of highly educated women job-seekers unemployed, reported Feb 2026).
- **Content**: gender stereotypes in textbooks (women as mothers and nurses; men as leaders).
- **Initiatives**: girls' stipends (Punjab Female School Stipend Programme, 2004; Waseela-e-Taleem under BISP), free textbooks, Ehsaas/BISP education conditional cash, Malala Fund projects, Global Summit on Girls' Education in Muslim Communities, Islamabad (January 2025).
- **Barriers**: poverty, child labour, distance, transport, lack of toilets, few female teachers, insecurity, early marriage, social norms, climate disasters (floods destroyed thousands of schools in 2022).

## 6.5 Women and employment (2019)

### Picture

- FLFP about 24% (old definition), among the lowest in the world outside the Middle East.
- About two-thirds of employed women work in **agriculture** (livestock, cotton picking, sowing), often as unpaid family helpers [verify LFS share].
- Others in manufacturing (textiles, garments, home-based work), education, health, domestic work.
- Very few in management: women hold a small share of senior and managerial posts.
- Gender pay gap: among the widest in the world (ILO Global Wage Report has put Pakistan's gap among the highest) [verify figure].
- Unemployment among educated women is high; many leave work after marriage.

### Challenges of paid work + reproductive role (the "double burden")

| Challenge | Detail |
|---|---|
| Double burden / second shift | Paid work added to full housework and care (Hochschild) |
| Lack of childcare | Few daycare centres; laws require them in some workplaces but enforcement weak |
| Maternity protection | Maternity and Paternity Leave Act 2020 (federal: 180 days for the first child, 120 and 90 days for later children; 30 days paternity) [verify]; private sector compliance weak |
| Harassment | At work and on transport; Protection against Harassment of Women at the Workplace Act 2010 (amended 2022) |
| Mobility | Unsafe public transport; family restrictions; Pink bus schemes in some cities |
| Discrimination | In hiring, promotion; assumption that women will leave after marriage |
| Informality | Home-based and domestic workers excluded from labour law (now partly covered by provincial laws) |
| Wage gap | Lower pay for similar work |
| Social stigma | Working women seen as challenging family honour |
| Digital and skills gaps | Lower access to phones, internet, vocational training |

### Policy responses

Federal 10% quota for women in civil service posts (raised in some provinces to 15–20%) [verify current]; harassment ombudspersons (FOSPAH and provincial); maternity laws; Punjab Women Protection and Empowerment initiatives; Home-Based Workers Acts (Sindh 2018; others); women's entrepreneurship schemes (SBP Banking on Equality Policy, 2021, targets women's financial inclusion); digital freelancing programmes (DigiSkills).

### Family law

Muslim Personal Law (Shariat) Application Act 1937 (and West Pakistan 1962 Act); Dissolution of Muslim Marriages Act 1939 (grounds for khula and judicial divorce); **Muslim Family Laws Ordinance 1961** (registration, polygamy needs arbitration council permission, talaq procedure); Family Courts Act 1964; Child Marriage Restraint Act 1929 (minimum age 16 for girls, 18 for boys, still applies in Punjab, KP and Balochistan); **Sindh Child Marriages Restraint Act 2013** (18 for both); **ICT Child Marriage Restraint Act 2025** (18 for both; up to 3 years' rigorous imprisonment for an adult man who marries a child; assented 30 May 2025; the Council of Islamic Ideology called it un-Islamic); Guardians and Wards Act 1890.

### Institutions

NCSW (2000; statutory 2012); provincial commissions on the status of women (Punjab 2014, KP, Sindh, Balochistan); Ministry of Human Rights (federal); Federal Ombudsperson for Protection against Harassment (FOSPAH); provincial ombudspersons; Punjab Women Protection Authority; Violence Against Women Centres; women police stations (since 1994) and gender units; gender-based violence courts (Lahore model court 2017; designated GBV courts nationwide); Women's Parliamentary Caucus; helplines (1043 Punjab women helpline; 1099 federal human rights helpline).

### Human rights system (2020 Q6)

National Commission for Human Rights (Act 2012); Ministry of Human Rights (2015); superior courts' jurisdiction under Articles 184(3) and 199; Federal and provincial ombudsmen; HRCP (non-state, 1987); treaty obligations (ICCPR 2010, ICESCR 2008, CEDAW 1996, CRC 1990, CAT 2010); EU GSP+ conditionality (27 conventions). Weaknesses: delays (over 2 million pending cases nationally [verify]), weak enforcement, police attitudes, parallel jirga justice, lack of legal aid, poor awareness.

### Problems with women and law

1. **Implementation gap**: police reluctance to register FIRs, out-of-court settlements, low conviction rates in rape cases (often cited as under 5% [verify]).
2. **Parallel justice**: jirgas and panchayats order vani, swara and karo-kari.
3. **Forgiveness and compoundability** still used in some violent crimes.
4. **Uneven provincial laws** after devolution (e.g. marriage age differs).
5. **Access to justice**: cost, distance, illiteracy, family pressure.
6. **Contested reforms**: resistance from religious bodies and some parties (e.g. Council of Islamic Ideology's objections).
7. **Inheritance denial** despite law (estimates suggest few women actually receive their share).
