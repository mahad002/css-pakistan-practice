## 7.1 Defining governance

- **World Bank** (*Governance and Development*, 1992): "the manner in which power is exercised in the management of a country's economic and social resources for development."
- **UNDP** (1997): the exercise of economic, political and administrative authority to manage a country's affairs at all levels; it comprises mechanisms, processes and institutions through which citizens and groups articulate interests, exercise legal rights, meet obligations and mediate differences.
- **Good governance** — eight characteristics (UNESCAP): participation, rule of law, transparency, responsiveness, consensus orientation, equity and inclusiveness, effectiveness and efficiency, accountability.
- World Bank's Worldwide Governance Indicators: voice and accountability, political stability, government effectiveness, regulatory quality, rule of law, control of corruption.

### Gender and governance

**Gender-responsive governance** means that institutions are representative of women, accountable to them, and deliver outcomes that address their needs. Three dimensions:
1. **Descriptive representation**: women's presence in decision-making (Hanna Pitkin, *The Concept of Representation*, 1967).
2. **Substantive representation**: acting for women's interests (laws, budgets).
3. **Symbolic representation**: role models change norms.

Key ideas: **critical mass** — Drude Dahlerup (1988), drawing on Rosabeth Moss Kanter (1977), argued that women need about 30% of seats to change institutional culture; Beijing set a 30% target. **Critical actors** (Sarah Childs and Mona Lena Krook, 2009): a few determined women (and men) matter more than numbers. **Politics of presence** (Anne Phillips, 1995). **Gender mainstreaming** and **gender-responsive budgeting** as governance tools.

Why women's participation matters (2017 question: "stabilise democracy and boost the economy"):
- Justice: half the population deserves half the voice.
- Legitimacy and stability: inclusive democracies are more stable; women's participation in peace processes makes agreements more durable (UN Women review of peace processes, 2015).
- Policy outcomes: women legislators prioritise health, education, child welfare, violence laws (e.g. India's panchayat studies by Chattopadhyay and Duflo, 2004 — women-led villages invested more in drinking water).
- Corruption: some studies link more women in government with lower corruption (Dollar, Fisman and Gatti, 2001), though causality is debated.
- Economy: closing gender gaps raises GDP (IMF and McKinsey estimates).
- Pakistan: the Women's Parliamentary Caucus drove the 2010–2016 pro-women laws across party lines.

## 7.2 The suffragist movement (2019 note)

- **Suffrage** = the right to vote. **Suffragists**: campaigners using peaceful, constitutional methods (UK: Millicent Fawcett's NUWSS, 1897). **Suffragettes**: militant wing (Emmeline Pankhurst's WSPU, 1903; window smashing, arson, hunger strikes, force-feeding; the "Cat and Mouse Act" 1913; Emily Davison died at the Epsom Derby, 1913).
- **US**: Seneca Falls (1848); Stanton and Anthony; split over the 15th Amendment (1870) which enfranchised Black men but not women; NAWSA (1890); Alice Paul's National Woman's Party (picketing the White House 1917); **19th Amendment (1920)**.
- **Global milestones**: New Zealand 1893 (first national); Australia 1902 (with limits on Aboriginal people); Finland 1906; Norway 1913; UK 1918/1928; USA 1920; France 1944; Switzerland 1971 (federal level); Saudi Arabia 2015 (municipal).
- **Subcontinent**: Indian women's associations demanded the vote from 1917 (Sarojini Naidu's delegation to Montagu); provinces enfranchised women from 1921 with property qualifications; Government of India Act 1935 expanded female electorate to about 6 million; reserved seats for women in provincial legislatures (1935). **Pakistan** gave universal adult franchise from the start (1947), and women voted in the 1951 Punjab elections and 1970 general election.
- **Significance**: established women as citizens; opened the door to later rights; but the vote alone did not bring equality (second wave).

## 7.3 Gender issues in women as voters

### Problems

| Issue | Pakistan detail |
|---|---|
| **Registration gap** | Women need a CNIC to register. The gap on electoral rolls was about 12.5 million in 2018 [verify]; ECP drives cut it to about 7% (roughly 9–10 million) by 2025 |
| **Turnout gap** | 2024 election: women 42.6%, men 51.6% (FAFEN); women cast about 41% of NA votes |
| **Bans on women voting** | Local agreements among candidates and elders in parts of KP and Punjab (e.g. Lower Dir by-election 2015, annulled by ECP) |
| **Family control** | Male relatives decide how women vote ("biradari" and household voting) |
| **Mobility and security** | Distance to polling stations, need for escorts, fear of violence |
| **Separate polling booths** | Shortage of women polling staff; booths combined in some areas |
| **Lack of information** | Low literacy, little campaign outreach to women |
| **Gendered issues ignored** | Parties rarely target women voters with specific promises |

### Legal and institutional responses

- **Elections Act 2017, Section 9**: the ECP may declare a constituency poll void if women's turnout is below **10%** of total votes, presuming that women were barred by agreement.
- **Section 47**: NADRA and ECP to take special measures where women's registration lags (gender gap above 10%) [verify section number].
- ECP Gender and Disability Electoral Working Group; NADRA mobile registration vans; BISP linked CNIC registration (cash transfers require a CNIC, so millions of women registered).
- ECP's Gender Affairs Wing; women-only polling stations; FAFEN monitoring.

## 7.4 Gender issues in women as candidates

### Barriers

1. **Party tickets**: parties give few general-seat tickets to women, especially in winnable seats.
2. **Money**: campaigns are expensive; women have less control over assets.
3. **Dynastic politics**: women enter mainly through families (wives, daughters of politicians) — both an entry route and a limit.
4. **Electables and biradari**: constituency politics run by male networks.
5. **Violence and harassment**: threats, character assassination, online abuse; murder of politicians (Zille Huma Usman, Punjab minister, 2007; Benazir Bhutto, 2007).
6. **Mobility and time**: care responsibilities; evening meetings in male spaces (dera, hujra).
7. **Cultural norms**: public life seen as unsuitable for women; religious objections.
8. **Media bias**: focus on appearance and family.

### Legal response

- **Elections Act 2017, Section 206**: political parties must give at least **5%** of their general-seat tickets for national and provincial assemblies to women.
- Effect: compliance usually in letter (women given tickets in unwinnable seats). In 2024, 902 women contested 509 of 859 national and provincial constituencies (FAFEN), about double 2018's numbers [figures as reported by Dawn/IGC].
- Proposals: raise to 10–33% and require winnable seats; public funding of women candidates; party gender wings with budgets.

### "Should women pull each other up?" (2020)

- **For**: mentoring and networks counter the "old boys' network"; the "queen bee" syndrome (senior women distancing themselves from juniors) is a product of tokenism; role models raise aspirations; leadership pipelines need active sponsorship.
- **Against / concerns**: risk of perceived favouritism; merit concerns; burden placed on women to fix structural problems; men must also sponsor women.
- **Balanced view**: mentoring is not preferential treatment; it corrects unequal access to networks. Structural reforms (quotas, transparent promotion, childcare) are still needed. Related concepts: **glass ceiling** (invisible barrier to top posts), **glass cliff** (women appointed to lead in crises with higher risk of failure — Michelle Ryan and Alex Haslam, 2005; 2026 MCQ), **sticky floor** (women stuck in low-paid, low-mobility jobs; 2026 MCQ), **leaky pipeline**.

## 7.5 Gender issues in women as representatives (2022)

Problems women legislators face in Pakistan:

1. **Reserved-seat members seen as second-class**: chosen by party leaders from lists, not elected by voters; no constituency, no development funds in some periods; colleagues call them "nominated".
2. **Party discipline** (Article 63-A) limits independent action.
3. **Few leadership posts**: few women chair standing committees or hold major ministries (finance, interior, defence).
4. **Agenda limited to "women's issues"**, or not even that.
5. **Sexist remarks** in assemblies (several reported incidents of male members insulting women colleagues).
6. **Lack of training** and research staff.
7. **Dependence on male relatives** for politics.
8. **Low re-nomination**: women serve one term and vanish.

**Evidence of performance.** Farzana Bari, *Performance Assessment of Women Parliamentarians in Pakistan* (2002; on the FPSC list), and FAFEN's parliamentary reports have found that women members, especially on reserved seats, were often **more active** than men in questions, resolutions and private members' bills [FAFEN repeatedly reports this; verify for the latest assembly]. The **Women's Parliamentary Caucus** (formed in 2008 under Speaker Fehmida Mirza) brought women of all parties together to pass the harassment law (2010), anti-women practices law (2011), acid law (2011), honour and rape laws (2016).

**Notable women in Pakistani politics.** Fatima Jinnah (presidential candidate 1965); Benazir Bhutto (PM 1988–90, 1993–96); Dr Fehmida Mirza (first woman Speaker, 2008–13); Hina Rabbani Khar (first woman Foreign Minister, 2011); Maryam Nawaz (first woman Chief Minister, Punjab, 2024); Sherry Rehman; Dr Shireen Mazari; Begum Nusrat Bhutto; Asma Jahangir (civil society; HRCP).

## 7.6 Impact of political quota in Pakistan (2016, 2021, 2024, 2025)

### Types of quotas (Drude Dahlerup's typology)

1. **Reserved seats** (constitutional/legal): seats only women can fill — Pakistan, India's panchayats (33% since 1992–93).
2. **Legal candidate quotas**: parties must field a share of women — Pakistan's 5% (2017), France's parité law (2000).
3. **Voluntary party quotas**: parties adopt their own — Sweden's Social Democrats, South Africa's ANC.

### History of women's quota in Pakistan (2025 question)

| Year | Provision |
|---|---|
| 1947 | Two women in the first Constituent Assembly (Begum Jahanara Shahnawaz, Begum Shaista Ikramullah) |
| 1954 | Charter of Women's Rights (pushed by women members; endorsed by the Assembly) asked for reserved seats and equal rights [verify] |
| 1956 Constitution | 10 seats reserved for women in the National Assembly (5 each for East and West Pakistan), filled through special women's territorial constituencies, for 10 years [verify exact mechanism]; women could also contest general seats |
| 1962 Constitution | 6 reserved seats (3 per wing), elected indirectly |
| 1973 Constitution | 10 reserved seats in a 200-member NA, for 10 years or three general elections; 5% in provincial assemblies |
| 1985 | Zia's Revival of Constitution of 1973 Order raised reserved NA seats to 20 |
| 1988–1990 | The quota lapsed after the 1988 elections (the sunset clause expired); the 1990, 1993 and 1997 assemblies had no reserved women's seats (women won only a handful of general seats) |
| 2000 | Devolution of Power Plan (local government ordinances 2001): **33% of seats reserved for women** in union, tehsil and district councils |
| 2002 | Legal Framework Order: **60 of 342 NA seats** (about 17.5%), **17 Senate seats** and **128** provincial seats reserved for women, allocated to parties by proportional representation based on general seats won |
| 2010 | 18th Amendment retained reserved seats |
| 2017 | **Elections Act**: 5% of party tickets to women (s. 206); 10% turnout rule (s. 9) |
| 2018 | 25th Amendment (FATA merger): NA reduced to 336 seats with **60 women's seats**; KP women's seats rose with merged districts. Provincial women's seats now: Punjab 66, Sindh 29, KP 26, Balochistan 11 (total 132) |
| Local governments | Provincial laws after 2013 reduced women's share in many councils (often below 33%), varying by province [verify current] |

Senate: 17 of 96 seats reserved for women (4 per province + 1 for Islamabad).

### Arguments for quotas (pros)

1. Fast-track to representation (Dahlerup's "fast track" vs Nordic "incremental track").
2. Compensates for structural barriers (money, networks, norms).
3. Critical mass to change agenda and culture.
4. Role models for girls and voters.
5. International commitments (CEDAW Article 4 permits temporary special measures; Article 7 on political life; Beijing's 30% target).
6. Pakistan evidence: women's numbers rose from 2 elected women (1997) [verify] to over 70 in the NA after 2002; the Women's Caucus and the pro-women laws of 2006–2016 followed.

### Arguments against (cons)

1. Undermines merit; tokenism.
2. Reserved-seat members accountable to party leaders, not voters (indirect election through party lists).
3. Elite capture: seats go to relatives of party leaders, urban upper-class women.
4. Ghettoisation: parties use reserved seats as an excuse not to field women on general seats.
5. Can stigmatise women legislators.
6. Treats women as a single group (essentialism).

### Assessing the impact in Pakistan (2021: "is the quota showing results?")

**Positive:**
- Visibility and numbers: about 17–20% women in the NA since 2002 (vs under 3% in the 1990s).
- Legislative output: women legislators led or co-led most pro-women laws (2006–2016, 2020–2026).
- Women's Parliamentary Caucus: cross-party cooperation.
- Normalisation of women in public office; women ministers, Speaker, Foreign Minister, Chief Minister.
- Local quotas in 2001–2005 brought about 36,000 women councillors into politics [verify], many from rural and working-class backgrounds.

**Limits:**
- Women elected on general seats remain few (around a dozen in the NA) [verify 2024 count].
- Reserved seats chosen by party leaders; elite and family-based.
- No constituency and weak resources.
- Little influence over budgets, security, finance.
- Party tickets rule (5%) followed only in letter.
- Quotas did not reach cabinet and committee leadership.

**Reforms suggested:**
1. Direct election to reserved seats (e.g. rotating women-only constituencies, as in India's panchayats, or dual-member constituencies).
2. Raise party-ticket quota to 10–33% and require tickets in winnable seats.
3. Quotas for committee chairs and cabinet posts.
4. Restore 33% women's seats in all local governments.
5. Campaign finance support and anti-harassment rules in assemblies.
6. Training through the Pakistan Institute for Parliamentary Services (PIPS).

### Quotas in comparative perspective

- **Rwanda**: 30% constitutional quota (2003); women about 60% of the lower house — world leader.
- **India**: 33% in panchayats (73rd and 74th amendments, 1992); Women's Reservation Act (106th Amendment, 2023) reserves one-third of Lok Sabha and state assembly seats, to take effect after the next census and delimitation.
- **Bangladesh**: 50 reserved seats in a 350-seat parliament.
- **Nordic countries**: voluntary party quotas.
- **IPU (2025)**: women hold about 27% of seats in national parliaments worldwide [verify].

## 7.7 Is political participation a human right? (2024)

Yes:
- **UDHR** (1948) Article 21: right to take part in government and equal access to public service.
- **ICCPR** (1966) Article 25: right to vote, be elected and access public service without discrimination; Article 3: equal rights of men and women.
- **CEDAW** Article 7: equality in voting, standing for election, formulating policy, holding office; Article 4: temporary special measures (quotas) are not discrimination.
- **Constitution of Pakistan**: Article 17 (freedom of association, forming parties), Article 25, Article 34.
- Convention on the Political Rights of Women (1952).
- Beijing Platform, critical area G: women in power and decision-making.
- SDG 5.5: women's full participation and equal opportunities for leadership.
