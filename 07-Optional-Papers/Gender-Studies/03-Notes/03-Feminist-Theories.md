# 3. Feminist Theories and Practice

Syllabus section III. Asked in 6 of 11 papers: "Radical vs socialist feminism" (2016), "Which feminism suits Pakistan?" (2018), "Postmodern feminism" note (2019), "Marxist/socialist feminism" and "Psychoanalytic feminism" (2021), "Any two Western schools" (2022), "Radical feminism" note (2024). Standard textbook: Rosemarie Tong, *Feminist Thought* (FPSC list).

Present each school neutrally: what it says, who said it, what it wants, its critics. The examiner rewards balance, not advocacy.

## 3.1 What is feminism?

**Definition.** Feminism is a body of thought and a set of social movements that hold that women have been systematically disadvantaged because of their sex, and that seek equal rights, opportunities and dignity for women.

Useful definitions:
- bell hooks, *Feminism Is for Everybody* (2000): "a movement to end sexism, sexist exploitation, and oppression."
- Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie, *We Should All Be Feminists* (2014): a feminist is a person who believes in the social, political and economic equality of the sexes.

**Shared ground of all schools.** (1) Women are subordinated; (2) this is not natural or inevitable; (3) it should change. They disagree on **causes** (law? patriarchy? capitalism? the unconscious? language?) and **remedies** (reform? revolution? separatism? deconstruction?).

**Origin of the word.** "Féminisme" appeared in 19th-century France; Charles Fourier is often credited (1837) [verify]. In English it spread in the 1890s.

**Feminism as theory and practice (praxis).** Theory explains oppression; practice is activism: campaigns, law reform, consciousness-raising groups, shelters, NGOs, protests (e.g. WAF in 1983, Aurat March since 2018).

### Summary table (memorise)

| School | Root cause of oppression | Key thinkers | Remedy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Liberal | Legal and customary barriers; unequal opportunity | Wollstonecraft, J.S. Mill, Harriet Taylor, Friedan | Equal rights, education, law reform, within existing system |
| Radical | Patriarchy itself, especially control of women's bodies and sexuality | Millett, Firestone, Daly, Dworkin, MacKinnon, Rich | Transform or overthrow patriarchy; women-only spaces; reproductive control |
| Marxist | Capitalism and private property; class | Engels, Benston, Dalla Costa and James | Socialist revolution; socialise housework |
| Socialist | Capitalism **and** patriarchy together | Juliet Mitchell, Heidi Hartmann, Iris Young, Alison Jaggar, Zillah Eisenstein | Change both economic and gender systems |
| Psychoanalytic | Early childhood and the unconscious; family structure; language (Lacan) | Freud's critics, Horney, Dinnerstein, Chodorow, Mitchell; Irigaray, Cixous, Kristeva | Dual parenting; new symbolic order; women's writing |
| Men's (pro-feminist) | Patriarchy harms women and men | J.S. Mill, Connell, Kimmel, Messner, hooks | Men as allies; change masculinities |
| Postmodern | Fixed categories and grand narratives; language and discourse | Cixous, Irigaray, Kristeva, Butler, Haraway, Spivak, Mohanty | Deconstruct binaries; recognise difference |

## 3.2 Liberal feminism

**Roots.** Enlightenment liberalism: reason, individual rights, equality before law.

**Key thinkers and texts.**
- **Mary Wollstonecraft**, *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman* (1792): women appear inferior only because they are denied education; they are rational beings and should be educated as such.
- **John Stuart Mill**, *The Subjection of Women* (1869) (and **Harriet Taylor Mill**, "The Enfranchisement of Women", 1851): the legal subordination of women is wrong and a barrier to human progress; demanded suffrage and equal property rights. Mill presented a women's suffrage petition to Parliament in 1866.
- **Betty Friedan**, *The Feminine Mystique* (1963): "the problem that has no name" — the dissatisfaction of educated American housewives confined to the home. Co-founded the **National Organization for Women (NOW, 1966)**. Later, *The Second Stage* (1981) urged men and women to share family life.
- Contemporary: Martha Nussbaum (capabilities approach), Susan Moller Okin, *Justice, Gender, and the Family* (1989).

**Main claims.**
1. Women and men have the same rational capacity.
2. Subordination comes from legal and customary barriers, not nature.
3. Remedy: equal rights in law, education, voting, employment, property; equal opportunity.
4. Reform within the existing system (democracy, market).
5. Public/private distinction: the state should guarantee equality in public life.

**Achievements.** Suffrage, married women's property acts, equal pay laws (US Equal Pay Act 1963; UK Equal Pay Act 1970), Title VII of the US Civil Rights Act 1964, anti-discrimination law, access to universities and professions. Internationally, CEDAW (1979) is largely liberal in spirit.

**Critiques.**
- Radicals: it treats symptoms; leaves patriarchy, family and sexuality untouched ("the personal is political").
- Marxists: ignores class; benefits middle-class, white, educated women (Friedan ignored working women and women of colour — bell hooks).
- Postmodern and Black feminists: assumes a universal "woman" and a male norm ("equality = becoming like men").
- Communitarians: too individualist; ignores care and relationships (Gilligan's ethic of care).

**Pakistan.** Most Pakistani reforms are liberal in form: Article 25 equality clause, reserved seats, the harassment law 2010, the honour-crime law 2016, women's property rights law 2020, scholarships and quotas in jobs (e.g. 10% quota for women in federal services [verify current percentage]). APWA's welfare and education work fits liberal feminism.

## 3.3 Radical feminism

**Context.** Late 1960s US, from women in the civil rights and New Left movements who found sexism inside those movements.

**Key thinkers and texts.**
- **Kate Millett**, *Sexual Politics* (1970): patriarchy is the most pervasive ideology of our culture; sex is a status category with political implications; patriarchy is kept by socialisation, family, religion, literature and force.
- **Shulamith Firestone**, *The Dialectic of Sex* (1970): the root of oppression is biological reproduction; liberation needs technology (artificial reproduction) to free women from childbearing.
- **Carol Hanisch**, "The Personal Is Political" (1969, published 1970): private problems (housework, sex, violence) are political.
- **Mary Daly**, *Gyn/Ecology* (1978); **Andrea Dworkin**, *Pornography* (1981); **Catharine MacKinnon**, *Toward a Feminist Theory of the State* (1989) — sexuality is to feminism what work is to Marxism; sexual harassment as discrimination.
- **Adrienne Rich**, *Of Woman Born* (1976); "Compulsory Heterosexuality and Lesbian Existence" (1980).
- **Susan Brownmiller**, *Against Our Will* (1975): rape as a means of keeping all women in fear.

**Main claims.**
1. Patriarchy is the oldest, most universal form of oppression; all men benefit from it.
2. Oppression operates through the body: sexuality, reproduction, violence, beauty norms, pornography.
3. The personal is political: family and sexuality are sites of power.
4. Liberal reform cannot work because institutions themselves are patriarchal.
5. Consciousness-raising groups help women see shared oppression.

**Two strands (Tong).**
- **Radical-libertarian**: gender roles constrain everyone; aim at androgyny; sexual freedom; reproductive technology liberates (Firestone; early Millett).
- **Radical-cultural**: women's nature (care, connection, peace) is better; value femininity; critique of pornography and male sexuality; sometimes separatism (Daly, Dworkin, MacKinnon).

**Practice.** Rape crisis centres, women's shelters (first modern refuge: Chiswick, London, 1971, Erin Pizzey), anti-pornography campaigns, Take Back the Night marches, the concept of sexual harassment, marital rape laws, abortion rights.

**Critiques.**
- Essentialism: treats all men as oppressors and all women as one class.
- Ignores race and class (Black feminists, Marxists).
- Biological determinism (Firestone).
- Separatism is impractical.
- Anti-pornography stance criticised by "sex-positive" feminists (1980s "sex wars").

**Pakistan.** Radical ideas underlie campaigns on violence, bodily autonomy and sexual harassment, and the Aurat March slogan "Mera Jism, Meri Marzi" (my body, my choice), which triggered a national controversy in 2019–2020. Critics in Pakistan see radical feminism as clashing with family and religious values; supporters say it names violence that liberal reform ignored.

## 3.4 Marxist and socialist feminism

### Marxist feminism

**Roots.** **Friedrich Engels**, *The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State* (1884): with the rise of private property, men sought to pass wealth to legitimate heirs, so they controlled women's sexuality through monogamous marriage — "the world-historic defeat of the female sex". Women's oppression will end when private property ends and women enter public production.

**Later thinkers.** August Bebel, *Woman and Socialism* (1879); Alexandra Kollontai (Soviet Union); **Margaret Benston**, "The Political Economy of Women's Liberation" (1969) — housework as unpaid production; **Mariarosa Dalla Costa and Selma James**, *The Power of Women and the Subversion of the Community* (1972); the **Wages for Housework** campaign (1972–) and **Silvia Federici**, "Wages Against Housework" (1975), *Caliban and the Witch* (2004).

**Main claims.**
1. Women's oppression comes from class society and capitalism.
2. Unpaid domestic labour reproduces labour power for free (social reproduction), subsidising capital.
3. Women form a "reserve army of labour", drawn into work when needed and pushed out when not.
4. Remedy: socialism; socialised childcare and housework; women in paid production.

**Critique.** Reduces gender to class; cannot explain patriarchy in socialist states or before capitalism; ignores sexuality and violence ("sex-blind").

### Socialist feminism

**Idea.** Neither class nor patriarchy alone explains oppression. They interact.

- **Juliet Mitchell**, "Women: The Longest Revolution" (1966) and *Woman's Estate* (1971): four structures — production, reproduction, sexuality, socialisation of children. All must change.
- **Heidi Hartmann**, "The Unhappy Marriage of Marxism and Feminism" (1979): **dual-systems theory** — capitalism and patriarchy are separate systems in partnership (e.g. the "family wage" deal between male workers and capitalists kept women dependent).
- **Iris Marion Young** (1980): **unified-systems theory** — one system, "capitalist patriarchy", where gender division of labour is central.
- **Zillah Eisenstein**, *Capitalist Patriarchy and the Case for Socialist Feminism* (1979).
- **Alison Jaggar**, *Feminist Politics and Human Nature* (1983): alienation of women in sexuality, motherhood, intellect.
- **Maria Mies**, *Patriarchy and Accumulation on a World Scale* (1986): "housewifisation" — capitalism defines women as housewives to cheapen their labour, globally.

### Marxist vs socialist feminism

| | Marxist | Socialist |
|---|---|---|
| Main cause | Capitalism/class | Capitalism and patriarchy together |
| Patriarchy | A by-product of class | An independent (or fused) system |
| Remedy | Socialist revolution | Change both economic and gender structures, incl. family and sexuality |
| Influence | Wages for Housework; social reproduction theory | Care economy, unpaid work measurement, GAD approach |

### Radical vs socialist feminism (asked 2016)

| | Radical | Socialist |
|---|---|---|
| Root | Patriarchy (sex class) | Capitalism + patriarchy |
| Focus | Body, sexuality, violence, reproduction | Work (paid and unpaid), class, the state, also family |
| Men | Beneficiaries of patriarchy as a class | Working men are also exploited; alliance possible |
| Strategy | Consciousness-raising, women-only organising, cultural change | Coalition with labour movements, policy on childcare and wages |
| Relevance to development | Violence and bodily autonomy agendas | GAD, Moser's triple roles, unpaid care |

**Pakistan.** The Women Democratic Front (founded 2018), Lady Health Workers' unions and home-based workers' movements (e.g. HomeNet Pakistan; laws for home-based workers in Sindh 2018 and Punjab) reflect socialist-feminist concerns: wages, social protection and unpaid work. Pakistan's women do most unpaid care work and are concentrated in low-paid agriculture.

## 3.5 Psychoanalytic feminism (asked 2021)

**Starting point: Freud.** Freud's theory of psychosexual development (Oedipus complex, castration anxiety, **penis envy**) explained femininity as a response to lack. Feminists attacked this (Friedan, Millett) as male bias, but some used psychoanalysis to explain why gender is so deeply rooted in the psyche.

**Key thinkers.**
- **Karen Horney** (1920s–30s): rejected penis envy; proposed "womb envy" (men envy women's ability to create life) and cultural causes of female inferiority feelings.
- **Clara Thompson**: women envy men's social power, not their anatomy.
- **Dorothy Dinnerstein**, *The Mermaid and the Minotaur* (1976): because women alone raise infants, both sexes come to fear and resent female power; men flee into public life, women are kept out.
- **Nancy Chodorow**, *The Reproduction of Mothering* (1978): girls, mothered by a same-sex parent, develop relational selves and want to mother; boys define themselves by separating from the mother, so they become emotionally distant and devalue the feminine. Remedy: **dual parenting**.
- **Juliet Mitchell**, *Psychoanalysis and Feminism* (1974): read Freud as a description (not a recommendation) of how patriarchy is internalised.
- **Carol Gilligan**, *In a Different Voice* (1982): against Kohlberg's moral stages (built on boys), women reason with an **ethic of care**, not only an ethic of justice. (Often grouped with psychoanalytic/cultural feminism.)
- **French (Lacanian) feminists**: Lacan said the child enters the "Symbolic Order" (language, law) under the "Law of the Father". **Luce Irigaray** (*Speculum of the Other Woman*, 1974; *This Sex Which Is Not One*, 1977) — women are defined as the mirror of men and need their own language; **Hélène Cixous** ("The Laugh of the Medusa", 1975) — **écriture féminine** (women's writing from the body; 2025 MCQ); **Julia Kristeva** — the "semiotic" (pre-verbal, maternal) disrupts the "symbolic".

**Main claim.** Men's need to dominate and women's subordination are reproduced in early childhood and in language, below the level of law. So legal reform is not enough.

**Critiques.** Unfalsifiable; based on Western nuclear families with full-time mothers; may naturalise difference; ignores class, race and culture; little practical programme. Joint-family systems in Pakistan, where many women raise children, do not fit Chodorow's model neatly.

**Uses.** Explains internalised oppression (why women may uphold patriarchy — compare Kandiyoti's "patriarchal bargain"), why gender identity feels natural, and supports policies for shared parenting (paternity leave).

## 3.6 Men's feminism (pro-feminist men)

**Meaning.** Men who support feminist goals, and the study of how patriarchy shapes men. Also called pro-feminist men's movement.

**History.**
- 19th century: **J.S. Mill**; **Frederick Douglass** spoke at Seneca Falls (1848) in support of the suffrage resolution.
- In South Asia: social reformers such as **Raja Ram Mohan Roy** (against sati), Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar (widow remarriage), **Sir Syed Ahmad Khan** (cautious about girls' schooling, but his circle produced reformers), **Sheikh Abdullah** of Aligarh (Aligarh women's school, 1906), **Maulvi Mumtaz Ali**, *Huquq-e-Niswan* (1898), **Allama Iqbal**'s mixed but respectful views; **Quaid-i-Azam** (1944 Aligarh speech: no nation can rise unless women are side by side with men).
- 1970s–80s: men's consciousness-raising groups; **NOMAS** (National Organization for Men Against Sexism, US); the White Ribbon Campaign (Canada, 1991) after the 1989 Montreal massacre.
- Academic: **R.W. Connell** (hegemonic masculinity), **Michael Kimmel**, **Michael Messner**, Jeff Hearn.
- Policy: **UN Women's HeForShe** campaign (2014, launched by Emma Watson); engaging men and boys in Cairo ICPD (1994) and Beijing (1995) documents.

**Arguments for.** Patriarchy also harms men (emotional suppression, violence, early death, pressure to provide); men hold power, so change needs them; boys learn from male role models.

**Debates.** Can men be feminists or only allies? (Alice Jardine and Paul Smith, *Men in Feminism*, 1987). Risk that men take over the space or seek credit. Distinguish from the **men's rights movement**, which often opposes feminism.

**Pakistan.** Male allies have joined WAF and Aurat March; the honour-crime and anti-harassment laws were passed with male legislators' support. But male resistance is strong; engaging men through religious leaders and community elders is a strategy NGOs use.

## 3.7 Postmodern feminism (2019 note)

**Background.** Postmodernism rejects universal truths and "grand narratives" (Jean-François Lyotard, *The Postmodern Condition*, 1979). Jacques Derrida's **deconstruction** shows that binaries (man/woman, reason/emotion) privilege one term. Foucault: power works through discourse and knowledge.

**Thinkers.** Cixous, Irigaray, Kristeva (French feminists, also psychoanalytic); **Judith Butler** (gender performativity); **Donna Haraway**, "A Cyborg Manifesto" (1985) — blurs human/machine and natural/artificial boundaries; **Linda Nicholson** (ed.), *Feminism/Postmodernism* (1990); postcolonial feminists **Chandra Talpade Mohanty**, "Under Western Eyes" (1984) — Western feminists portray "Third World women" as one victimised group; **Gayatri Spivak**, "Can the Subaltern Speak?" (1988) and **strategic essentialism** — groups may temporarily act as if their identity is fixed to win political goals (2026 MCQ).

**Main claims.**
1. No single, universal "woman": women differ by class, race, religion, sexuality, nation.
2. Gender and even sex are constructed in language and discourse.
3. Reject single explanations of oppression (patriarchy, capitalism).
4. Deconstruct binaries; celebrate difference and plurality.
5. Knowledge is situated and partial.

**Strengths.** Gives voice to non-Western and marginalised women; links to intersectionality; useful for analysing media and law.

**Critiques.** Relativism weakens political action ("if there is no woman, who does feminism represent?"); obscure language; ignores material conditions (Nussbaum); elitist and academic.

**Pakistan.** Helps critique both Western "saviour" narratives about Pakistani women and local essentialist narratives. Supports recognising diversity (rural Sindhi peasant women, Hazara women in Quetta, Christian sanitary workers, Kalash women).

## 3.8 Which feminism suits Pakistan? (2018) — a balanced answer frame

1. No single school; a **contextual, plural approach**.
2. **Liberal**: most acceptable and effective so far — law reform, education, quotas, harassment law.
3. **Socialist**: essential for rural and working women — wages, land rights, social protection (BISP), unpaid care.
4. **Radical** insights: necessary on violence, honour crimes, bodily integrity, but its framing meets resistance.
5. **Postcolonial/Islamic feminism**: rights argued from within Islamic sources (Asma Barlas, Riffat Hassan, Fatima Mernissi), which wins wider social legitimacy; Quaid's vision; Article 2A with Articles 25, 34.
6. Conclusion: a combination — rights-based (liberal), economic justice (socialist), culturally rooted (Islamic/postcolonial) — with radical attention to violence.
