## 10.1 Intersectionality (2025, 2026)

### Origin

- **Kimberlé Crenshaw**, "Demarginalizing the Intersection of Race and Sex" (1989) and "Mapping the Margins" (1991). Her example: *DeGraffenreid v. General Motors* (1976) — Black women could not prove discrimination because GM hired Black men (in factory jobs) and white women (in office jobs). The law looked at race or sex separately, so Black women fell through the gap. Metaphor: a traffic intersection where harms from several directions collide.
- Forerunners: Sojourner Truth, "Ain't I a Woman?" (1851); **Combahee River Collective Statement** (1977) on "interlocking oppressions"; Audre Lorde; bell hooks; Deborah King's "multiple jeopardy" (1988).
- **Patricia Hill Collins**, *Black Feminist Thought* (1990): the **matrix of domination** — race, class, gender, sexuality, nation organised across structural, disciplinary, hegemonic and interpersonal domains (2026 MCQ; the listed options were Kandiyoti, Davis and hooks, so the key was "None of these").
- **Elisabeth Schüssler Fiorenza** coined **kyriarchy** (1992): interlocking structures of domination (gender, race, class, etc.), broader than patriarchy (2026 MCQ).
- Nira Yuval-Davis: intersectionality and belonging; Leslie McCall's three approaches (anti-categorical, intra-categorical, inter-categorical, 2005).

### Core claims

1. Identities (gender, class, race/ethnicity, caste, religion, sexuality, disability, age, location) are not separate; they combine to produce unique experiences of privilege and oppression.
2. Oppression is not additive (gender + class), but multiplicative and qualitatively different.
3. Policies designed for the "average woman" (often urban, middle-class, majority religion) miss the most marginalised.
4. Analysis must look at structures (laws, markets, institutions), not just individual identities.

### Critiques

- Can fragment movements into ever-smaller identity groups.
- Hard to operationalise in data and policy.
- Origin in US race politics; must be adapted (caste, sect, ethnicity, rural/urban in South Asia).
- Some argue class should remain central (Marxist critique).

### Pakistan applications (2026 Q7: rural, minority, disabled women)

|---|---|---|---|---|

| **Religious minorities** (Hindu, Christian, Ahmadi, Sikh) | Gender + religion + often caste/class (Christian sanitary workers, scheduled-caste Hindus in Sindh) | Discrimination in public facilities [anecdotal; verify] | Textbook content alienating; harassment; dropout | Sanitation jobs advertised "for non-Muslims"; bonded labour in brick kilns |
| | Forced conversion and marriage of girls, especially Hindu girls in Sindh (HRCP and other reports) | | | |
| **Ethnic minorities** (Hazara women in Quetta, Baloch, Pashtun in conflict zones) | Gender + ethnicity + security | Movement restricted by sectarian attacks | Insecurity affects schooling | Restricted markets |
| **Women with disabilities** | Gender + disability + poverty | Inaccessible clinics; reproductive health ignored | Few inclusive schools; disability-specific institutions limited | Disability job quota (federal 2–3% [verify]) rarely met; double discrimination; higher risk of abuse |

**Policy implications.** Disaggregated data (by sex, disability, location, religion); targeted programmes (BISP's targeting by poverty score; disability-inclusive schools); minority women's representation (reserved minority seats rarely go to women); legal protection against forced conversion (a Sindh bill was rejected in 2016 and 2019 [verify]); Pakistan ratified the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (2011) and passed the ICT Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act 2020.

## 10.2 Ecofeminism and feminist environmentalism (2026 Q5)

### Ecofeminism

- Term coined by **Françoise d'Eaubonne**, *Le Féminisme ou la Mort* (1974).
- Core idea: the domination of women and the domination of nature are connected; both come from patriarchal, dualist thinking (man/woman, culture/nature, reason/emotion) and from capitalist development.
- **Cultural (spiritual) ecofeminism**: women have a special closeness to nature (through biology, care); celebrates "the feminine principle" (Mary Daly; Susan Griffin; Starhawk).
- **Social/socialist ecofeminism**: the link is historical and economic, not biological (Carolyn Merchant, *The Death of Nature*, 1980; Val Plumwood, *Feminism and the Mastery of Nature*, 1993).
- **Vandana Shiva**, *Staying Alive: Women, Ecology and Development* (1988): Western "maldevelopment" destroys nature and marginalises women's knowledge; celebrates the **Chipko movement** (1973, Uttarakhand, India — women hugging trees to stop logging). **Maria Mies and Vandana Shiva**, *Ecofeminism* (1993).
- Critiques: essentialism (women as "naturally" closer to nature); romanticises rural women's burdens; ignores class and caste differences among women (Bina Agarwal's critique).

### Feminist environmentalism / feminist political ecology

- **Bina Agarwal**, "The Gender and Environment Debate: Lessons from India" (1992): women's relationship with nature comes from their **material reality** — the gender division of labour and property (they collect water, fuel and fodder), not their biology. So environmental degradation hits women harder because of their roles and lack of land rights. Remedy: land rights, voice in resource management.
- Feminist political ecology (Dianne Rocheleau et al., 1996): gendered knowledge, rights and organising around resources.
- Women, Environment and Development (WED) approach in development (1980s–90s; Agenda 21 chapter 24 at the Rio Earth Summit 1992).

### Gendered impacts of climate change in Pakistan

**Context.** Pakistan is among the countries most vulnerable to climate change (Germanwatch's Climate Risk Index ranked it among the most affected in 2022; Global Climate Risk Index 2025 placed Pakistan first for 2022 impacts [verify]). The **2022 floods** submerged about a third of the country, affected about 33 million people, killed over 1,700 and caused about US$30 billion in damage and losses (Post-Disaster Needs Assessment). Further monsoon floods hit in 2025 (especially Punjab and KP) [verify scale]. Heatwaves (Jacobabad and Sindh among the world's hottest places; 2024 and 2025 heatwaves).

| Impact | How women are affected differently |
|---|---|
| Mortality and injury | Disasters often kill more women (mobility limits, inability to swim, waiting for male permission, clothing) — evidence from the 1991 Bangladesh cyclone; Pakistan data limited |
| Maternal health | About 650,000 pregnant women in flood areas in 2022 (UNFPA); births in camps without skilled care |
| Menstrual and sanitation | No privacy or sanitary materials in camps; infections |
| GBV | Increased harassment, early marriage as a coping strategy ("flood brides" reported in Sindh after 2022), trafficking risks in camps |
| Workload | More time collecting water and fuel; caring for the sick; livestock losses hit women's assets |
| Livelihoods | Women farm workers lose wages; cotton pickers exposed to extreme heat (heat stress affects pregnancies) |
| Assets and relief | Relief and compensation registered to male heads with CNICs; women without CNICs excluded (BISP's women-centred lists helped) |
| Education | Girls more likely to drop out after schools are destroyed |
| Migration | Men migrate for work; women left managing farms without land titles ("feminisation of agriculture") |
| Voice | Few women in disaster management authorities and climate negotiations |

**Applying the theories.** *Ecofeminist reading*: floods and heat are products of extractive, patriarchal-capitalist development; women's ecological knowledge (seed saving, water management) is ignored. *Feminist environmentalist reading* (Agarwal): women suffer more because of their roles, poverty and lack of land and voice — the remedy is rights and representation, not celebration of women's "nature". The second reading usually fits Pakistan's data better; use both and conclude with your judgement.

**Policy.** National Climate Change Policy (2012, updated 2021) and Pakistan's Climate Change Gender Action Plan (ccGAP, launched 2022 with IUCN [verify]); gender-responsive disaster management (NDMA's Gender and Child Cell, created 2010); women's CNICs for relief; women in disaster committees; climate-resilient health; Living Indus Initiative (2022).

## 10.3 Gender and language (2020 Q4)

- **Grammatical gender**: Urdu, Arabic and French assign gender to nouns and verbs; English has natural gender in pronouns.
- **Androcentric language**: "man" and "mankind" as generic; "chairman", "policeman"; male as norm, female as marked ("lady doctor", "woman pilot").
- **Dale Spender**, *Man Made Language* (1980): men controlled meaning; language reproduces male dominance.
- **Robin Lakoff**, *Language and Woman's Place* (1975): "women's language" features (hedges, tag questions, polite forms) signal powerlessness. Critiqued by later research.
- **Deborah Tannen**, *You Just Don't Understand* (1990): "difference" model — men's "report talk" vs women's "rapport talk". Critics say it ignores power.
- **Deficit, dominance, difference, and dynamic/social constructionist approaches** — four models of gender and language.
- **Insults and sayings**: many abuses in Urdu and Punjabi target mothers and sisters; proverbs justify control ("aurat ki aqal..."-type sayings); calling a weak man "choorian pehen lo" (wear bangles) equates femininity with weakness.
- **Naming**: women take husbands' names; addressing a wife as "the mother of X" (making her identity relational).
- **Reform**: gender-neutral terms (chairperson, police officer, firefighter); UN gender-inclusive language guidelines (2019); "they" as singular pronoun (contested). Pakistan: textbooks and laws now use "chairperson" in places.
- Écriture féminine (Cixous) — writing the feminine body; Kristeva's semiotic; Showalter coined **gynocriticism** (women's literature criticism) (2024 MCQ).
- **Conclusion**: language both reflects and shapes gender; changing it is part of changing attitudes.

## 10.4 The digital gender gap and technology-facilitated GBV (2026 Q8a)

### Facts

- **GSMA Mobile Gender Gap Report 2025** (data for 2024): Pakistan's **mobile internet gender gap fell from 38% to 25%**; about **8 million more women** came online in 2024, the largest year-on-year reduction among the countries surveyed, driven mainly by rural women's adoption. (The 2026 paper quoted these figures.)
- **GSMA report 2026** (data for 2025, as reported by Pakistani press in June 2026): mobile internet gap about **8%** (53% of women vs 58% of men using mobile internet); mobile ownership gap about 27% (down from 37%).
- GSMA estimated that closing the gap could add about US$55 billion to GDP over eight years (as reported by MoITT).
- Drivers of progress: cheaper smartphones, BISP digital payments, PTA/MoITT Digital Gender Inclusion Strategy (2024 [verify]), women's freelancing, Raast and mobile wallets, social norms easing.
- Remaining barriers: affordability, literacy and digital skills, family permission and fears of "misuse", online harassment, lack of relevant content in local languages, phones registered in men's names (SIMs require CNICs).

### Gendered implications

| Opportunity | Risk |
|---|---|
| Home-based income (freelancing, online stores, tutoring) | Online harassment, blackmail with images, deepfakes |
| Access to information (health, rights, markets) | Surveillance by family; "honour" violence over phone use |
| Financial inclusion (mobile wallets, BISP digital payments) | Fraud and scams targeting less literate women |
| Political voice (digital activism, Aurat March organising) | Trolling and threats against women journalists and activists |
| Education (online learning) | Algorithmic bias; AI tools trained on male-dominated data |

**Law.** PECA 2016 ss. 20–24 (dignity, modesty, cyberstalking); complaints to the FIA Cyber Crime Wing, now the **National Cyber Crime Investigation Agency** (created 2024–25 [verify]); PECA (Amendment) Act 2025 created a Social Media Protection and Regulatory Authority [verify], criticised by journalists for free-speech risks. **Digital Rights Foundation**'s Cyber Harassment Helpline (2016) receives thousands of complaints a year, most from women.

## 10.5 Transgender rights in Pakistan (2026 Q2)

| Year | Event |
|---|---|
| Tradition | **Khawaja sira**/hijra communities with guru–chela structures; historic roles in Mughal courts; colonial Criminal Tribes Act (1871) stigmatised them |
| 2009 | Supreme Court (Dr Muhammad Aslam Khaki case, CJ Iftikhar Chaudhry) ordered recognition and protection; NADRA later issued CNICs with a third-gender option (2011–12) |
| 2017 | Transgender persons counted in the census for the first time (about 10,000 recorded; activists said far more); "X" passports |
| 2018 | **Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Act** passed with wide support: recognition by **self-perceived gender identity**; ban on discrimination in education, employment, health, transport, property, inheritance; protection against harassment; right to vote and hold office |
| 2018 | Marvia Malik became the first transgender news anchor |
| 2021–22 | Religious parties (JI, JUI-F) campaigned against the Act, saying self-identification could enable same-sex marriage and violate Islamic injunctions; amendment bills moved |
| **May 2023** | **Federal Shariat Court** declared sections 2(f) (gender identity), 2(n)(iii) (definition of transgender person based on self-perception), 3 (recognition of self-perceived identity) and 7 (inheritance as framed) repugnant to Islam; it accepted intersex persons (khunsa) and eunuchs as entitled to rights |
| 2023 | Appeals filed before the Shariat Appellate Bench of the Supreme Court (e.g. by PPP's Farhatullah Babar and by trans activists); NADRA reportedly paused "X" CNIC registration; final outcome not confirmed by Nov 2026 [verify] |
| Ongoing | Violence: dozens of transgender persons killed in KP alone over the past decade (activists' figures) [verify] |

**Theory link.** Social constructionism and queer theory (gender identity as self-defined and performative) vs biological and juristic definitions (sex as fixed; khunsa as an intersex category). Present both sides: rights activists (dignity, non-discrimination, Article 25) vs critics (religious law, family structure, concern about misuse). A balanced conclusion: protect the rights to life, dignity, work and education of khawaja siras and intersex people (agreed by both sides), while the legal definition of identity remains contested.
