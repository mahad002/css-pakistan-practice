# 1. Introduction to Gender Studies

Syllabus section I. Asked in 7 of 11 papers (2016–2023). Typical questions: "Differentiate gender studies from women's studies", "Autonomy vs integration debate", "Status of gender studies in Pakistan", "Multidisciplinary nature".

## 1.1 Key definitions

| Term | Meaning (use in introductions) |
|---|---|
| **Sex** | Biological categories (chromosomes, hormones, reproductive organs) usually labelled female and male. Intersex people do not fit the binary neatly |
| **Gender** | The social meanings, roles, behaviours and expectations a society attaches to being female or male. Learned, varies across cultures and time, linked to power |
| **Gender role** | Expected behaviour for each sex (e.g. man as breadwinner, woman as carer). Term popularised by John Money (1955) |
| **Gender identity** | A person's inner sense of their own gender |
| **Gender relations** | Power relations between women and men (and among them) in families, markets and the state |
| **Patriarchy** | A system of social structures and practices in which men dominate, oppress and exploit women (Sylvia Walby, *Theorizing Patriarchy*, 1990). Walby names six structures: household production, paid work, the state, male violence, sexuality, cultural institutions |
| **Gender equality** | Equal rights, responsibilities and opportunities regardless of sex |
| **Gender equity** | Fair treatment according to need; may require special measures (quotas) to reach equality |
| **Gender mainstreaming** | Assessing the effect on women and men of every policy, law and programme at all levels (UN ECOSOC definition, 1997). Adopted globally at Beijing 1995 |

## 1.2 What is Gender Studies?

**Definition.** Gender Studies is an interdisciplinary academic field that studies how gender (and its links with sexuality, class, race, religion and other identities) is constructed, experienced and represented, and how it shapes power and inequality in society.

**Core assumptions.**
1. Gender is socially constructed, not fixed by biology.
2. Gender is relational: femininity and masculinity are defined against each other, so men must be studied too.
3. Gender is a system of power (patriarchy), not just a difference.
4. Gender intersects with other identities (class, caste, ethnicity, religion, disability).
5. Knowledge itself is gendered: what counts as "objective" often reflects male experience (feminist epistemology, standpoint theory).
6. The field has a commitment to change, not only description (praxis).

### Origins and evolution

| Stage | When | What happened |
|---|---|---|
| Feminist critique of knowledge | 1960s | Second-wave activists asked why women were absent from history, literature and social science ("add women and stir") |
| **Women's Studies** | 1970s | First formal programme at San Diego State University (1970). Florence Howe and the Feminist Press. By 1980 hundreds of US programmes. UK, India and others followed |
| Shift to "gender" | late 1970s–1980s | Ann Oakley's *Sex, Gender and Society* (1972) and Gayle Rubin's "sex/gender system" (1975) made gender an analytic category. Joan Scott, "Gender: A Useful Category of Historical Analysis" (1986) |
| **Gender Studies** | 1980s–1990s | Men's studies and masculinity studies (Connell), queer theory (1990), postcolonial critique (Mohanty, Spivak). Many departments renamed "Women's and Gender Studies" or "Gender Studies" |
| Intersectional and global turn | 1990s–now | Crenshaw (1989), Black feminism, transnational feminism, sexuality studies, digital and climate topics |

### Why the discipline was needed (asked 2019)

- Traditional disciplines took men's lives as the norm and ignored women's work, voices and history ("androcentrism").
- Women's unpaid work was invisible in economics; women's history was missing from history.
- Policy failed women because data was not disaggregated by sex.
- The women's movement needed theory and evidence; universities needed to train planners (WID, then GAD).
- To study men and masculinities as gendered, not as "neutral humans".
- In Pakistan: the state needed trained people for its CEDAW and Beijing commitments, and NGOs needed research on violence, law and development.

## 1.3 Gender Studies vs Women's Studies (asked 2017, 2018, 2019, 2023)

| Basis | Women's Studies | Gender Studies |
|---|---|---|
| Period | 1970s, second wave | 1980s–1990s onwards |
| Subject | Women: their lives, history, oppression and achievements | Gender as a social system that shapes women, men and gender-diverse people |
| Unit of analysis | Women as a group | Relations between and among genders; masculinity and femininity |
| Men | Mostly absent, seen as the dominant group | Studied as gendered beings (masculinity studies) |
| Theory base | Liberal, radical, Marxist feminism | Adds social constructionism, postmodernism, queer theory, intersectionality |
| Political stance | Openly activist; women-centred | Activist but broader, more analytical; sometimes criticised as depoliticised |
| Aim | Make women visible; correct bias ("add women") | Transform how all knowledge treats gender |
| Example question | Why are women absent from Pakistan's labour statistics? | How do ideas of male honour and female modesty shape both men's and women's work choices? |

**Critical points for analysis.**
- Supporters of the shift say "gender" is more inclusive and shows that inequality is relational.
- Critics (e.g. some radical feminists; in Pakistan, Rubina Saigol and others discussed this) warn that "gender" can hide women's specific oppression, make the field sound neutral and donor-friendly, and dilute the political project.
- Middle view: Gender Studies grew out of Women's Studies; they are stages of one field, not rivals. Women remain central because they are the main disadvantaged group.

**Pakistani example.** Centres set up in 1989 were called "Women's Studies"; several later became "Gender Studies" departments (e.g. at Quaid-i-Azam University and the University of the Punjab), mirroring the global shift.

## 1.4 Multidisciplinary nature (asked 2022, 2023)

Gender Studies borrows concepts and methods from many disciplines and gives each a gender lens. Make a table in the exam:

| Discipline | What gender analysis adds | Example thinker / concept |
|---|---|---|
| Sociology | Gender roles, socialisation, family, "doing gender" | Parsons (roles), West and Zimmerman (1987) |
| Anthropology | Cross-cultural variation proves gender is cultural | Margaret Mead, *Sex and Temperament* (1935); Sherry Ortner (1974) |
| Psychology | Gender identity, socialisation, androgyny | Sandra Bem (1974); Carol Gilligan (1982) |
| Economics | Unpaid care work, labour market discrimination, feminisation of poverty | Marilyn Waring, *If Women Counted* (1988); Boserup (1970) |
| Political Science | Representation, quotas, citizenship, the state | Pateman, *The Sexual Contract* (1988) |
| History | Recovering women's history; history of masculinities | Joan Scott (1986) |
| Literature and linguistics | Representation, gendered language, écriture féminine | Showalter (gynocriticism), Cixous (1975), Robin Lakoff (1975) |
| Law | Discrimination, family law, violence law, CEDAW | Catharine MacKinnon |
| Philosophy | Feminist ethics and epistemology | de Beauvoir (1949); Sandra Harding |
| Biology and medicine | Sex differences, intersex, gendered medicine | Anne Fausto-Sterling (1993, 2000) |
| Development studies | WID, WAD, GAD, gender planning | Moser (1993), Kabeer (1994) |
| Media and communication | Representation, stereotypes, the "male gaze" | Laura Mulvey (1975) |
| Religious studies | Feminist readings of scripture | Asma Barlas (2002), Fatima Mernissi |
| Environmental studies | Ecofeminism, gender and climate | Vandana Shiva, Bina Agarwal |

**Why it matters.** Gender cuts across every sphere of life, so no single discipline can explain it. The field is also **interdisciplinary** (it combines methods, e.g. feminist research methods using qualitative interviews, life histories, participatory research) and **transdisciplinary** (it creates new concepts like intersectionality).

**Challenges of multidisciplinarity.** Lack of a single method; fights for budget and recognition; teachers trained in other disciplines; risk of being seen as "soft".

## 1.5 Autonomy vs Integration debate (asked 2016, 2017, 2021, 2023)

**Source.** Gloria Bowles and Renate Duelli Klein (eds.), *Theories of Women's Studies* (1983). Their introduction set out the debate. It is on the FPSC reading list.

**The question.** Should Women's/Gender Studies be an **autonomous** discipline with its own departments, degrees, staff and budget, or should it be **integrated** (mainstreamed) into existing disciplines so that every course in sociology, history, economics etc. includes gender?

| | Autonomy | Integration (mainstreaming) |
|---|---|---|
| Argument | Feminist knowledge is new and needs its own space; existing disciplines are male-centred and will absorb and dilute it | Gender must change all knowledge; a separate department becomes a "ghetto" that others can ignore |
| Benefits | Own degrees, research agendas, journals, PhDs; career paths for feminist scholars; theory-building | Reaches all students; changes curricula across the university; less isolation |
| Risks | Marginalisation ("women's issues are for women"); small budgets; seen as less rigorous | Co-optation; gender reduced to one lecture; loss of radical edge; depends on goodwill of male-dominated departments |
| Associated with | Radical feminists; separate centres | Liberal feminists; curriculum transformation projects |

**Resolution: the "dual strategy".** Most scholars, including Bowles and Klein, argue for both: autonomous centres that produce theory and train specialists, **and** integration of gender into all disciplines. This is now common practice (e.g. separate Gender Studies departments plus compulsory gender modules in social science degrees).

**Contribution of the debate to the field (asked 2021).**
1. Clarified the identity and aims of the discipline.
2. Produced the dual strategy, which is how the field grew.
3. Pushed gender into mainstream curricula (gender modules in law, medicine, development).
4. Forced reflection on the link between academia and activism.
5. Anticipated today's gender-mainstreaming policy debate (separate women's ministries vs gender focal persons in every ministry).

**Pakistan angle.** Pakistan followed both routes: autonomous centres/departments (1989 onward) and gender courses within sociology, social work, law and public administration. HEC also introduced a gender studies paper in some BS programmes. Weakness: integration is often token; centres are small and under-funded.

**Your view (for "give your views").** Support the dual strategy for Pakistan: keep autonomous departments for research and PhDs, but make a gender course compulsory in civil service training (CSA, NIPA/NIM, police academies), law and medical education, where it changes practice.

## 1.6 Status of Gender Studies in Pakistan (asked 2016, 2018, 2019, 2023)

### History

| Year | Development |
|---|---|
| 1979–80s | Women's Division created (1979); women's movement (WAF, 1981) and NGOs (Shirkat Gah, ASR, Aurat Foundation) produced research on law and violence. Khawar Mumtaz and Farida Shaheed, *Women of Pakistan: Two Steps Forward, One Step Back?* (1987) |
| 1989 | Ministry of Women Development set up **Women's Studies Centres at five universities**: Quaid-i-Azam (Islamabad), Karachi, Punjab (Lahore), Peshawar and Balochistan (Quetta) [verify the exact list in Farzana Bari / Rubina Saigol's surveys] |
| 1990s | MA/MSc programmes; Allama Iqbal Open University offered women's studies; Fatima Jinnah Women University (1998) added the field |
| 2000s–2010s | Renaming to Gender Studies; MPhil and PhD programmes; HEC curricula; departments at many public universities (e.g. Punjab, QAU, Karachi, Peshawar, Sindh, Bahauddin Zakariya, GCU Faisalabad and others) |
| Since 2016 | CSS made Gender Studies an optional subject (CE-2016 syllabus). It became the most-chosen subject in Group V: about 7,700 sat it in 2023 and 8,245 in 2025 |

Key writers on the state of the field (FPSC reading list): **Rubina Saigol**, "The State of the Discipline of Women's Studies in Pakistan"; **Farzana Bari**, "The State of Women's Studies in Pakistan"; **Nighat Said Khan** et al., *Locating the Self* (1998).

### Strengths

- Institutional presence in most large public universities.
- Close link with activism and NGOs, so research is policy-relevant (laws on harassment 2010, honour killing 2016, domestic violence).
- CSS and PMS have created student demand.
- Growing data: PBS gender statistics, NCSW reports, provincial gender parity reports (e.g. Punjab Commission on the Status of Women).

### Weaknesses

1. **Under-funding and small faculty**; many teachers trained in other fields.
2. **Western-centric syllabi**: few local texts, few Urdu or regional-language materials.
3. **Social resistance**: the field is called "Western" or "anti-religion"; teachers self-censor on sexuality and religion.
4. **Donor dependence** for research; agenda set from outside ("NGO-isation").
5. **Weak research culture**: few peer-reviewed journals; limited fieldwork funding.
6. **Gap with policy**: graduates rarely enter decision-making roles; integration into other disciplines is token.
7. **Exam-driven learning**: CSS demand produces guidebooks, not understanding.

### Way forward

- HEC to fund a national gender research grant and an indigenous textbook series (Urdu and English).
- Link departments to NCSW, provincial women's commissions and statistics bureaus for data.
- Engage religious scholarship (Islamic feminist and reformist work) to answer the "Western" charge.
- Compulsory gender module in civil service and police training.
- Include masculinity studies so men see the field as relevant to them.

## 1.7 Ready conclusions

- "Gender Studies began as Women's Studies' effort to make women visible and has grown into the study of how gender organises all social life. In Pakistan it has gained institutional space but not yet intellectual independence or policy influence."
- "The autonomy versus integration debate is best settled by a dual strategy: autonomy builds knowledge; integration spreads it."
