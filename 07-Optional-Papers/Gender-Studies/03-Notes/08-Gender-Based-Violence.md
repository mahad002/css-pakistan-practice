# 8. Gender-Based Violence (GBV)

Syllabus section VIII. Asked in 9 of 11 papers: sites and forms of VAW (2016), forms and strategies (2017, 2023), honour killing note (2017), government initiatives (2018 note), structural and direct violence with Mukhtaran Mai (2019), power and control (2020), Punjab 2016 Act (2024), root causes and intersections (2025), masculinity and violence (2026 note).

## 8.1 Definitions

- **UN Declaration on the Elimination of Violence against Women (DEVAW)**, General Assembly resolution 48/104, December 1993, Article 1: violence against women means any act of gender-based violence that results in, or is likely to result in, physical, sexual or psychological harm or suffering to women, including threats, coercion or arbitrary deprivation of liberty, in public or private life.
- DEVAW Article 2 groups violence by site: (a) in the **family**; (b) in the **community**; (c) **perpetrated or condoned by the state**.
- **CEDAW General Recommendation 19** (1992): GBV is discrimination — "violence directed against a woman because she is a woman or that affects women disproportionately". Updated by **General Recommendation 35** (2017).
- **GBV** is broader than VAW: violence based on gender norms against anyone (including men who break masculine norms and transgender persons), but women and girls are the majority of victims.
- **Istanbul Convention** (Council of Europe, 2011): first binding European treaty on VAW and domestic violence.
- Scale (global): WHO (2021) estimates about **1 in 3 women** worldwide (about 30%) have experienced physical or sexual violence by a partner or sexual violence by a non-partner in their lifetime; about 736 million women. The 2025 MCQ on "how many women face GBV" expected "one in three".

## 8.2 Forms of GBV — across the life cycle (useful table)

| Stage | Forms (global) | Pakistan examples |
|---|---|---|
| Before birth | Sex-selective abortion | Son preference |
| Infancy | Female infanticide; neglect | Less food and care for girls in poor households |
| Childhood | Child marriage; sexual abuse; FGM (in some countries) | Child marriage; child domestic workers abused (Rizwana 2023; Zohra Shah 2020 case) |
| Adolescence | Forced marriage; trafficking; harassment | Vani/swara (girls given in settlement); exchange marriage (watta satta); harassment on the way to school |
| Adulthood | Intimate partner violence; marital rape; dowry violence; honour crimes; acid attacks; workplace harassment; trafficking; cyber harassment | Karo-kari/siyahkari; acid attacks; stove-burning; dowry harassment; marriage to the Quran (haq bakhshwana); denial of inheritance; forced conversion of minority girls (Sindh) |
| Old age | Neglect, property grabbing, abuse of widows | Widows deprived of land |

### By type

1. **Physical**: beating, burning, acid, murder.
2. **Sexual**: rape (including marital rape), harassment, forced prostitution, trafficking.
3. **Psychological/emotional**: threats, insults, isolation, controlling behaviour, threats of divorce or second marriage (named in the ICT DV Act 2026).
4. **Economic**: denying money, inheritance, property, or the right to work; taking wages.
5. **Cultural/customary**: honour killings, vani/swara, forced marriages.
6. **Structural**: unequal laws, poverty, lack of services (see 8.4).
7. **Technology-facilitated**: cyberstalking, non-consensual image sharing, deepfakes, doxxing (PECA 2016 ss. 21, 24, and the 2025 amendments on fake news did not target this specifically).
8. **State/institutional**: custodial abuse, police refusal to register cases.

### Sites (2016 question)

- **Family/home**: most common site; domestic violence, marital rape, honour killing.
- **Community**: street harassment, jirga decisions, workplaces, schools, transport.
- **State**: custodial violence, discriminatory laws (Zina Ordinance era), failure to protect.
- **Cyber space**: new site.
- **Conflict and disaster zones**: displacement camps after floods; militancy in Swat (2007–09).

## 8.3 Theories of violence against women

| Theory | Explanation | Thinkers | Critique |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Feminist / patriarchy** | VAW is a means to maintain male dominance; rooted in gender inequality, not individual pathology | Brownmiller (1975); Dobash and Dobash, *Violence against Wives* (1979); Walby | May underplay class, race, individual factors |
| **Power and control (Duluth model)** | Abuse is a pattern of tactics to gain power and control: intimidation, emotional abuse, isolation, minimising and blaming, using children, male privilege, economic abuse, coercion and threats — with physical and sexual violence at the rim | Domestic Abuse Intervention Project, Duluth, Minnesota (1984); Ellen Pence | Developed for Western heterosexual couples; some say it ignores mutual violence |
| **Cycle of violence** | Tension-building → acute battering → reconciliation ("honeymoon") → repeat | Lenore Walker, *The Battered Woman* (1979) | Not all relationships follow the cycle |
| **Learned helplessness / battered woman syndrome** | Repeated abuse leads women to believe they cannot escape | Martin Seligman (helplessness); Walker | Portrays women as passive; many resist |
| **Social learning** | Violence is learned by observing parents and community, and reinforced | Albert Bandura (1973); intergenerational transmission | Not all who witness violence become violent (2025 MCQ: VAW as "learned behaviour") |
| **Resource theory** | Men use violence when they lack other resources (money, status) to assert dominance | William Goode (1971) | Contradicts cases of wealthy abusers |
| **Exchange / social control theory** | People hit family members because the rewards exceed the costs (weak sanctions) | Richard Gelles (1983) | Rational-choice assumptions |
| **Family systems / stress theory** | Violence arises from family stress (poverty, unemployment) and dysfunctional interaction | Murray Straus and Gelles | Ignores gendered power; "family conflict" framing |
| **Subculture of violence** | Some groups hold norms that approve violence | Wolfgang and Ferracuti (1967) | Stereotypes poor and minority groups |
| **Psychopathology / biology** | Mental illness, alcohol, testosterone, personality disorders | — | Most abusers are not mentally ill; excuses men |
| **Ecological model** | Violence results from factors at four levels: individual (history of abuse), relationship (marital conflict, male control of wealth), community (poverty, isolation, weak sanctions), society (norms of male entitlement, acceptance of violence) | Lori Heise (1998); adopted by WHO (2002) | Broad, hard to test, but best for policy |
| **Intersectionality** | Violence shaped by overlapping gender, class, caste, religion, ethnicity, disability | Crenshaw, "Mapping the Margins" (1991) | — |
| **Structural/cultural violence** | Direct violence rests on structural and cultural violence | Johan Galtung (1969, 1990) | — |
| **Masculinity theory** | Violence used to prove or restore threatened masculinity; honour | Connell; Kimmel | — |

**Best exam move.** Use the **ecological model** as your framework and slot the others into its levels. For Pakistan, the 2025 question ("root causes and how they intersect") is perfect for this.

### Root causes in Pakistan (2025) — by ecological level

| Level | Causes |
|---|---|
| Individual | Witnessing violence as a child; low education; drug use; frustration from unemployment |
| Relationship | Male control of income and decisions; large age gaps; dowry disputes; joint-family conflict with in-laws; marriage without consent |
| Community | Honour codes; jirga justice; poverty; low female literacy; acceptance of wife-beating (in PDHS 2017–18, a large share of both women and men said a husband was justified in beating his wife for at least one reason [verify percentages]); weak police presence |
| Society/state | Patriarchal norms; misuse of religion; compoundable offences and forgiveness (qisas and diyat); slow courts and low conviction rates; media portrayals normalising abuse in dramas; economic crisis |

**How they intersect.** Poverty + honour codes + weak state = a woman who cannot leave, a family that will not report, a jirga that decides, and a court that acquits after a compromise. Class, ethnicity and religion add layers: minority girls (Hindu and Christian) face forced conversion and marriage; disabled women face higher abuse; rural women have no shelters nearby.

## 8.4 Structural and direct forms of violence (2019, Galtung)

**Johan Galtung**, "Violence, Peace, and Peace Research" (1969) and "Cultural Violence" (1990):

| | Direct violence | Structural violence | Cultural violence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Visible act by an identifiable actor causing harm | Harm built into social, economic and political structures; no single perpetrator; prevents people meeting basic needs | Beliefs, values and symbols (religion, ideology, language, art) that make direct and structural violence look right or acceptable |
| Examples | Rape, beating, acid attacks, honour killing, murder | Unequal laws, denial of inheritance, lack of maternal health care (preventable deaths), low wages, no schools for girls, exclusion from politics | Honour codes, sayings that justify beating, misinterpretation of religion, drama plots glorifying obedience |
| Pakistani case | Mukhtaran Mai's gang rape (2002) | The panchayat system, police and court failures that allowed it; poverty and caste/biradari hierarchy (Mastoi vs Gujjar) | "Honour" demanded that a woman pay for a man's alleged offence |

The **violence triangle**: cultural violence legitimises structural violence, which produces direct violence; direct violence reinforces structures.

**Using Mukhtaran Mai (2019 question).** Direct: gang rape ordered by a village council. Structural: tribal/caste hierarchy, landlord power, jirga as parallel justice, police complicity, years of court delays, acquittals. Cultural: the idea that women's bodies carry family honour and can be used to settle men's disputes. See `09-Case-Studies.md`.

## 8.5 Honour killing (2017 note)

- **Definition**: the murder of a woman (sometimes a man) by family members who believe she brought dishonour (choice marriage, alleged affair, rape victimhood, refusing a marriage, divorce request). Local names: **karo-kari** (Sindh), **siyahkari** (Balochistan), **tor tora** (KP), **kala kali** (Punjab).
- **Scale**: HRCP records hundreds of cases each year; the true number is higher because many are disguised as suicide or accidents [verify latest HRCP figure].
- **Causes**: honour codes; control of women's sexuality and marriage choices; property (preventing a woman's share leaving the family); the qisas and diyat law that let heirs (usually the killers' own family) forgive; jirga sanction.
- **Law**: Criminal Law (Amendment) Act 2004 (defined honour crimes); **Criminal Law (Amendment) (Offences in the name or pretext of Honour) Act 2016**: killing in the name of honour is treated as **fasad-fil-arz**; if heirs forgive, the court must still impose **life imprisonment** (forgiveness only avoids the death penalty). Passed after **Qandeel Baloch**'s murder by her brother (July 2016); her brother was convicted in 2019 but acquitted on appeal by the Lahore High Court in 2022 after their parents' pardon [verify] — showing gaps.
- **Sharmeen Obaid-Chinoy's** *A Girl in the River* (Oscar 2016) about Saba Qaiser, who survived an honour attack, pushed the 2016 law.

## 8.6 Consequences of GBV (2024)

- **Health**: injury, death, disability, chronic pain, sexually transmitted infections, unwanted pregnancy, miscarriage, depression, PTSD, suicide.
- **Economic**: lost workdays, lower earnings; global cost estimated at about 2% of world GDP (UN Women estimate) [verify].
- **Social**: isolation, stigma, girls' school dropout, family breakdown, intergenerational transmission to children.
- **Political**: women avoid public life; fear of harassment limits participation.
- **Development**: undermines SDG 5 and other goals.

## 8.7 Punjab Protection of Women against Violence Act 2016 (2024)

- **Violence defined** (s. 2) to include: domestic violence, **emotional, psychological and verbal abuse**, **economic abuse**, **stalking**, and **cyber crime**, against a woman.
- **Remedies**: protection orders (no contact, stay away), residence orders (not to be evicted from the home), monetary orders (maintenance, compensation, medical costs); GPS bracelets for offenders in serious cases.
- **Institutions**: District Women Protection Committees; Women Protection Officers; **Violence Against Women Centres** (one-stop: police, prosecution, medical, legal, shelter — first in Multan, March 2017); toll-free helpline; **Punjab Women Protection Authority** (2017).
- **Controversy**: the Council of Islamic Ideology and some religious parties called it un-Islamic and anti-family; supporters said it filled a gap. Implementation has been slow and limited to a few districts [verify current coverage].

## 8.8 Strategies to eliminate VAW (2016, 2017, 2023)

### Frameworks

- **4P framework**: Prevention, Protection, Prosecution, Provision of services (plus Partnership).
- **UN Women / WHO RESPECT framework** (2019): Relationship skills strengthened; Empowerment of women; Services ensured; Poverty reduced; Environments made safe; Child and adolescent abuse prevented; Transformed attitudes, beliefs and norms.
- **Due diligence**: states must prevent, investigate, punish and compensate (DEVAW Art. 4; CEDAW GR 19/35).

### Strategy table for Pakistan

| Area | Measures |
|---|---|
| **Legal** | Uniform DV laws in every province (Punjab 2016, Sindh 2013, Balochistan 2014, KP 2021, ICT 2026 exist; harmonise); make all GBV offences non-compoundable; criminalise marital rape explicitly [verify current legal position]; enforce minimum marriage age 18 nationwide |
| **Justice system** | Gender-based violence courts in every district; women prosecutors and judges; time-bound trials; witness protection; victim compensation funds; strict action against unlawful jirgas |
| **Police** | Women police stations and desks; gender training; mandatory FIR registration; special sexual offences units (Anti-Rape Act 2021); forensic DNA labs (Punjab Forensic Science Agency model) |
| **Services** | One-stop crisis centres (VAWC model); Darul Amans and shelters with dignity; free legal aid; psychosocial support; helplines (1043, 1099, 15) |
| **Prevention** | School curricula on respect and consent; engaging men and boys (masculinity programmes); religious leaders' sermons against violence; community mobilisation (SASA! model from Uganda); media codes (PEMRA guidelines against glamorising abuse) |
| **Economic empowerment** | BISP cash to women, inheritance enforcement (2020 Act), jobs and skills; economic independence makes leaving possible |
| **Data and research** | Regular national prevalence surveys; GBV case tracking; NCSW and provincial commissions' monitoring |
| **Digital** | PECA enforcement by the FIA (now the National Cyber Crime Investigation Agency, 2024 [verify]); platform reporting; Digital Rights Foundation helpline |
| **Political will** | Budgeting for implementation; Women's Parliamentary Caucus oversight |

### Government initiatives (2018 note)

Federal: Ministry of Human Rights helpline 1099; Shaheed Benazir Bhutto Women Centres (crisis centres); NCSW; FOSPAH; Anti-Rape Act 2021; ICT DV Act 2026; designated GBV courts. Punjab: PWAVA 2016, VAWC, Women Protection Authority, 1043 helpline, Virtual Women Police Station (2024 [verify]), Punjab Safe Cities (panic buttons). Sindh: DV Act 2013, Sindh Commission on the Status of Women, women's police stations. KP: DV Act 2021, Bolo Helpline. Balochistan: DV Act 2014; anti-harassment ombudsperson.
