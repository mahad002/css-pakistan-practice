# 9. Case Studies: Mukhtaran Mai, Malala Yousafzai, Sharmeen Obaid-Chinoy

Syllabus section IX. Asked as full questions: Mukhtaran Mai (inside 2019 Q7), Sharmeen Obaid-Chinoy (2020), Malala (2025). Use these three as evidence in many other answers (violence, education, media, movements).

For each case, the exam wants: facts → what it reveals (theory) → impact (law, policy, society) → criticisms and controversies → lessons.

## 9.1 Mukhtaran Mai (Mukhtar Mai)

### Facts

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Who | A woman from Meerwala village, Muzaffargarh district, south Punjab, from the Tatla (Gujjar) clan |
| When | June 2002 |
| What happened | Her 12-year-old brother, Shakoor, was accused by the more powerful Mastoi clan of illicit relations with a Mastoi woman (later evidence suggested he himself had been sexually assaulted by Mastoi men). A village council (panchayat/jirga) dominated by Mastois ordered that Mukhtaran be gang-raped as "punishment" to restore Mastoi honour. She was gang-raped and paraded |
| Response | Instead of committing suicide, as was expected, she went to the police. A local imam spoke about the case at Friday prayers; journalists reported it; it became national and international news |
| Trial | Aug 2002: an Anti-Terrorism Court in Dera Ghazi Khan sentenced six men to death |
| Appeal | Mar 2005: the Lahore High Court acquitted five, citing insufficient evidence, and commuted one sentence to life imprisonment. The Federal Shariat Court and then the Supreme Court took up the matter |
| Final verdict | April 2011: the Supreme Court upheld the acquittals except for Abdul Khaliq (life imprisonment). She expressed disappointment but continued her work |
| Afterwards | She used compensation money to open schools for girls and boys in Meerwala (Mukhtar Mai Women's Welfare Organization); a crisis centre and helpline. In 2005 the government briefly put her name on the Exit Control List to stop her travelling to the US, causing an international outcry. Glamour magazine's Woman of the Year (2005). Memoir: *In the Name of Honor* (2006, with Marie-Thérèse Cuny). In 2009 she married a police constable |

### What the case reveals

1. **Structural violence** (Galtung): power of landlord clans; caste/biradari hierarchy; jirga as parallel justice; police complicity; slow and uncertain courts.
2. **Direct violence**: rape ordered as collective punishment.
3. **Cultural violence**: women's bodies as carriers of family honour; women used to settle men's disputes (like vani/swara).
4. **Intersectionality**: a poor, rural, lower-status woman against a powerful clan.
5. **Weak evidence regimes**: acquittals on technical grounds; the difficulty of proving rape; lack of forensic evidence and witness protection.
6. **Agency**: she refused the victim's script (suicide or silence), became an activist — a symbol of resistance.

### Impact

- International attention to Pakistan's rape laws and jirga justice; helped the debate that produced the **Protection of Women Act 2006** (which moved rape out of the Hudood Zina Ordinance).
- Inspired other survivors to report.
- Exposed state sensitivity to "image" (General Musharraf's 2005 remark in a Washington Post interview that rape had become a way to get a visa abroad caused outrage; he later said he was misquoted).
- Supreme Court rulings against jirgas (e.g. 2019 judgment holding jirgas/panchayats unlawful when they act as courts [verify]).

### Criticisms and controversies

- Some argued she became a tool of foreign media and NGOs; others accused critics of victim-blaming.
- The acquittals showed the justice system's failure rather than her case's weakness.

### Lessons

Reform evidence and investigation laws; ban jirgas acting as courts; protect witnesses; speed up trials; support survivors economically.

## 9.2 Malala Yousafzai (2025 full question)

### Facts

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Born | 12 July 1997, Mingora, Swat |
| Family | Father Ziauddin Yousafzai ran a girls' school (Khushal Public School) and was an education activist |
| Background | 2007–09: the Pakistani Taliban (under Mullah Fazlullah) controlled much of Swat, banned girls' education (Dec 2008 edict) and destroyed hundreds of schools |
| Activism | From January 2009, aged 11, she wrote an anonymous diary for **BBC Urdu** under the pen name **"Gul Makai"**. Featured in a *New York Times* documentary (Adam Ellick, 2009). Spoke publicly for girls' education. Nominated for the International Children's Peace Prize (2011, by Desmond Tutu); received Pakistan's first **National Youth Peace Prize** (2011, later renamed the National Malala Peace Prize) |
| Attack | **9 October 2012**: a Taliban gunman shot her in the head on her school bus in Swat; two classmates also injured. Treated in Peshawar and Rawalpindi, then Queen Elizabeth Hospital, Birmingham, UK |
| Global platform | Spoke at the UN on her 16th birthday, **12 July 2013** ("Malala Day"): "One child, one teacher, one book and one pen can change the world." Co-founded the **Malala Fund** (2013) with her father. Memoir *I Am Malala* (2013, with Christina Lamb) |
| Nobel Peace Prize | **2014**, shared with India's **Kailash Satyarthi** (child rights). Aged 17, the **youngest-ever Nobel laureate**; second Pakistani Nobel laureate after Dr Abdus Salam (Physics, 1979) |
| Other | Sakharov Prize (2013); honorary Canadian citizenship (2014); UN Messenger of Peace (2017); graduated in PPE from the University of Oxford (2020); married Asser Malik (2021); produced films and joined Apple TV+ content deal (2021); attended the Global Summit on Girls' Education in Muslim Communities, Islamabad (January 2025) |
| Malala Fund | Works in countries with large numbers of out-of-school girls, including Pakistan, Afghanistan, Nigeria, Brazil; "Education Champion" network of local activists; advocates on climate and education, and against Taliban restrictions on Afghan girls ("gender apartheid") |

### Local context (Pakistan)

- Government named a degree college in Swat after her; Malala Day observed; she visited Pakistan in March 2018 for the first time since the attack.
- Mixed reception: admired by many; conspiracy theories and criticism on social media, private schools' associations once banned her book (2013), some called her a "Western agent".
- Reasons for criticism: suspicion of Western media narratives; perception that she portrayed Pakistan negatively; anti-Western sentiment after drone strikes; politicisation.
- Her case reinforced military operations against the Taliban in Swat (Rah-e-Rast 2009 had already happened) and the national push against extremism after the Army Public School attack (2014).

### Global context

- Became the symbol of the right to education (SDG 4) and girls' education (SDG 5).
- UN Special Envoy Gordon Brown launched the "I am Malala" petition; the UN's "Malala Day"; Pakistan's commitment to Article 25-A gained attention.
- Malala Fund grants and advocacy; G7 and World Bank commitments on girls' education.

### Critical evaluation (2025 asks "critically examine and evaluate")

**Achievements:**
- Global awareness of girls' education in conflict zones.
- Institutional legacy: Malala Fund's investments, local activists' network.
- Moral courage: challenged violent extremism at great personal risk.
- Inspired girls in Pakistan and abroad; showed youth activism.

**Limits / critiques:**
- Out-of-school children in Pakistan remain about 25 million; individual symbolism cannot replace state action.
- Critics argue Western media used her story to justify narratives about Muslim societies (postcolonial critique: "saving brown women") — compare Spivak and Mohanty.
- Her work is mainly advocacy; the hard work of school construction, teachers and stipends lies with the state.
- Polarised reception at home has limited her direct influence in Pakistan.

**Balanced conclusion.** She converted personal tragedy into global advocacy and made girls' education a world issue; but Pakistan's gaps show that symbols must be matched by budgets, teachers and safety.

## 9.3 Sharmeen Obaid-Chinoy (2020 full question)

### Profile

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Born | 1978, Karachi |
| Education | Smith College (USA), Stanford University (MA) |
| Profession | Journalist, documentary filmmaker; founder of **SOC Films** (2007) (2024 MCQ: "famous as a filmmaker") |
| Early work | *Terror's Children* (2003); *Pakistan: Children of the Taliban* (2009), which won an **International Emmy** (2010) — about children recruited for suicide bombing |
| Oscars | **Two Academy Awards for Best Documentary (Short Subject)** — the first Pakistani to win an Oscar |
| Other | *3 Bahadur* (2015), Pakistan's first full-length animated film; *Song of Lahore* (2015, co-directed); honoured with the **Hilal-i-Imtiaz** (2012); co-chair of the Lahore Biennale [verify]; directed episodes of Marvel's *Ms. Marvel* (2022); announced (2023) as director of a *Star Wars* feature film |

### The two Oscar films (2020 question asks for the background)

**1. *Saving Face* (2012 Oscar, co-directed with Daniel Junge)**
- Subject: **acid attacks** on women in Pakistan.
- Follows Pakistani-British plastic surgeon **Dr Mohammad Jawad** returning to Pakistan to operate on survivors, including **Zakia** (attacked by her husband) and **Rukhsana** (attacked by her husband and in-laws).
- Background: acid is cheap and easily available; attacks in south Punjab and elsewhere, often by husbands or rejected suitors; perpetrators rarely punished.
- Context and impact: released as Parliament passed the **Acid Control and Acid Crime Prevention Act 2011** (amending the PPC to add ss. 336A and 336B — punishment up to life imprisonment and minimum fine of Rs1 million for causing hurt by corrosive substance). The film increased awareness and support for survivors (Acid Survivors Foundation Pakistan).
- Controversy: one subject (Rukhsana) reportedly said she feared consequences from being shown; questions about consent and representation of Pakistan.

**2. *A Girl in the River: The Price of Forgiveness* (2016 Oscar)**
- Subject: **honour killing**.
- Story of **Saba Qaiser**, 18, from Gujranwala/Punjab, who married a man her family disapproved of. Her father and uncle shot her and threw her into a river; she survived. Under community pressure she later "forgave" them, and they were freed under the qisas and diyat law.
- Background: the **forgiveness loophole** let families kill daughters and then forgive each other.
- Impact: PM **Nawaz Sharif** watched the film at a screening in the PM House and pledged to change the law; Parliament passed the **Criminal Law (Amendment) (Offences in the name or pretext of Honour) Act 2016** in October 2016 (after Qandeel Baloch's murder in July). Mandatory life imprisonment even after forgiveness.

### Significance for Gender Studies

- **Media as advocacy**: documentary as a tool for legal reform (agenda-setting).
- **Visibility**: made hidden violence visible globally.
- **Debate**: critics said she "sells Pakistan's dirty laundry" abroad; supporters say silence protects perpetrators. Postcolonial lens: risk of feeding stereotypes vs value of local voice telling local stories.
- **Women in media leadership**: role model for women filmmakers.

### Model structure for the Sharmeen question

1. Introduction: who she is; two Oscars.
2. *Saving Face*: background of acid violence, the film, the law.
3. *A Girl in the River*: background of honour killings, forgiveness loophole, the film, the 2016 law.
4. Impact on policy and public opinion.
5. Criticisms.
6. Conclusion: power of media in gender justice; need for implementation.
