# 2. Social Construction of Gender

Syllabus section II. Asked in 7 of 11 papers. Typical questions: "Theories of social construction of gender" (2016), "Sex vs gender debate, nature vs nurture" (2018), "Deconstruct the word gender" (2020), "Masculinity and femininity: natural or social?" (2021, 2023), "Is sex also a social construct?" (2024), "Social constructionism and queer theory, with the Transgender Act 2018" (2026).

## 2.1 The core idea

**Social constructionism** holds that categories we treat as natural (gender, sexuality, race, even "sex") are produced by social processes: language, institutions, laws, everyday interaction and power. They feel natural because they are repeated and institutionalised, not because biology fixes them.

**Peter Berger and Thomas Luckmann**, *The Social Construction of Reality* (1966): people create social order through habit (habitualisation), which hardens into institutions (institutionalisation), which then appear objective and are taught to the next generation (legitimation, internalisation). Gender is a prime example.

**Simone de Beauvoir**, *The Second Sex* (1949): "One is not born, but rather becomes, a woman." Woman is made the "Other" against man as the norm. This line is the starting point of constructionism in feminism (and a frequent MCQ).

## 2.2 Historicizing constructionism (how the idea developed)

| Stage | Thinkers | Idea |
|---|---|---|
| Early anthropology | **Margaret Mead**, *Sex and Temperament in Three Primitive Societies* (1935) | Among the Arapesh both sexes were gentle; among the Mundugumor both were aggressive; among the Tchambuli women were dominant and practical and men were decorative and emotional. So "masculine" and "feminine" temperaments are cultural |
| Existentialist feminism | de Beauvoir (1949) | Woman as Other; becoming a woman |
| Sexology and psychology | **John Money** (1955) coined "gender role"; **Robert Stoller**, *Sex and Gender* (1968), separated biological sex from gender identity | Gender as learned identity |
| Sociology of roles | **Talcott Parsons** and Robert Bales (1955) | Instrumental (male) and expressive (female) roles serve the family. Functionalist, not critical, but it treats roles as social |
| Second-wave feminism | **Ann Oakley**, *Sex, Gender and Society* (1972); **Gayle Rubin**, "The Traffic in Women" (1975) — the "sex/gender system" | Sex = biology; gender = culture. Inequality comes from gender, so it can be changed |
| Anthropology of universals | **Sherry Ortner**, "Is Female to Male as Nature Is to Culture?" (1974) | Every culture devalues women because it links them with nature (childbirth) and men with culture. The link is symbolic, not biological |
| Interactionism | **Erving Goffman**, *Gender Advertisements* (1979); **Candace West and Don Zimmerman**, "Doing Gender" (1987) | Gender is something we *do* in everyday interaction and are held accountable for |
| Social construction of gender as institution | **Judith Lorber**, *Paradoxes of Gender* (1994), essay "Night to His Day: The Social Construction of Gender" (on the FPSC list) | Gender is a social institution, like the economy or religion. It sorts people, assigns work and power, and is so routine that we only notice it when someone breaks it |
| Post-structuralism | **Michel Foucault**, *The History of Sexuality* vol. 1 (1976); **Judith Butler**, *Gender Trouble* (1990) | Even sex and sexuality are produced by discourse and power; gender is performative |

### Judith Lorber's argument (learn it; it is on the reading list)

- Gender is built into every social institution: family, work, religion, law, sport.
- It starts at birth: name, clothes, toys, the way parents speak to the child.
- It produces differences and then uses them to justify inequality ("women are better at caring, so they should do unpaid care").
- Its parts: gender statuses, division of labour, kinship, sexual scripts, personalities, social control, ideology, imagery.
- "Paradox": we think gender reflects nature, but it is the social order that makes sexes look naturally different.

### West and Zimmerman, "Doing Gender" (1987)

- Distinguish **sex** (biological criteria), **sex category** (how others place you socially, by appearance), and **gender** (managing your conduct according to norms for your sex category).
- People are "accountable" to gender norms: a man who cries or a woman who swears is judged.
- Gender is thus an ongoing accomplishment, not a trait. 2026 MCQ tested this.

## 2.3 Problematising "sex": queer theory and "is sex socially determined too?"

### The question

If gender is cultural and sex is biological, is biology itself free of social influence? Constructionists of the 1990s said no: the way we define and count sexes is also social.

### Arguments that sex is (partly) socially constructed

1. **Intersex variation.** **Anne Fausto-Sterling**, "The Five Sexes" (1993) and *Sexing the Body* (2000): bodies vary in chromosomes (XXY, XO), hormones and anatomy. She estimated about 1.7% of births show some intersex variation (critics such as Leonard Sax argue a far lower figure using a narrower definition). Doctors historically "corrected" intersex infants surgically to fit two boxes, which shows that society enforces the binary.
2. **History of science.** **Thomas Laqueur**, *Making Sex* (1990): until the 18th century European medicine used a "one-sex model" (the female body as an inverted, imperfect male body). The "two-sex model" of opposite sexes emerged with modern politics. So how we see sex depends on the era.
3. **Measurement choices.** Which marker defines sex: chromosomes, gonads, hormones, genitals, or legal documents? Sports bodies have changed their tests several times (chromosome tests, then testosterone limits). That choice is social.
4. **Butler's view.** In *Gender Trouble* (1990) and *Bodies That Matter* (1993), Butler argues that "sex" was "always already gender": we read bodies through gender categories, so there is no pre-social sex we can access.
5. **Cross-cultural categories.** Many societies recognise more than two categories: khawaja siras/hijras in South Asia, fa'afafine in Samoa, two-spirit people among some Native Americans.

### Arguments that sex is biological (the critique)

1. In humans, reproductive roles (eggs vs sperm) are binary even if some bodies vary; intersex conditions are rare and do not create new sexes.
2. Sex differences matter in medicine (drug doses, heart disease symptoms); denying them can harm women.
3. Some feminists (often called "gender-critical") argue that women's rights, spaces and data depend on sex as a category; others call this exclusionary. Present both sides neutrally in the exam.
4. Many religious traditions, including mainstream Islamic jurisprudence, accept two sexes while recognising the intersex person (khuntha) as a legal category with its own rules.

### A balanced conclusion

Most scholars now take a middle position: biological variation is real, but the meanings, boundaries and consequences attached to it are social. Both "sex" and "gender" are shaped by nature **and** culture. The exam rewards this balance.

### Queer theory

**Origin.** Term coined by **Teresa de Lauretis** (1990/1991). Key texts: Foucault (1976), Butler, *Gender Trouble* (1990), **Eve Kosofsky Sedgwick**, *Epistemology of the Closet* (1990), Adrienne Rich, "Compulsory Heterosexuality" (1980) as forerunner.

**Main claims.**
1. **Anti-essentialism**: no fixed, natural identities of gender or sexuality.
2. **Heteronormativity**: society treats heterosexuality and the gender binary as the default; queer theory exposes this norm. Butler's "heterosexual matrix": sex → gender → desire assumed to line up.
3. **Performativity** (Butler): gender is the repeated performance of acts, gestures and styles; it creates the illusion of an inner gender. Not a theatrical choice, but repetition under social pressure. Drag shows this by exposing gender as imitation (2026 MCQ: "stylized repetition of acts").
4. **Power and discourse** (Foucault): modern power does not just repress sexuality; it produces categories (the "homosexual" as a type of person was a 19th-century medical invention). This is Foucault's critique of the "repressive hypothesis" (2026 MCQ).
5. **Deconstruction** of binaries (man/woman, straight/gay, natural/unnatural).

**Criticisms.**
- Too abstract; ignores material inequality (Marxist and radical feminists, e.g. Martha Nussbaum's 1999 critique of Butler, "The Professor of Parody").
- Dissolving "woman" may weaken feminist politics, which needs a subject to represent.
- Western and urban; may not translate into societies like Pakistan, where identities are organised by kinship and religion.
- Clashes with religious and cultural norms; politically contentious.

**Pakistan link (asked 2026).** The **Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Act 2018** allowed people to register their gender by **self-perceived gender identity** with NADRA and banned discrimination in jobs, education, health and inheritance. In **May 2023 the Federal Shariat Court** held key sections (the definition of "gender identity" and "transgender person" based on self-perception, the right to change gender on records, and the inheritance section as framed) to be against Islamic injunctions. Appeals were filed before the Shariat Appellate Bench of the Supreme Court (2023); I could not confirm a final decision by Nov 2026 [verify]. The debate shows the clash between constructionist (self-identification) and biological/religious definitions. See `10-Beyond-the-Syllabus.md`.

## 2.4 Masculinities and femininity (asked 2021, 2023, 2026)

### Definitions

- **Femininity**: the set of traits, roles and behaviours a society expects of women (care, modesty, softness, beauty, emotional expression, domestic skill).
- **Masculinity**: the traits expected of men (strength, rationality, protection, breadwinning, control of emotion, honour).
- Both are plural ("masculinities", "femininities") and change across class, ethnicity, region and time.

### Raewyn (R.W.) Connell, *Gender and Power* (1987), *Masculinities* (1995)

Four kinds of masculinity in a hierarchy:

| Type | Meaning | Pakistani illustration |
|---|---|---|
| **Hegemonic** | The culturally dominant ideal that legitimises men's power over women and over other men. Few men fully live it, but most benefit | The "ghairatmand" protector-provider who controls family women's mobility and honour |
| **Complicit** | Men who do not embody the ideal but gain the "patriarchal dividend" | Men who disapprove of violence but accept household privilege |
| **Subordinated** | Masculinities pushed down, especially gay men or "effeminate" men | Mockery of men who do housework or are seen as soft |
| **Marginalised** | Men of subordinated class or ethnicity, even if they meet some hegemonic traits | Poor rural labourers, migrants |

Connell also uses **emphasised femininity**: the femininity that accommodates men's interests (compliance, care, attractiveness). There is no hegemonic femininity, because femininities are organised around subordination.

### Other key thinkers

- **Michael Kimmel**, *Manhood in America* (1996), *Guyland* (2008): masculinity is a "homosocial enactment" — men prove manhood to other men.
- **Jack Halberstam**, *Female Masculinity* (1998): masculinity is not tied to male bodies.
- **bell hooks**, *The Will to Change* (2004): patriarchy also damages men emotionally.
- **Sandra Bem** (1974): androgyny — high in both "masculine" and "feminine" traits; she argued androgynous people are more adaptable. Gender schema theory (1981).
- Toxic masculinity: popular term for harmful norms (dominance, violence, suppression of emotion). Use carefully; scholars prefer "harmful masculine norms".

### Where do gender roles come from? (2023) — the theories

| Theory | Explanation | Thinker | Critique |
|---|---|---|---|
| Biological / evolutionary | Hormones (testosterone), brain differences, reproductive strategies make men competitive and women nurturing | E.O. Wilson (sociobiology), David Buss | Overstates fixed traits; cannot explain variation across cultures or fast change |
| Psychoanalytic | Children identify with the same-sex parent; girls stay attached to mothers, boys separate | Freud; Nancy Chodorow, *The Reproduction of Mothering* (1978) | Hard to test; Western family model |
| Social learning | Children copy models and are rewarded or punished for gender-appropriate behaviour | Albert Bandura; Walter Mischel (1966) | Treats children as passive |
| Cognitive development | Children build a concept of gender (gender constancy by about age 6) and then seek matching behaviour | Lawrence Kohlberg (1966); Bem's gender schema theory | Underplays culture |
| Functionalist | Roles serve family and society: instrumental and expressive | Parsons and Bales (1955) | Justifies inequality; ignores conflict |
| Feminist / conflict | Roles maintain male power and the gendered division of labour | Millett, Walby, Marxist feminists | May ignore biology and agency |
| Interactionist | Gender is "done" and policed in daily interaction | West and Zimmerman (1987) | Less on structure |
| Post-structuralist | Discourse and performance create gender | Butler, Foucault | Abstract |

**Agents of socialisation in Pakistan.** Family (son preference, boys served first, girls trained in housework), school textbooks (women shown as mothers, nurses; men as soldiers, leaders — documented in textbook reviews by researchers such as Rubina Saigol and A.H. Nayyar), religion as taught locally, media (dramas showing the suffering, obedient wife), peer groups, and the state (laws on guardianship, mobility norms in public spaces).

### Masculinity studies and violence (2026 short note)

- Violence is often a way to prove or restore masculinity when a man feels his honour or control is threatened (Connell; Kimmel).
- Honour (ghairat) codes make male identity depend on controlling female sexuality, so women's choices are read as attacks on male honour: a driver of honour killing.
- Economic stress (unemployment, inflation) can make men unable to meet the breadwinner ideal; some respond with control and violence ("masculinity crisis").
- Implication: anti-violence work must engage men and boys (e.g. UN Women's HeForShe, 2014; Rozan's work with men in Pakistan [verify organisation programme names]).

## 2.5 Nature versus culture (nurture) debate (2018, 2021)

| | Nature (essentialism) | Culture/nurture (constructionism) |
|---|---|---|
| Claim | Gender differences come from biology: genes, hormones, brain structure, evolution | Gender differences come from socialisation, culture, institutions and power |
| Evidence | Prenatal hormone exposure (e.g. congenital adrenal hyperplasia studies); average sex differences in physical strength; cross-cultural pattern of male violence | Mead's three societies; huge change in women's education and work within two generations; differences within each sex larger than between sexes; the John/Joan (David Reimer) case showed limits of pure nurture but also of medical intervention |
| Politics | Used to justify separate roles ("biology is destiny") | Used to argue for change |
| Feminist users | Cultural feminists sometimes celebrate female nature (care, peace) | Liberal, socialist, postmodern feminists |

**Interactionist / biosocial view (best conclusion).** Biology and culture interact. Biology gives tendencies and capacities; culture amplifies, suppresses or reinterprets them. Example: average male strength mattered for ploughing (Boserup's argument about the plough and female seclusion), but in a service economy it matters little — yet norms persist because institutions persist. Sherry Ortner's point: the problem is not biology but the cultural *value* placed on what women and men do.

**Deconstructing "gender" (2020).** The word has several meanings:
1. Grammatical gender (masculine/feminine nouns — in Urdu, Arabic, French).
2. Social roles and identity (sex/gender distinction, Oakley).
3. A system of power relations (patriarchy; Scott 1986: "gender is a primary way of signifying relationships of power").
4. A performance (Butler).
5. A policy category ("gender" in development = women, often; gender mainstreaming).
6. An identity spectrum (transgender, non-binary — contested).
Deconstruction (Derrida) shows each meaning depends on a binary (man/woman) in which one term is privileged.

## 2.6 Ready-made structure for "Is sex also socially constructed?"

1. Introduction: Oakley's distinction; the 1990s challenge.
2. The traditional view: sex biological, gender social.
3. Constructionist arguments: Fausto-Sterling, Laqueur, Butler, measurement, cross-cultural third genders.
4. Counter-arguments: binary reproduction, medicine, gender-critical feminism, religious views.
5. Pakistan: khawaja sira tradition; Supreme Court orders of 2009–2012 recognising a third gender on CNICs; the 2018 Act and the 2023 FSC ruling.
6. Conclusion: biosocial view; categories of sex are biologically grounded but socially drawn and enforced.
