## Model Answer 1: Critically analyse the WID, WAD and GAD approaches to development. Which best suits Pakistan?

*(Asked in 2016, 2021; WID and GAD in 2025; GAD note 2017.)*

**Outline**
1. Introduction
2. Context: from welfare to women in development
3. WID: assumptions, strategies, critique
4. WAD: assumptions, strategies, critique
5. GAD: assumptions, tools, critique
6. Comparative table
7. Pakistan's experience
8. Which suits Pakistan: way forward
9. Conclusion

**Introduction**

For two decades after 1945, development planners treated women mainly as mothers and recipients of welfare. Ester Boserup's *Woman's Role in Economic Development* (1970) broke this view by showing that modernisation had often made women worse off. Three approaches followed: Women in Development (WID), Women and Development (WAD) and Gender and Development (GAD). Each answered a different question: how to add women, on what terms they had been added, and how gender relations themselves must change. This answer argues that GAD offers the most complete analysis, but in Pakistan it has largely been implemented as WID, and real progress needs GAD's strategic agenda backed by state resources.

**From welfare to WID**

Caroline Moser describes the earliest "welfare" approach of the 1950s–60s, which saw women as passive beneficiaries: food aid, nutrition and family planning. Boserup's evidence from Africa and Asia showed that colonial and post-colonial planners gave land titles, credit and technology to men, displacing women from farming roles they had held for centuries. Development was not gender-neutral.

**Women in Development (WID)**

*Assumptions.* WID, a term from the Washington chapter of the Society for International Development, rested on liberal feminism and modernisation theory. It assumed that women were left out of development and that the solution was to integrate them into existing processes. Women were an untapped resource whose inclusion would make development more efficient.

*Strategies.* The US Percy Amendment (1973) required American aid to integrate women. Donors funded women-specific projects: income generation, credit, training and education. Governments created "national machineries" such as women's ministries.

*Achievements.* WID made women visible in statistics and plans, and created institutions that still exist.

*Critique.* First, it accepted existing structures and never asked why women were excluded. Second, it looked at women in isolation and ignored gender relations and men. Third, it ignored women's reproductive work, so income projects added to their burden. Fourth, it treated women as one homogeneous group, ignoring class and ethnicity. Finally, women's projects were small, under-funded add-ons.

**Women and Development (WAD)**

*Assumptions.* WAD emerged in the late 1970s from Marxist and dependency thinking. Lourdes Benería and Gita Sen argued that women had always been part of development; the problem was the unequal terms of their integration into a global capitalist system that exploited both men and women in the South.

*Strategies.* WAD favoured women-only projects to avoid male control and called for changes in international and class structures.

*Critique.* WAD under-theorised patriarchy within households and communities, focusing on class and global inequality. Like WID, it concentrated on productive work. In practice its projects often looked like WID projects.

**Gender and Development (GAD)**

*Assumptions.* GAD arose in the 1980s from socialist feminism and Southern feminist networks such as DAWN (1984). Its focus is gender relations, not women alone. Subordination comes from the social relations of gender, class and global structures, in both production and reproduction. Women are agents of change, not recipients.

*Tools.* Moser's framework (triple roles; practical needs versus strategic interests), Naila Kabeer's social relations approach and her definition of empowerment as resources, agency and achievements, the Harvard analytical framework, gender budgeting and gender mainstreaming, adopted at Beijing in 1995.

*Critique.* GAD is hard to implement because it demands changes in power relations that states and families resist. In practice it is often reduced to WID-style projects with a new name. Mainstreaming can become technical box-ticking, and "gender" can divert attention and resources from women.

**Comparison**

| | WID | WAD | GAD |
|---|---|---|---|
| Theory | Liberal, modernisation | Marxist, dependency | Socialist feminism |
| Focus | Women | Women and global class structures | Gender relations |
| Problem | Exclusion | Unequal integration | Unequal power |
| Solution | Integrate women | Structural change | Transform relations; empower |
| Needs met | Practical | Mainly productive | Practical and strategic |

**Pakistan's experience**

Pakistan has used all three. WID logic shaped the Women's Division (1979), vocational centres, the First Women Bank (1989) and microcredit. WAD-style critiques are heard from labour and left feminist groups on export-led textile growth and IMF-driven austerity, which push women into low-paid home-based work. GAD language appears in the National Policy for Development and Empowerment of Women (2002), the Gender Reform Action Plans, gender-responsive budgeting, reserved seats, the NCSW and BISP, which put cash in women's hands and tied it to CNICs and bank accounts.

Results are mixed. Pakistan ranked 148th of 148 countries in the WEF Global Gender Gap Report 2025 and 143rd of 145 in 2026. Its weakest area is economic participation, with female labour force participation at about 24% (LFS 2024–25). This shows that projects have met some practical needs but have not shifted strategic interests such as land, mobility, safety and decision-making.

**Which suits Pakistan? Way forward**

GAD is the right framework because Pakistan's barriers are relational: honour norms, male control of assets and mobility, and unpaid care. But GAD must be grounded locally:
1. Pair every practical project with a strategic element: microcredit with joint land titles; health clinics with women's voice in health committees.
2. Use gender budgeting in federal and provincial budgets, with audit by the Auditor-General.
3. Enforce the Enforcement of Women's Property Rights Act 2020.
4. Invest in care infrastructure (childcare, safe transport) to raise labour participation.
5. Engage men and religious scholars to shift norms.
6. Reduce donor dependence by funding gender programmes from domestic budgets.

**Conclusion**

WID made women visible, WAD made global inequality visible, and GAD made power visible. Each corrected its predecessor. Pakistan's policies speak the language of GAD but are mostly implemented as WID. Moving from adding women to changing gender relations, backed by the state's own money, is the key to closing one of the world's widest gender gaps.

## Model Answer 2: Trace the evolution of the feminist movement in Pakistan from the Women's Action Forum to the digital activism of the 2020s. How has the focus shifted from legal rights to bodily autonomy?

*(CSS 2026 Q3; similar 2016, 2022, 2024.)*

**Outline**
1. Introduction
2. Roots: pre-partition and welfare feminism
3. WAF and the struggle for legal rights (1981–1988)
4. NGOs, democracy and legislation (1988–2017)
5. Aurat March and digital feminism (2018–present)
6. The shift: legal rights to bodily autonomy
7. Critical assessment
8. Way forward
9. Conclusion

**Introduction**

Pakistan's women's movement has always responded to the state. In the 1980s it fought laws that the state made against women. Today it also challenges norms that families and communities enforce on women's bodies, mobility and voice. This answer traces that journey and argues that the movement has moved from defending legal personhood to asserting bodily autonomy, while legal reform remains its main achievement and implementation its main failure.

**Roots: welfare feminism**

Muslim women's activism began with reformers and associations such as the All India Muslim Ladies' Conference (1914) and women's participation in the Pakistan Movement under Fatima Jinnah and Begum Jahanara Shahnawaz. After 1947, APWA (1949), founded by Begum Ra'ana Liaquat Ali Khan, focused on welfare, education and health. It supported the Muslim Family Laws Ordinance 1961, which regulated polygamy and divorce. This was elite, state-friendly and reformist.

**WAF and legal rights (1981–1988)**

Zia-ul-Haq's Islamisation changed everything. The Hudood Ordinances (1979) meant that a woman who reported rape but could not prove it risked prosecution for zina. The proposed Law of Evidence reduced women's testimony in financial matters. The Women's Action Forum was founded in Karachi in September 1981 after a couple was sentenced under the Zina Ordinance. On 12 February 1983, WAF and women lawyers marched on The Mall in Lahore against the draft evidence law and were baton-charged by police. The Safia Bibi case (1983), in which a blind rape survivor was convicted of zina, drew national outrage until the conviction was overturned.

WAF was non-party, non-hierarchical and volunteer-led. Its language was legal: equal citizenship, repeal of discriminatory laws, democracy. Khawar Mumtaz and Farida Shaheed described this period as "two steps forward, one step back".

**NGOs, democracy and legislation (1988–2017)**

With democracy and the Beijing Conference (1995), the movement professionalised. Shirkat Gah, Aurat Foundation and others produced research and draft laws. Pakistan acceded to CEDAW in 1996. Under Musharraf, 60 reserved seats and 33% local quotas brought women into politics, and the Protection of Women Act 2006 moved rape out of the Zina Ordinance. The Women's Parliamentary Caucus (2008) helped pass a wave of laws: workplace harassment (2010), acid crimes and anti-women practices (2011), provincial domestic violence laws (Sindh 2013, Punjab 2016), and the 2016 honour-killing law after Qandeel Baloch's murder. This was the high point of the legal-rights strategy.

**Aurat March and digital feminism (2018–present)**

The first Aurat March took place in Karachi on 8 March 2018, organised by the Hum Auratein collective, and spread to other cities. A younger, social-media-literate generation raised issues of harassment, consent, public space, unpaid care, and minority and trans rights. Slogans such as "Mera Jism, Meri Marzi" provoked a national backlash, court petitions and television confrontations. Online campaigns followed the motorway gang-rape (2020) and Noor Mukadam's murder (2021). Digital Rights Foundation's helpline addressed cyber-harassment. New laws continued: the Anti-Rape Act 2021, the ICT Child Marriage Restraint Act 2025 and the ICT Domestic Violence Act 2026.

**The shift: from legal rights to bodily autonomy**

1. *Target*: from discriminatory laws made by a military state to norms enforced by families, communities and the internet.
2. *Agenda*: from equal testimony and repeal of the Hudood laws to consent, harassment, mobility, reproductive choice and freedom from violence at home.
3. *Method*: from lawyers' petitions and press conferences to marches, art, hashtags and personal testimony.
4. *Base*: from professional elites to students, workers and minorities, though still mainly urban.
5. *Theory*: from liberal feminism to radical ("the personal is political"), intersectional and fourth-wave feminism.

**The role of courts, media and the state**

The movement's tools changed along with its goals. In the 1980s courts were a defensive arena: lawyers such as Asma Jahangir and Hina Jilani defended women charged under the Hudood laws, and the Federal Shariat Court's reversal in the Safia Bibi case showed that judicial pressure could work. In the 2000s the state became a partner: Musharraf's government created the NCSW and reserved seats, and later elected governments let the Women's Parliamentary Caucus lead law-making. In the 2010s private television and then social media became central. Sharmeen Obaid-Chinoy's Oscar-winning documentaries on acid attacks and honour killing pushed Parliament directly; Qandeel Baloch's murder, debated intensely online, sped up the 2016 honour law. Since 2018 activists have used Instagram, X and WhatsApp to organise marches, share testimonies of harassment and crowdfund legal support. The same tools also expose them to coordinated trolling and threats, so the digital sphere is both a new platform and a new site of violence.

**Critical assessment**

Strengths: the movement has won major laws, kept women's issues on the national agenda and adapted to new media. Weaknesses: it remains urban and middle-class, with little reach among rural and working women; donor dependence earlier created "NGO-isation"; and the Aurat March's framing allowed opponents to call it Western. Laws are weakly implemented: police reluctance, out-of-court settlements and low conviction rates persist. Critics argue that bodily-autonomy language alienates religious majorities; supporters respond that safety and consent are universal values grounded in Islam's emphasis on dignity.

**Way forward**

1. Build rural and working-class leadership through peasant, home-based worker and Lady Health Worker networks.
2. Frame demands in constitutional language (Articles 9, 14, 25, 34) and Islamic principles of justice.
3. Shift from law-making to implementation monitoring: GBV courts, protection orders, police data.
4. Protect activists from online harassment through PECA enforcement.
5. Build alliances with men, unions and student groups.

**Conclusion**

The movement began by defending women's legal personhood against the state and now asserts women's ownership of their own bodies, time and public space. The shift reflects success: once some laws were won, activists could confront the everyday power that laws do not reach. Its future depends on carrying that agenda beyond cities and from statute books into police stations and homes.

## Model Answer 3: What are the root causes of gender-based violence in Pakistan? How do they intersect, and what practical strategies can eliminate it?

*(CSS 2025 Q2; similar 2016, 2017, 2023.)*

**Outline**
1. Introduction and definition
2. Forms and scale
3. Theoretical lens: the ecological model
4. Root causes by level
5. How the causes intersect
6. Existing legal and institutional response
7. Practical strategies (4Ps)
8. Conclusion

**Introduction**

The UN Declaration on the Elimination of Violence against Women (1993) defines violence against women as any act of gender-based violence that results in, or is likely to result in, physical, sexual or psychological harm to women, including threats and deprivation of liberty, in public or private life. In Pakistan, GBV ranges from domestic abuse to honour killings, acid attacks and online harassment. This answer argues that GBV is not the product of individual deviance but of interacting causes at individual, family, community and state levels, so it can be ended only by acting at every level at once.

**Forms and scale**

Forms include domestic violence, marital rape, honour killings (karo-kari), acid attacks, forced and child marriage, vani and swara, denial of inheritance, workplace and street harassment, custodial abuse and cyber-harassment. The Pakistan Demographic and Health Survey 2017–18 found that over a quarter of ever-married women aged 15–49 had experienced physical violence. HRCP records hundreds of honour killings each year, and many more go unreported. Globally, the WHO estimates that about one in three women experience violence in their lifetime.

**Theoretical lens**

Feminist theory sees GBV as a tool to maintain male dominance. Social learning theory (Bandura) explains how violence passes between generations. Resource theory (Goode) explains why men lacking other power may use violence. Galtung shows how direct violence rests on structural and cultural violence. Lori Heise's ecological model (1998) combines these into four levels and is the most useful framework for Pakistan.

**Root causes**

*Individual level.* Witnessing or suffering abuse in childhood, low education, drug use and frustration from unemployment.

*Relationship level.* Male control over income and decisions, large age gaps, marriages without consent, dowry disputes and conflict with in-laws in joint families.

*Community level.* Honour codes that tie family respect to women's sexuality; jirgas and panchayats that order punishments such as vani and, in Mukhtaran Mai's case, rape; acceptance of wife-beating (PDHS data show many women and men justify it for at least one reason); poverty; low female literacy.

*Society and state level.* Patriarchal norms reinforced by misinterpretation of religion; the qisas and diyat law that allowed heirs to forgive killers; slow courts and low conviction rates; police reluctance to register cases; media dramas that normalise abuse; economic crisis and inflation.

**How the causes intersect**

The causes reinforce each other. A poor rural woman married young, with no income and no land, living in a district where jirgas settle disputes and the nearest shelter is hours away, faces violence that every level sustains. Her family discourages a police report to protect honour; the police push for a compromise; the court accepts a pardon. Class, ethnicity, religion and disability add layers. Minority girls in Sindh face forced conversion and marriage; women with disabilities face higher abuse and fewer services. This is what Kimberlé Crenshaw called intersectionality: overlapping identities produce distinct and deeper vulnerability.

**Consequences**

GBV harms far more than its immediate victims. For women it means injury, disability, death, unwanted pregnancy, depression and suicide. For children it means trauma and a higher chance of repeating the cycle as adults, which social learning theory predicts. Economically, violence reduces women's work and earnings and raises health and justice costs; UN Women estimates the global cost at around 2% of world GDP. Politically, fear of harassment keeps women out of public life and lowers their turnout. Socially, honour-based violence teaches whole communities that women's choices can be punished, which narrows the freedom of women who are never directly attacked. For Pakistan, high levels of violence undermine its commitments under CEDAW and SDG 5, and they weaken the country's GSP+ trade status, which is tied to human-rights conventions.

**Existing response**

Pakistan's laws have improved: the Protection of Women Act 2006, the workplace harassment law (2010, amended 2022), the acid and anti-women practices laws (2011), provincial domestic violence laws (Sindh 2013, Balochistan 2014, Punjab 2016, KP 2021), the honour-killing law (2016), the Anti-Rape (Investigation and Trial) Act 2021 and the ICT Domestic Violence (Prevention and Protection) Act 2026. Institutions include Violence Against Women Centres in Punjab, women police stations, GBV courts, helplines (1043, 1099) and the NCSW. The problem is implementation.

**Why laws alone have not worked**

Laws are only as strong as the people who enforce them. Police officers often share the same honour norms as the families involved and press victims to compromise. Prosecutors are overloaded, forensic evidence is weak outside big cities, and survivors withdraw under family pressure. Shelters (Darul Amans) are few and sometimes treat women like detainees. Strategy must therefore target implementation and attitudes, not only statutes.

**Practical strategies**

*Prevention*
1. School curricula on respect, consent and non-violence.
2. Programmes with men and boys to challenge harmful masculinity; sermons by religious scholars against violence.
3. Media codes against glamorising abuse in dramas.
4. Economic empowerment: BISP payments, enforcement of inheritance rights, jobs.

*Protection*
5. One-stop crisis centres (the Multan VAWC model) in every district, with shelter, medical and legal help.
6. Protection orders enforced by trained police; helplines connected to rapid response.

*Prosecution*
7. Make all GBV offences non-compoundable; extend the 2016 honour law's logic to other crimes.
8. Special sexual offences units, DNA labs and time-bound trials in GBV courts.
9. Action against jirgas that act as courts.

*Partnership and data*
10. Regular national surveys on violence; case tracking by the NCSW and provincial commissions; a budget line for implementation.

**Conclusion**

GBV in Pakistan grows where poverty, honour codes and weak institutions meet. No single law can end it, because it is sustained at every level from the household to the courtroom. The country already has much of the legal framework; the next step is to fund and enforce it, while changing the norms that make violence seem normal. When the cost of violence rises and its social acceptance falls, the cycle can be broken.

## Model Answer 4: Elaborate the historical evolution of women's quotas in Pakistan and assess their impact on women's political mainstreaming.

*(CSS 2025 Q5; similar 2016, 2021, 2024.)*

**Outline**
1. Introduction
2. Why quotas: theory
3. Evolution, 1947–2026
4. Impact: gains
5. Impact: limits
6. Comparative lessons
7. Reforms
8. Conclusion

**Introduction**

Political quotas are measures that reserve a minimum share of seats or candidacies for women. Pakistan has used reserved seats since its first constitution, with long gaps. Today 60 of 336 National Assembly seats, 17 of 96 Senate seats and 132 provincial seats are reserved for women, and parties must give women at least 5% of general-seat tickets. This answer traces that history and argues that quotas have given women presence and produced important laws, but have not yet given them independent power.

**Why quotas?**

Drude Dahlerup distinguishes the slow "incremental track" of Nordic countries from the "fast track" of quotas. CEDAW Article 4 allows temporary special measures, and the Beijing Platform set a 30% target. The case for quotas rests on justice, the critical mass argument (women need about 30% to change institutional culture), role models, and better policy for women. The case against points to merit, tokenism and elite capture.

**Evolution**

| Period | Provision |
|---|---|
| 1947 | Two women in the first Constituent Assembly |
| 1956 | 10 reserved NA seats (5 per wing) |
| 1962 | 6 reserved seats, indirectly elected |
| 1973 | 10 seats for 10 years or three elections; 5% in provincial assemblies |
| 1985 | Raised to 20 seats |
| 1988–2002 | Quota lapsed after the 1988 election; very few women in assemblies |
| 2000–01 | Devolution plan: 33% of local council seats for women |
| 2002 | Legal Framework Order: 60 NA seats, 17 Senate seats, 128 provincial seats, filled by proportional representation based on general seats won |
| 2017 | Elections Act: 5% party tickets for women (s. 206); poll may be voided if women's turnout is under 10% (s. 9) |
| 2018 | 25th Amendment: NA reduced to 336 seats with 60 women's seats retained; provincial women's seats now 132 |

**Impact: gains**

1. *Presence.* Women's share of NA seats rose from under 3% in the 1990s to around 17–20% since 2002.
2. *Legislation.* The Women's Parliamentary Caucus, formed in 2008, worked across party lines to pass the workplace harassment law (2010), the acid and anti-women practices laws (2011), the NCSW Act (2012), and the honour and rape laws (2016). Women members later pushed the 2025 child marriage law and the 2026 domestic violence law in Islamabad.
3. *Activity.* Farzana Bari's assessment (2002) and FAFEN's parliamentary monitoring have repeatedly found reserved-seat women among the most active members in questions, resolutions and bills.
4. *Normalisation and leadership.* Pakistan has had a woman Speaker (Fehmida Mirza, 2008), a woman Foreign Minister (Hina Rabbani Khar, 2011) and a woman Chief Minister (Maryam Nawaz, 2024).
5. *Local politics.* The 33% local quota (2001–05) brought tens of thousands of women, many rural and working-class, into councils for the first time.

**Impact: limits**

1. *Indirect selection.* Reserved-seat members are chosen from party lists by leaders, so they owe loyalty to leaders rather than voters, and are often called "nominated".
2. *Elite and family capture.* Seats often go to relatives of party leaders.
3. *Ghettoisation.* Parties use reserved seats as an excuse not to field women on general seats; the 5% ticket rule is met with unwinnable seats. Only around a dozen women win general NA seats.
4. *No constituency, little power.* Reserved-seat members lack constituencies and rarely hold finance, interior or defence portfolios or chair powerful committees.
5. *Local regression.* Post-2013 provincial local government laws reduced women's share in many councils.
6. *Weak link to outcomes.* Despite quotas, Pakistan's political empowerment score in the WEF index remains low, and the electoral rolls still had roughly 9–10 million more men than women in 2025.

**Women as voters: the other half of mainstreaming**

Political mainstreaming is not only about seats. Women's power as voters shapes how parties treat them. In 2018 the electoral rolls had about 12.5 million more men than women; by 2025 the gap had fallen to about 7%, helped by NADRA's mobile registration and by BISP, which requires a CNIC and so pushed millions of women to register. The Elections Act 2017 lets the ECP void a poll where women's turnout is below 10%, aimed at local agreements that barred women from voting, as in a Lower Dir by-election in 2015. In the 2024 election women's turnout was about 43% against about 52% for men, according to FAFEN. As women's votes grow, parties have more reason to field women and address their concerns. Quotas and voter registration therefore work together: one gives women seats, the other gives them leverage.

**Comparative lessons**

Rwanda's constitutional quota produced a majority-female lower house. India reserves one-third of panchayat seats by rotating constituencies, which gives women a direct electorate, and passed the Women's Reservation Act in 2023 for Parliament. Bangladesh, like Pakistan, uses indirectly filled reserved seats and faces the same "nominated" criticism. The lesson: quotas work best when women are directly elected and when party candidate quotas cover winnable seats.

**Reforms**

1. Direct election to reserved seats, through rotating women-only constituencies or dual-member constituencies.
2. Raise the ticket quota to at least 10–15% and require that a share be in seats the party won last time.
3. Restore 33% women's seats in all local governments.
4. Quotas for committee chairs and cabinet positions.
5. Public funding and training for women candidates.
6. Close the registration gap through NADRA–ECP drives and BISP-linked CNIC registration.

**Conclusion**

Quotas brought Pakistani women into the assemblies and, through cross-party cooperation, into the law-making process. They have produced presence and some influence, but not yet independent power, because women are selected by party leaders and kept out of key portfolios. The next stage is to make women accountable to voters rather than to leaders. Then quotas can move from a fast track into assemblies to a fast track into power.

## Model Answer 5: Evaluate Pakistan's performance in the World Economic Forum's Global Gender Gap Report. Identify the socio-economic and political factors behind its ranking.

*(CSS 2026 Q4 asked about the 2025 report; similar 2018 Q5. This answer covers 2025 and the 2026 update.)*

**Outline**
1. Introduction
2. The index and Pakistan's rank
3. Economic participation and opportunity
4. Educational attainment
5. Health and survival
6. Political empowerment
7. Cross-cutting causes
8. Limits of the index
9. Way forward
10. Conclusion

**Introduction**

The World Economic Forum's Global Gender Gap Index, published since 2006, measures the gaps between women and men in four areas: economic participation and opportunity, educational attainment, health and survival, and political empowerment. A score of 100% means parity. In the 2025 report Pakistan ranked last, 148th of 148 countries, with a parity score of 56.7%, slightly down from 57% in 2024. In the 2026 report it rose to 143rd of 145, with 59.5%, its best year-on-year gain in a decade. This answer argues that Pakistan's low rank is driven above all by women's exclusion from the economy, rooted in social norms, unpaid care and weak institutions, while education shows that rapid progress is possible.

**The index and Pakistan's rank**

| Year | Rank | Score |
|---|---|---|
| 2006 | — | 54.3% |
| 2024 | 145 of 146 | 57.0% |
| 2025 | 148 of 148 | 56.7% |
| 2026 | 143 of 145 | 59.5% |

At this pace of change Pakistan would need generations to reach parity.

**Economic participation and opportunity**

This is the weakest area: 144th in 2026 with 36.4%, last in South Asia. Female labour force participation was about 24% in the Labour Force Survey 2024–25 (22.7% on the new international definition), against roughly 68% for men. Most employed women work in agriculture, often as unpaid family helpers, or in home-based piece-rate work outside labour law. Very few are senior officials, legislators or managers, and wage gaps are wide.

Causes: norms that see men as providers and women's work outside the home as a threat to family honour; the burden of unpaid care with almost no childcare; unsafe transport and harassment; employers' bias; a skills mismatch; and an economy that creates too few formal jobs. Educated women face high unemployment: PBS data reported in early 2026 showed about a quarter of highly educated women job-seekers unemployed.

**Educational attainment**

This is the most improved area. In 2026 Pakistan reached a record 91.7% (130th). Women now match or exceed men in tertiary enrolment. But basic gaps remain: the 2023 Census put female literacy at 52.8% against 68% for men, and only about a third of women in Balochistan are literate. Around 25 million children are out of school, most of them girls. Causes include distance, few girls' secondary schools, a shortage of female teachers, early marriage, poverty, insecurity and climate disasters.

**Health and survival**

Pakistan ranks low (about 127th in 2026). Son preference skews the sex ratio and family investment. The maternal mortality ratio has fallen from 276 per 100,000 live births in 2006 to about 155 in 2023 (UN estimate), but remains high, especially in Balochistan. Malnutrition and anaemia are widespread, fertility is high, and women often need male permission to seek care.

**Political empowerment**

Pakistan improved 11 places in 2026, partly because of more women in senior positions, including the first woman Chief Minister of Punjab (2024). But women hold only about 17% of elected seats, mostly reserved seats chosen by parties, and few hold key ministries. Only one woman has led a government in Pakistan's history.

**Cross-cutting causes**

1. *Patriarchal norms and honour codes* that restrict mobility and choice.
2. *Implementation gaps*: laws exist (harassment 2010, honour crimes 2016, property rights 2020, domestic violence 2026 in Islamabad) but enforcement is weak.
3. *Low public spending* on health and education.
4. *Economic crisis* and IMF-driven austerity that cut services and push women into informal work.
5. *Population growth* (2.55% a year) that strains services.
6. *Devolution without capacity* after the 18th Amendment.
7. *Contested religious interpretations* used to oppose reform, such as the Council of Islamic Ideology's opposition to the 2025 child marriage law.

**Regional comparison**

Pakistan's neighbours show that the gap is not destiny. Bangladesh, with a similar history and income level, ranked 79th in 2026, the best in South Asia, largely because of women's employment in the garment industry, strong NGOs in health and microfinance, and decades of women heads of government. Sri Lanka and Nepal also rank well ahead of Pakistan on education and health. India sits near Pakistan at the bottom of the region on economic participation. The comparison suggests that economic opportunity, safe mobility and a sustained state commitment to girls' schooling and family planning, not religion or culture alone, explain the differences.

**Limits of the index**

The index measures gaps, not levels, and uses a narrow set of indicators. It ignores unpaid work, violence and informal employment, and some data are dated or survey-based. Pakistan's government has sometimes questioned its methods. Still, its consistency over twenty years makes it a useful mirror, and investors and donors read it.

**Way forward**

1. *Economy*: childcare at workplaces, safe transport (women-only buses, Safe City cameras), enforce maternity leave and harassment laws, digital and freelancing skills, credit for women entrepreneurs, and count unpaid care through time-use surveys.
2. *Education*: build girls' middle and high schools, recruit female teachers, scale stipends and enforce Article 25-A.
3. *Health*: expand Lady Health Workers and community midwives, emergency obstetric care, nutrition programmes and minimum marriage age 18 nationwide.
4. *Politics*: direct election to women's seats, a larger party-ticket quota, women in cabinet portfolios beyond "soft" ministries.
5. *Institutions*: fund the NCSW and provincial commissions, track outcomes with gender statistics, and use gender-responsive budgeting.

**Conclusion**

Pakistan's last place in 2025 and modest rise in 2026 show both the depth of the problem and the possibility of change. Education proves that targeted investment can close gaps fast; the economy shows how norms and missing infrastructure keep half the population out of work. If Pakistan treats women's economic participation as an economic policy, not a social favour, its ranking will follow.

## Model Answer 6: Critically evaluate the concept of "gendered globalisation" with reference to world-system theory. How has global capitalism created new forms of patriarchal power?

*(CSS 2025 Q3; globalisation also 2017, 2018, 2019.)*

**Outline**
1. Introduction
2. Globalisation and "gendered globalisation"
3. World-system theory
4. Gendering the world-system
5. New forms of patriarchal power
6. Counter-view: globalisation as opportunity
7. Pakistan
8. Way forward
9. Conclusion

**Introduction**

Globalisation is the growing integration of economies and societies through trade, capital, technology, migration and communication. Feminist scholars argue that this process is not gender-neutral: it relies on, and reshapes, gender inequality. This answer uses Immanuel Wallerstein's world-system theory to show how global capitalism extracts value from women's paid and unpaid labour, and argues that it has created new forms of patriarchal power even as it opens some opportunities.

**"Gendered globalisation"**

The term describes how globalisation affects women and men differently and how gender relations shape globalisation. Women are recruited as cheap and flexible labour, carry the burden of cuts in public services, migrate as care workers, and face new forms of violence and control. At the same time, global norms (CEDAW, the Beijing Platform, the SDGs) and technologies spread ideas of equality.

**World-system theory**

Wallerstein's *The Modern World-System* (1974) describes a single capitalist world-economy since the sixteenth century, divided into a core (high wages, high technology), a semi-periphery and a periphery (raw materials and cheap labour). Unequal exchange transfers surplus from the periphery to the core. The theory explains global inequality structurally rather than blaming the culture of poor countries, as modernisation theory did.

**Gendering the world-system**

Wallerstein's own analysis was largely gender-blind, but feminist scholars extended it:
1. *The household as a unit*: Wallerstein and Joan Smith showed that households pool wages with subsistence and unpaid work. Women's unpaid labour lowers the wage employers must pay. It is a hidden subsidy to global capital.
2. *Housewifisation*: Maria Mies argued that capitalism defines women as housewives so that their earnings are seen as "supplementary", justifying low pay, even when they produce for world markets.
3. *Feminisation of labour*: Guy Standing showed that global competition favours flexible, insecure, low-paid jobs, filled first by women and then spreading to men.
4. *Nimble fingers*: Diane Elson and Ruth Pearson showed how export factories recruit young women as "naturally" dexterous and docile, a social construction that keeps wages low.
5. *Global care chains*: Arlie Hochschild described women from the periphery migrating to care for families in the core, leaving their own children with relatives.

**New forms of patriarchal power**

1. *Corporate patriarchy*: control of women's labour by distant brands and subcontractors who set prices and deadlines but deny responsibility for factory conditions. The Baldia factory fire in Karachi (2012) and the Rana Plaza collapse in Bangladesh (2013) show the cost.
2. *State patriarchy under debt*: structural adjustment and austerity shift care from the state to women's unpaid time; Diane Elson called women the "shock absorbers".
3. *Migration regimes*: the kafala sponsorship system in the Gulf ties domestic workers to employers, enabling abuse.
4. *Trafficking and the sex trade*, organised across borders.
5. *Commodification of bodies*: global media and beauty industries set norms, such as fairness creams in South Asia.
6. *Fundamentalist backlash*: Valentine Moghadam shows how identity movements reacting to globalisation often focus on controlling women's dress and mobility.
7. *Digital patriarchy*: online harassment, deepfakes and surveillance, and platform work without rights.

**Counter-view**

Liberal economists argue that export jobs give women income, independence and bargaining power. Research on Bangladesh's garment sector found that girls stayed in school longer and married later where factory jobs were available. Global norms gave women's movements leverage: the EU's GSP+ scheme links trade preferences to implementing CEDAW. Mobile technology has opened home-based work. Critics of world-system theory also say it is too economistic and ignores women's agency.

**Theoretical assessment**

How does world-system theory compare with other lenses? Modernisation theory would see globalisation as the route by which traditional societies become modern and gender-equal; the evidence is mixed, since women's employment grows but often in poor conditions. Liberal feminism focuses on removing legal barriers to women's market participation, but cannot explain why so many women enter only the lowest-paid jobs. Dependency theory shares world-system theory's structural focus but treats nations as units, while world-system theory adds the household and semi-periphery, which makes it better at explaining how unpaid female labour subsidises global production. Postcolonial feminists such as Chandra Mohanty add that women in the South are not just victims; they organise, unionise and resist. The most convincing account combines world-system theory's structure with attention to women's agency and to local patriarchies that predate capitalism.

**Pakistan**

Pakistan sits on the periphery or semi-periphery. Its main exports, textiles and garments, rely on low-paid women in stitching and home-based work, often through layers of subcontractors. Male migration to the Gulf leaves women managing households, which brings more decision-making but also more work. IMF programmes, most recently the 2024 Extended Fund Facility, raised energy prices and cut development spending, hitting women as managers of household budgets. On the positive side, about 8 million more women came online in 2024, and the mobile internet gender gap fell from 38% to 25% (GSMA 2025), opening freelancing and online business. Yet female labour force participation remains about 24%.

**Way forward**

1. Enforce labour laws in export supply chains, with brand accountability and social audits.
2. Extend social security to home-based workers under provincial laws.
3. Protect health and education spending in IMF programmes; assess their gender impact.
4. Protect migrant women through bilateral agreements and pre-departure training.
5. Use GSP+ leverage for gender reforms.
6. Expand digital skills and online safety.

**Conclusion**

World-system theory shows that global capitalism depends on women's paid and unpaid labour, and feminist scholars show how it reshapes patriarchy rather than ending it. Control over women now comes not only from fathers and husbands but from brands, debt regimes, migration laws and platforms. Globalisation also brings tools for resistance. The task is to capture its opportunities while making its new forms of power accountable.

## Model Answer 7: Differentiate gender studies from women's studies. Write a note on the status of gender studies in Pakistan and give your views on the autonomy versus integration debate.

*(Combines CSS 2023 Q2 and Q3; similar 2016, 2017, 2018, 2019, 2021.)*

**Outline**
1. Introduction
2. Origins of women's studies
3. The shift to gender studies
4. Key differences (table)
5. Status of gender studies in Pakistan
6. The autonomy versus integration debate
7. My view for Pakistan
8. Conclusion

**Introduction**

Gender studies is an interdisciplinary field that examines how gender is socially constructed and how it shapes power, identity and inequality. It grew out of women's studies, which began in the 1970s to make women visible in knowledge. This answer distinguishes the two, assesses the field in Pakistan, and argues for a dual strategy in the autonomy versus integration debate.

**Origins of women's studies**

Second-wave feminists noticed that traditional disciplines treated men's lives as the norm. History left out women, economics ignored unpaid work, and psychology built theories on male subjects. The first women's studies programme began at San Diego State University in 1970, and hundreds followed. Their aim was to recover women's experiences and challenge bias.

**The shift to gender studies**

From the late 1970s scholars moved from "women" to "gender". Ann Oakley's distinction between sex and gender (1972), Gayle Rubin's "sex/gender system" (1975) and Joan Scott's essay on gender as a category of analysis (1986) showed that inequality is relational: femininity and masculinity are defined against each other. Masculinity studies (R.W. Connell), queer theory (Judith Butler) and intersectionality (Kimberlé Crenshaw) broadened the field.

**Key differences**

| Basis | Women's studies | Gender studies |
|---|---|---|
| Period | 1970s | 1980s onwards |
| Subject | Women's lives, oppression, achievements | Gender as a system shaping women, men and gender-diverse people |
| Men | Mostly absent | Studied as gendered beings |
| Theory | Liberal, radical, Marxist feminism | Adds constructionism, postmodernism, queer theory, intersectionality |
| Aim | Make women visible | Transform how all knowledge treats gender |

Some scholars welcomed the shift as more inclusive. Others warned that "gender" can hide women's specific oppression and sound neutral enough to please donors and administrators. The best view is that the two are stages of one field, with women still central because they remain the main disadvantaged group.

**Status of gender studies in Pakistan**

*History.* In 1989 the Ministry of Women Development set up women's studies centres in several public universities, including Quaid-i-Azam University, Karachi, Punjab, Peshawar and Balochistan. Many later became gender studies departments offering BS, MPhil and PhD degrees. Since 2016 Gender Studies has been a CSS optional subject and is now the most chosen subject in its group: 8,245 candidates took it in 2025.

*Strengths.* The field has a presence in most major universities; strong links with activism and NGOs have produced policy-relevant research that fed into laws on harassment (2010), honour killing (2016) and domestic violence; and data are improving through the NCSW, provincial commissions and PBS.

*Weaknesses.* Rubina Saigol and Farzana Bari, both on the FPSC reading list, identified chronic problems that persist: small and under-funded departments; Western-centred syllabi with few local or Urdu texts; dependence on donor funding; social suspicion that the field is "Western" or anti-religious; weak research culture; and little influence on policy. CSS demand has also encouraged guidebook learning rather than research.

**The autonomy versus integration debate**

Gloria Bowles and Renate Duelli Klein, in *Theories of Women's Studies* (1983), framed the question: should the field have autonomous departments with its own staff, degrees and budget, or should gender be integrated into every existing discipline?

*Autonomy* protects new feminist knowledge from being absorbed and diluted by male-centred disciplines, builds theory and creates careers. Its risk is marginalisation: a small department that others can ignore.

*Integration* (mainstreaming) reaches every student and changes the whole curriculum. Its risk is co-optation: gender reduced to a single lecture, dependent on the goodwill of departments that may not care.

Most scholars, including Bowles and Klein, recommend a dual strategy: autonomous centres to produce knowledge and train specialists, plus integration of gender into all disciplines. The debate itself clarified the field's identity and anticipated today's policy debates on women's ministries versus gender focal points in every ministry.

**Multidisciplinary nature**

The field's breadth explains why both autonomy and integration matter. Gender studies draws on sociology (gender roles, "doing gender"), anthropology (Margaret Mead's cross-cultural evidence), psychology (Sandra Bem's androgyny, Carol Gilligan's ethic of care), economics (unpaid work, Marilyn Waring's critique of GDP), political science (representation and quotas), history (Joan Scott), law (CEDAW, family law), literature and linguistics (representation, gendered language), biology (Anne Fausto-Sterling on sex variation), development studies (Boserup, Moser, Kabeer) and media studies (Laura Mulvey's "male gaze"). Gender cuts across all spheres of life, so no single discipline can explain it. The field is also interdisciplinary, combining methods such as life histories, participatory research and statistics, and it has produced new concepts like intersectionality. This breadth is a strength, but it also creates problems: no single method, competition for budgets, and teachers trained in other disciplines who may treat gender as an add-on.

**My view for Pakistan**

Pakistan needs both, but integration is the weaker half. Autonomous departments should be strengthened through HEC research grants, indigenous textbooks in Urdu and English, and links with the NCSW and statistics bureaus. At the same time, a gender module should be compulsory in civil service training, police academies, law and medical colleges, and teacher training, where attitudes shape daily decisions affecting women. Engaging Islamic scholarship on women's rights would answer the charge that the field is foreign.

**Evidence from practice**

The dual strategy is already visible where it works. Gender studies departments have trained researchers who staff the NCSW, provincial women's commissions and NGOs, and their research shaped laws on harassment and honour crimes. Where integration has happened, as in some law and public administration courses that now teach CEDAW and harassment law, graduates bring gender awareness into courts and offices.

**Conclusion**

Women's studies made women visible; gender studies made gender relations visible. In Pakistan the discipline has won institutional space but not yet intellectual independence or policy influence. A dual strategy, autonomy to build knowledge and integration to spread it, offers the best path.

## Model Answer 8: Critically examine social constructionism and queer theory on the categories of sex and gender. How do they illuminate the debate over the Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Act 2018 in Pakistan?

*(CSS 2026 Q2; "Is sex also a social construct?" 2024; sex vs gender 2018.)*

**Outline**
1. Introduction
2. The sex/gender distinction
3. Social constructionism
4. Queer theory and the problem of "sex"
5. Critiques
6. The Transgender Act 2018 and the 2023 Federal Shariat Court ruling
7. What the theories illuminate
8. A balanced way forward
9. Conclusion

**Introduction**

For most of history, sex and gender were treated as one natural fact. Feminist scholarship first separated them, then questioned whether even sex is purely natural. These ideas are not only academic. In Pakistan they shaped the Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Act 2018 and the controversy that led the Federal Shariat Court to strike down parts of it in 2023. This answer explains social constructionism and queer theory, presents their critics fairly, and shows how they clarify what is at stake in the Pakistani debate.

**The sex/gender distinction**

Simone de Beauvoir's claim that one is not born but becomes a woman (1949) began the idea that womanhood is made. Ann Oakley's *Sex, Gender and Society* (1972) defined sex as biological and gender as social. This was liberating: if inequality comes from gender, it can be changed. Margaret Mead's study of three New Guinea societies (1935) had already shown that "masculine" and "feminine" temperaments vary across cultures.

**Social constructionism**

Berger and Luckmann (1966) showed how human habits harden into institutions that appear natural. Applied to gender:
- Judith Lorber argues that gender is a social institution, like religion or the economy, that sorts people and assigns work and power from birth.
- Candace West and Don Zimmerman (1987) describe "doing gender": we perform gender in daily interaction and are held accountable for it.
- R.W. Connell shows that masculinities are plural and hierarchical, with a hegemonic form that legitimises male dominance.

Constructionism thus treats gender roles and identities as products of socialisation, interaction and power.

**Queer theory and the problem of "sex"**

In the 1990s queer theorists asked whether sex itself is social. Michel Foucault argued that modern power produces categories of sexuality rather than simply repressing them. Judith Butler's *Gender Trouble* (1990) argued that gender is performative, the repeated stylisation of acts that creates the appearance of an inner core, and that "sex" was always already read through gender. Teresa de Lauretis named the field in 1990. Supporting evidence includes Anne Fausto-Sterling's work on intersex variation, Thomas Laqueur's history of the shift from a "one-sex" to a "two-sex" model, and the existence of third-gender categories such as khawaja siras in South Asia.

Queer theory's key claims are anti-essentialism, the critique of heteronormativity, performativity and the deconstruction of binaries.

**Critiques**

1. *Biological critique*: human reproduction is binary; intersex conditions are rare and do not create new sexes; sex differences matter in medicine.
2. *Materialist feminist critique*: Martha Nussbaum argued that Butler's abstraction neglects material oppression and practical politics.
3. *Political critique*: if "woman" is dissolved as a category, feminism loses the subject it represents; some feminists argue women's rights depend on sex-based categories, a view others see as exclusionary.
4. *Cultural and religious critique*: in many societies, including Pakistan, gender is grounded in religious law and family structure; Islamic jurisprudence recognises the intersex person (khunsa) but generally defines sex biologically.

A balanced position holds that biological variation is real but its boundaries and meanings are socially drawn and enforced.

**The Transgender Act 2018 and the FSC ruling**

Pakistan had a long tradition of khawaja siras, but they faced exclusion and violence. The Supreme Court ordered their protection in 2009, and NADRA introduced a third-gender option on CNICs. Parliament passed the Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Act in 2018 with broad support. It allowed registration according to self-perceived gender identity and banned discrimination in education, employment, health, property and inheritance.

From 2021 religious parties campaigned against it, arguing that self-identification could enable same-sex marriage and contradict Islamic injunctions. In May 2023 the Federal Shariat Court declared the definitions based on self-perceived identity, the right to change gender on that basis, and the inheritance section as framed repugnant to Islam, while upholding rights for intersex persons. Appeals were filed before the Shariat Appellate Bench of the Supreme Court, and I could not confirm a final decision.

**What the theories illuminate**

1. *Two definitions of identity*: the Act embodied a constructionist view (identity is self-defined); the FSC applied a biological and juristic view (identity follows physical sex characteristics). The legal clash is a clash between these theories.
2. *Performativity and social recognition*: khawaja siras' identity is lived through community, dress and ritual, which supports the constructionist claim that identity is social.
3. *Power and categories*: Foucault's insight that law produces categories is visible in how NADRA's forms define who counts.
4. *Limits of queer theory in context*: the backlash shows that theories framed in Western academic language can provoke resistance when transplanted without local grounding.
5. *Intersectionality*: transgender persons in Pakistan face exclusion by gender identity, class and stigma together, so many survive by begging or dancing.

**A balanced way forward**

1. Separate the agreed core from the contested part: both sides accept the rights of khawaja siras and intersex people to life, dignity, education, health, work and inheritance.
2. Implement those protections firmly: job quotas, protection units in police, healthcare access.
3. Resolve the identity-definition question through the Supreme Court's appellate process and wide consultation with medical experts, jurists and the community.
4. Protect transgender persons from violence, especially in KP, where many killings have been reported.

**Conclusion**

Social constructionism showed that gender is made; queer theory pushed the question to sex itself. Their critics remind us that biology, material conditions and culture also matter. Pakistan's debate over the 2018 Act is these theories in court. Whatever the final legal definition, the dignity and safety of a vulnerable community must not depend on winning the theoretical argument.

## Model Answer 9: Using intersectionality as a framework, examine the experiences of marginalised women in Pakistan in accessing health, education and employment.

*(CSS 2026 Q7; intersectionality 2025 Q6.)*

**Outline**
1. Introduction
2. Intersectionality: origin and concepts
3. Rural women
4. Religious and ethnic minority women
5. Women with disabilities
6. Other groups
7. How overlapping systems shape access
8. Critique of the framework
9. Policy recommendations
10. Conclusion

**Introduction**

Pakistani women are not one group. A university graduate in Islamabad and a cotton picker in Sanghar both face gender inequality, but their lives differ because of class, location, religion, ethnicity and disability. Intersectionality is the framework that explains how such identities combine. This answer applies it to rural women, minority women and women with disabilities, and argues that national averages hide the women who are furthest behind, so policy must be designed around their overlapping barriers.

**Intersectionality**

Kimberlé Crenshaw introduced the term in 1989. In *DeGraffenreid v. General Motors* (1976), Black women could not prove discrimination because the company hired Black men and white women; the law examined race and sex separately, and Black women fell between them. Crenshaw used the image of a traffic intersection where harms arrive from several directions. Earlier, the Combahee River Collective (1977) wrote of interlocking oppressions. Patricia Hill Collins (1990) described a matrix of domination. The key claims are that oppressions combine rather than simply add up, that the most marginalised are missed by policies designed for an "average" woman, and that structures, not just identities, produce inequality.

**Rural women**

*Health.* Rural women live far from facilities, often need male permission and money to travel, and give birth at home more often. Lady Health Workers reach many villages but coverage has gaps. Maternal mortality is far higher in rural Balochistan than in cities.

*Education.* The 2023 Census shows literacy of about 74% in urban areas and 52% in rural areas; for rural women it is much lower. Girls' schools beyond primary level are scarce, female teachers are few, and early marriage ends schooling.

*Employment.* Most employed rural women work in agriculture and livestock, frequently as unpaid family workers with no land titles. Cotton pickers face heat stress and pesticide exposure.

**Religious and ethnic minority women**

Hindu, Christian, Sikh and Ahmadi women face gender discrimination combined with religious and often caste or class discrimination. Christian women are concentrated in sanitation and domestic work, sometimes in jobs advertised "for non-Muslims only". Scheduled-caste Hindu women in Sindh face bonded labour in agriculture and brick kilns, and human rights groups report forced conversions and marriages of minority girls. Textbooks with religious content can alienate minority students. Hazara women in Quetta have faced restricted movement because of sectarian attacks, affecting schooling and jobs.

**Women with disabilities**

Women with disabilities face inaccessible clinics, neglect of their reproductive health, few inclusive schools, and very low employment despite disability job quotas. They are at higher risk of abuse and are often hidden by families. Pakistan ratified the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities in 2011 and passed the ICT Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act in 2020, but services lag.

**Other groups**

Transgender persons face exclusion from schools and jobs. Home-based and domestic workers lack labour protection. Women in climate-hit districts lost homes and livelihoods in the 2022 floods.

**How overlapping systems shape access**

1. *Poverty multiplies gender barriers*: a poor family withdraws daughters from school first and delays a woman's health care.
2. *Location multiplies everything*: distance makes mobility norms decisive.
3. *Religion and caste add discrimination* in jobs and justice, and vulnerability to forced conversion.
4. *Disability adds stigma and physical barriers.*
5. *Documentation gaps*: women without CNICs miss cash transfers, flood compensation and voting.

These are not separate problems. A disabled, poor, minority woman in a rural district faces barriers that no single-axis programme can remove.

**A case example**

Consider a Christian woman with a physical disability living in a Punjab village. Her gender limits her mobility and inheritance; her disability makes the nearest health unit, with stairs and no ramp, hard to use; her religion and her family's low-status occupation expose her to discrimination at school and at work; and her poverty means her family cannot afford private transport or treatment. A women's health programme designed for the average rural woman will not reach her, and neither will a disability programme designed for urban men. Only a programme that combines accessible facilities, outreach by a female health worker, protection from discrimination and cash support can meet her needs. Crenshaw's point is exactly this: the harm happens at the intersection, so the remedy must be built there.

**Critique of the framework**

Critics argue that intersectionality can fragment movements, is hard to measure, and was developed for American race politics. In Pakistan it must be adapted to caste, sect, ethnicity, language and the rural-urban divide. Marxist feminists insist that class remains central. These cautions refine rather than reject the framework.

**What the national averages hide**

Pakistan's 2026 Global Gender Gap score improved, and women now match men in tertiary enrolment. But these averages are pulled up by urban, middle-class women. The gains of a medical student in Lahore say nothing about a Hindu agricultural worker in Tharparkar. Intersectional analysis asks who is still left out after the averages improve.

**Policy recommendations**

1. *Data*: disaggregate national surveys by sex, disability, religion and district.
2. *Targeting*: use BISP's poverty-based targeting to link cash with health and schooling for the poorest women; add disability top-ups.
3. *Health*: expand Lady Health Workers and mobile clinics in underserved districts; make facilities accessible.
4. *Education*: girls' secondary schools in rural union councils, inclusive education, curricula respectful of minorities.
5. *Employment*: enforce disability quotas, extend labour law to agricultural, home-based and domestic workers, end bonded labour.
6. *Representation*: ensure minority and disabled women gain seats in local councils and legislatures.
7. *Protection*: legal safeguards against forced conversion and marriage of minors.

**Conclusion**

Intersectionality reveals what averages hide: the women who most need health care, schooling and work are those whose gender is combined with poverty, rurality, minority status or disability. Pakistan's improving national indicators will mean little if these women are left behind. Designing policy from the margins inward is both fairer and more effective.
